The Drug Enforcement Administration announced on July 1 that it will move to temporarily place kratom-related products containing elevated levels of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), along with several lab-made analogs, into Schedule I of the federal Controlled Substances Act, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/dea-moves-place-some-strong-kratom-related-products-under-strict-federal-drug-2026-07-01/). The proposal is slated for publication in the Federal Register on July 6, which would open the formal comment period and start the temporary scheduling process.

According to the Reuters report, the action would cover kratom plant material containing more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight and finished products containing more than 1 milligram of 7-OH. The named substances are 7-hydroxymitragynine, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, and MGM-16. The DEA said the targeted products act like opioids and could endanger consumers, per Reuters. Pills, gummies, powders, and liquid shots with elevated 7-OH content were identified as the primary targets.

7-OH occurs naturally in kratom leaf in trace amounts. The current regulatory focus is on concentrated extracts and synthetic preparations that contain the alkaloid at levels far above what occurs in unprocessed leaf. The Department of Health and Human Services and the FDA support the DEA's move, and according to the Reuters report, federal officials framed the action as aimed at concentrated and synthetic products while leaving natural kratom leaf with unenhanced 7-OH levels outside the scope of the scheduling. Products that stay below the thresholds would not be directly scheduled under the action, though the FDA's existing posture toward kratom, including import alerts and warning letters, remains in place.

The move operationalizes the FDA's [June recommendation that the DEA schedule concentrated 7-OH products][fda-recommends-scheduling-7oh-kansas-signs-ban], which drew the same line between concentrated preparations and natural leaf. It is also narrower than what some in Congress asked for: a bipartisan Senate letter in May [urged the FDA to pursue scheduling for the whole kratom leaf][senators-push-full-kratom-scheduling-states-crack-down], arguing that targeting only 7-OH would leave addictive products on shelves. The threshold-based approach likewise contrasts with the DEA's 2016 attempt to schedule the entire kratom plant and its primary alkaloids, an effort the agency later withdrew.

## Enforcement stakes for manufacturers and retailers

Once temporary scheduling takes effect, the DEA would have clear federal authority to pursue enforcement against manufacturers and distributors of covered products, according to the Reuters report. That exposure extends to retail: online sellers, vape shops, gas stations, and specialty vendors that continue to stock products above the 7-OH thresholds, or products containing the named analogs, would face significant federal legal risk that did not exist while the compounds were unscheduled, the report noted.

The New York Times [reported on July 1](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/01/us/kratom-trump-administration.html) that Trump administration officials supported the DEA's steps to temporarily ban supplements containing a synthetic version of kratom, and that the move delivers what the paper called a lucrative win for some supplement makers that had lobbied aggressively for the change while positioning their own non-kratom botanical products as alternatives. The Times framed the scheduling decision as part of broader industry and political maneuvering around kratom-derived compounds, with federal scheduling outcomes carrying significant commercial consequences for competing product categories.

## Industry and state reaction

The American Kratom Association, in [recent statements](https://www.americankratom.org), said the DEA's action confirms its position that 7-OH products are not kratom, and called on state officials to stop what the organization described as protecting 7-OH profiteers. The AKA has spent recent weeks pressing the distinction between natural kratom leaf and what it calls chemically manipulated 7-OH opioids, and it pointed to [Florida's emergency rule placing concentrated 7-OH in Schedule I][florida-emergency-rule-7oh-schedule-one] as the kind of enforcement it wants to see elsewhere.

The federal action lands in the middle of a wave of state activity. Kansas House Bill 2365, which places kratom and 7-OH in the state's Schedule I, and Tennessee's full kratom prohibition [both took effect July 1][tennessee-kansas-bans-take-effect-july-one]. For states that have already scheduled 7-OH, a parallel federal designation would reinforce existing state restrictions with federal enforcement authority. A recent overview from the [National Conference of State Legislatures](https://www.ncsl.org/resources/details/lawmakers-weigh-guardrails-bans-on-kratom-and-7-oh) described legislatures weighing guardrails such as age limits, labeling, and product standards against outright bans, and noted that one state this year permanently banned synthetic kratom compounds such as 7-OH through pharmacy rulemaking.

The July 6 Federal Register publication will set the formal timeline. Under the Controlled Substances Act's temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. Section 811(h), a temporary order takes effect no earlier than 30 days after the notice is published and remains in place for up to two years, with a possible one-year extension, while the government completes the analysis required for permanent scheduling. The published order will show whether the DEA's final thresholds and covered substances match what the agency previewed this week.
