The Drug Enforcement Administration's temporary scheduling rule for concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and three synthetic kratom-derived compounds appeared in the [Federal Register on July 6, 2026](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified), formalizing the action the agency [announced July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products].

The publication of two separate Federal Register notices marks the procedural step that starts the clock on the rule's effective date. Once effective, manufacture, distribution, and possession of covered products become federal crimes under the Controlled Substances Act, according to the notice text.

## What the rule covers

The first notice (Document 2026-13580) temporarily places 7-OH into Schedule I when it is present above specified concentration thresholds: products containing more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight or more than 1 milligram of 7-OH per finished unit fall under the rule, according to the [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified). The threshold-based approach means that natural kratom leaf, which contains 7-OH in trace quantities typically well below these levels, is not covered by this action.

A [companion notice (Document 2026-13581)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13581/schedules-of-controlled-substances-temporary-placement-of-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-and) separately places mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MP), MGM-15, and two related analogues into Schedule I. The DEA described these as synthetic or semisynthetic derivatives of the kratom alkaloid mitragynine, according to the notice.

Both temporary placements last two years, with the option to extend for a third year while permanent scheduling is considered, [The Hill reported](https://thehill.com/policy/healthcare/5951668-dea-temporarily-bans-synthetic-kratom/).

## DEA's stated rationale

The DEA stated that the targeted substances "pose an imminent threat to public safety" because of their unpredictable effects, according to [The Hill's coverage](https://thehill.com/policy/healthcare/5951668-dea-temporarily-bans-synthetic-kratom/). The agency emphasized that these synthetic compounds are often marketed as "kratom extracts" but function as synthetic opioid compounds rather than natural kratom alkaloids.

The FDA had previously recommended that 7-OH be listed as a Schedule I substance because of its opioid-like properties, including high affinity for the mu-opioid receptor and risks of tolerance, dependence, and addiction, [The Hill reported](https://thehill.com/policy/healthcare/5951668-dea-temporarily-bans-synthetic-kratom/). The [FDA's recommendation][fda-recommends-scheduling-7oh-kansas-signs-ban] distinguished concentrated 7-OH products from natural kratom leaf.

## Products and businesses affected

The rule targets products such as gummies, shots, tablets, and concentrated "extracts" that contain synthetic or highly concentrated 7-OH, MP, MGM-15, or MGM-16, according to [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/healthcare/5951668-dea-temporarily-bans-synthetic-kratom/). Retailers and manufacturers selling these products will need to reformulate or remove them from shelves to avoid Schedule I liability.

Businesses selling botanical kratom leaf or conventional powder are not directly covered by this rule, the DEA stated, though they continue to operate under FDA scrutiny and state law constraints. The DEA listed kratom as a "drug of concern" but specified that its temporary action "did not impact" kratom itself, according to the agency's statements reported by [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/healthcare/5951668-dea-temporarily-bans-synthetic-kratom/).

## State regulatory context

The Federal Register notice referenced the state-level regulatory landscape, noting that 19 states have enacted Kratom Consumer Protection Act (KCPA) legislation that sets labeling requirements for mitragynine and 7-OH content and imposes caps on 7-OH concentration, according to the [notice text](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified). A [2026 state-by-state tracker](https://restoration-recovery.com/resources/kratom-laws-by-state-2026.html) published by Restoration Recovery Center lists eight states that fully ban kratom as of mid-2026: Alabama, Arkansas, Indiana, Louisiana, Vermont, Wisconsin, Connecticut, and Tennessee. The federal scheduling action operates alongside these state frameworks, with federal law applying regardless of state-level rules once the temporary rule takes effect.

Several [states have moved recently][florida-emergency-rule-7oh-schedule-one] to impose their own restrictions on concentrated 7-OH products, and the DEA's acknowledgment of state KCPA frameworks in the Federal Register notice underscores the parallel federal and state regulatory approach to concentrated kratom-derived compounds.
