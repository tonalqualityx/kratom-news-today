The North Carolina Senate approved HB 328, a bill that would ban synthetic kratom products statewide and set a minimum purchase age of 21 for natural kratom, [BPR (Blue Ridge Public Radio) reported on July 2](https://www.bpr.org/2026-07-02/nc-senate-approves-new-hemp-regulations-expresses-frustration-with-houses-lack-of-vote). The bill now awaits House action, though the chamber is not scheduled to return until the week of July 27 under a current adjournment resolution.

## What the bill would do

HB 328 treats synthetic and natural kratom as distinct regulatory categories, according to [BPR's coverage](https://www.bpr.org/2026-07-02/nc-senate-approves-new-hemp-regulations-expresses-frustration-with-houses-lack-of-vote). Synthetic kratom products would face a total sales ban. Natural kratom products would remain legal for purchase by adults 21 and older.

The bill is primarily framed as hemp regulation, overhauling the state's definition of hemp products to align with a new federal hemp standard scheduled to take effect November 12 (including a 0.4 milligram THC-per-container cap), [BPR reported](https://www.bpr.org/2026-07-02/nc-senate-approves-new-hemp-regulations-expresses-frustration-with-houses-lack-of-vote). The kratom provisions are part of the same regulatory package.

## Black-market concerns

During Senate debate, a senator warned that "piecemeal" bans and restrictions on hemp and kratom could foster black-market sales rather than reducing use, [BPR reported](https://www.bpr.org/2026-07-02/nc-senate-approves-new-hemp-regulations-expresses-frustration-with-houses-lack-of-vote). The concern reflects a harm-reduction perspective: partial, product-by-product bans and age limits without a broader regulatory framework may push products into illicit channels rather than eliminating access.

Law enforcement complaints that current definitions allow sale of products with high levels of psychoactive compounds are driving support for tighter controls on both hemp derivatives and kratom in the state, according to [BPR](https://www.bpr.org/2026-07-02/nc-senate-approves-new-hemp-regulations-expresses-frustration-with-houses-lack-of-vote).

## Federal and state context

North Carolina's approach of distinguishing synthetic from natural kratom in statute tracks the distinction the DEA drew in its [recent temporary scheduling action][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], which targets concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products while leaving botanical kratom leaf unscheduled at the federal level. The DEA's rule was [formally published in the Federal Register on July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule].

A [2026 state-by-state tracker](https://restoration-recovery.com/resources/kratom-laws-by-state-2026.html) published by Restoration Recovery Center reports that more than 30 states have adopted some form of Kratom Consumer Protection Act (KCPA) legislation, which typically sets labeling requirements, caps on 7-OH concentration (commonly at 2 percent of total alkaloids), and prohibitions on synthetic alkaloid additives. Eight states fully ban kratom. If HB 328 becomes law, North Carolina would fall into a middle category: restricting rather than banning natural kratom, while prohibiting synthetic kratom products entirely.

The House's delayed return means final passage, if it happens, is weeks away. The bill could be amended, stalled, or advanced in its current form when the chamber reconvenes.
