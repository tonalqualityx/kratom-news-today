The Drug Enforcement Administration's notice of intent to place high-concentration 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products in Schedule I, [published in the Federal Register on July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule], contains the first firm timeline for when the ban takes hold: the temporary scheduling order "will be published in the Federal Register on or after August 5, 2026," according to the [notice text](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified). The notice also makes clear that there will be no formal public comment period before the order issues.

## The August timeline

Under 21 U.S.C. 811(h), the temporary scheduling statute the DEA is using, the agency must wait at least 30 days after publishing a notice of intent before issuing the order itself. The July 6 publication starts that clock, and the notice states the order will follow on or after August 5, 2026. Once the order is published, Schedule I controls and criminal, civil, and administrative sanctions apply to anyone who manufactures, distributes, imports, exports, or possesses covered products, according to the notice.

The covered products are those exceeding the thresholds the agency [spelled out last week][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products]: botanical kratom material containing more than 0.050 percent 7-OH on a dry weight basis, or synthetically derived and processed products containing more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by weight or volume or more than 1 milligram of 7-OH per article. Natural kratom leaf, in which the DEA notes 7-OH occurs in trace amounts, falls below those thresholds and is outside the order's scope.

## No comment window

The notice states that the notice-and-comment requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act do not apply to temporary scheduling actions, which the statute treats as orders rather than rules. Even if those requirements did apply, the DEA writes, the Administrator found good cause to forgo them on the grounds that further delay would pose an "imminent hazard to public safety," according to the notice text. The only consultation the statute requires, comments from the Department of Health and Human Services, has already taken place: the notice states the Administrator considered comments submitted by the HHS Acting Assistant Secretary under 21 U.S.C. 811(h)(4).

The result is that no public comment period will precede the temporary order. Temporary scheduling under 21 U.S.C. 811(h)(2) lasts up to two years, with a possible one-year extension while permanent scheduling is considered.

## Patient pushback emerges

Reaction from consumers who use the affected products is beginning to surface. In a July 3 column for Pain News Network titled ["The Coming Ban on 7-OH Will Ruin My Life"](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/7/3/the-coming-ban-on-7-oh-will-ruin-my-life), columnist Crystal Lindell wrote that she uses 7-OH products daily for chronic pain and that the coming ban "will be devastating for me." Lindell wrote that the products have "given me my life back" and said she worries about being able to keep working once they become illegal.

Lindell criticized the American Kratom Association for supporting federal action against 7-OH products, and argued the scheduling decision should be viewed through the lens of bodily autonomy. She also pointed to the DEA's 2016 attempt to temporarily schedule mitragynine and 7-OH, which the agency withdrew after public and congressional backlash, as precedent that scheduling plans can be reversed.

Lindell wrote that she hopes 7-OH manufacturers are developing new kratom alkaloid formulations that would remain legal after the order takes effect, though her column did not identify any specific company or product in development.

## What the docket shows

The DEA's own notice reflects the other side of the public conversation. The agency writes that commenters on its earlier docket compared the potency of concentrated 7-OH products to prescription opioids such as oxycodone and hydrocodone, and raised concerns about the products' availability at smoke shops and gas stations, where commenters said ease of access raised concerns about impulse use, according to the notice text.

The dueling framings, consumer reliance on one side and accessibility concerns on the other, echo the debate that preceded the agency's July 1 announcement and will likely continue as the August publication window approaches.
