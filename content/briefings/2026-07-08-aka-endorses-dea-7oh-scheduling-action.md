The American Kratom Association (AKA) has publicly endorsed the DEA's temporary scheduling of concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, calling them "opioids" that should be distinguished from natural kratom leaf.

Mac Haddow, AKA's Senior Fellow for Public Policy, [wrote in an op-ed published by Pain News Network on July 6](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/7/6/banning-7-oh-opioids-will-save-lives) that "the blame lies entirely at the feet of the 7-OH manufacturers who deliberately bypassed federal law and basic safety requirements in pursuit of profits." Haddow stated that AKA "will not defend chemically manipulated opioid products masquerading as kratom" and supports "drawing that line clearly, and enforcing it."

## A public break with the 7-OH segment

The op-ed represents a direct public statement distancing the organization from manufacturers producing concentrated 7-OH products. Haddow argued that chemically manipulated 7-OH products "are not kratom" and framed the distinction between natural kratom leaf and concentrated 7-OH formulations as central to the organization's consumer-protection agenda.

According to Haddow's column, AKA advocates for "proper labeling, age restrictions, contaminant testing, responsible manufacturing standards, and clear limits that prevent dangerous adulteration or chemical manipulation." Those positions track closely with the Kratom Consumer Protection Act (KCPA) framework that 19 states have adopted in some form, [according to the Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified) published the same day.

The AKA stated it "does not advocate for the purchase of any kratom product," but instead for consumer protection standards that would preserve access to mitragynine-dominant, natural leaf products, according to [Pain News Network](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/7/6/banning-7-oh-opioids-will-save-lives).

## The federal action AKA is endorsing

The DEA's [temporary scheduling order][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback], expected to take effect on or after August 5, would place 7-OH in Schedule I when present above 0.050 percent by dry weight or above 1 milligram per finished unit, according to the [Federal Register notice][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule]. The order would last two years, with a possible one-year extension.

The [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified) references branded 7-OH tablet and shot products, including 7OHMZ, Press'd, and Hydroxie, as examples of the product category targeted. Natural kratom leaf products with 7-OH below the specified concentration thresholds would not be covered by the order, according to the DEA and FDA position described in the notice.

Federal officials stated that temporary scheduling is meant to "give researchers time to evaluate [7-OH's] pharmacological properties and health risks" under more controlled conditions, according to reporting by [Addiction Center](https://www.addictioncenter.com/news/2026/07/dea-moves-to-ban-7-oh-products/).

## Legal analysis tracks the distinction

A legal analysis published by [Spencer Fane](https://www.spencerfane.com/insight/dea-taking-steps-to-issue-temporary-scheduling-order-for-kratom-based-products-including-high-levels-of-7-oh-and-7-oh-related-synthetic-components/), a national law firm, noted that the DEA's action "is focused specifically on synthetic alkaloids associated with Mitragyna speciosa, and not on the plant material itself." The analysis, authored by regulatory attorneys, observed that manufacturers and retailers selling products with high levels of 7-OH or synthetic analogs face potential exposure under the temporary order.

The FDA has cited inconsistent concentrations, inaccurate labeling, and undisclosed ingredients in concentrated 7-OH products as central reasons for supporting the emergency scheduling, [Addiction Center reported](https://www.addictioncenter.com/news/2026/07/dea-moves-to-ban-7-oh-products/). Public health organizations have referenced growing poison-center calls, emergency department visits, and overdoses involving 7-OH products in their coverage of the action.

## What this means for the market

AKA's endorsement of federal enforcement against concentrated 7-OH products signals that the segment of the market built around 7-OH formulations (shots, gummies, tablets, tinctures) will face both regulatory pressure and diminished advocacy support. Haddow's op-ed assigns blame for the current regulatory crackdown to 7-OH manufacturers specifically, and the organization's refusal to defend that product category increases the legal and reputational risk for businesses continuing to sell above-threshold products.

The AKA's position aligns with the [DEA's announcement on July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] and its subsequent [Federal Register publication][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule], completing a picture in which the industry's most prominent advocacy organization and the primary federal enforcement agency agree on targeting concentrated 7-OH while leaving natural kratom leaf unscheduled at the federal level.
