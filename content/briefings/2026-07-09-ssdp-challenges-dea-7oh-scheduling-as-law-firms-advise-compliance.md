Students for Sensible Drug Policy (SSDP), a harm-reduction advocacy group, published a response to the DEA's temporary scheduling of concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, calling the action "an emergency scheduling order that pushes criminalization over harm reduction" and arguing it risks slowing development of safer pain treatments. The [SSDP blog post](https://ssdp.org/blog/deas-holiday-weekend-emergency-scheduling-order-pushes-criminalization-over-harm-reduction-and-risks-slowing-development-of-safer-pain-medicines-and-addiction-treatments/) urged scientists and health professionals to submit comments by July 31, 2026, criticizing the holiday-weekend publication of the Federal Register notice as a barrier to public participation.

The response highlights a growing division among advocacy groups over the DEA action. The [American Kratom Association endorsed the scheduling last week][aka-endorses-dea-7oh-scheduling-action], with senior fellow Mac Haddow describing concentrated 7-OH products as "opioids, not kratom." SSDP took the opposite position, arguing that the emergency scheduling framework limits meaningful input from researchers and could impede pharmacological studies on kratom-derived compounds.

SSDP's critique centers on the procedural structure of the temporary scheduling. The [Federal Register notice published July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule] stated that the order could take effect [as early as August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback], and the agency indicated that standard notice-and-comment procedures do not apply to temporary scheduling actions under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA). SSDP argued that the limited comment window and holiday-weekend timing effectively constrain the ability of researchers and clinicians to weigh in before the order is finalized.

The group also raised concerns about the impact on scientific research. Once the temporary order takes effect, researchers working with isolated or concentrated 7-OH would need Schedule I research registrations, secure facilities, and DEA oversight, according to the [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/06/2026-13580/schedules-of-controlled-substance-temporary-placement-of-7-hydroxymitragynine-above-a-specified). SSDP described these requirements as likely to slow ongoing pharmacology and toxicology work on kratom alkaloids.

## Industry compliance guidance

Separately, the law firm Spencer Fane published a [client alert](https://www.spencerfane.com/insight/dea-taking-steps-to-issue-temporary-scheduling-order-for-kratom-based-products-including-high-levels-of-7-oh-and-7-oh-related-synthetic-components/) analyzing the temporary scheduling order and its implications for kratom businesses. The alert, which has circulated among industry counsel in the days since the Federal Register publication, laid out specific compliance steps for companies in the supply chain.

Spencer Fane's attorneys noted that the DEA's action targets products containing 7-OH at concentrations above 0.050 percent by dry weight or more than 1 milligram per finished product, along with three synthetic compounds: mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, according to the [AddictionCenter's coverage](https://www.addictioncenter.com/news/2026/07/dea-moves-to-ban-7-oh-products/) of the DEA action. The attorneys emphasized that the scheduling "should not affect botanical kratom products that contain naturally occurring mitragynine and 7-OH" at typical leaf concentrations, according to the Spencer Fane alert.

The alert advised affected businesses to review ingredient specifications and certificates of analysis for all products referencing 7-OH, 7-hydroxymitragynine, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, or MGM-16. Spencer Fane also recommended that companies selling botanical kratom products strengthen documentation demonstrating that their products contain naturally occurring alkaloid levels and do not include synthetic derivatives, according to the firm's analysis.

For manufacturers and retailers selling concentrated or enhanced products that meet or exceed the DEA's thresholds, Spencer Fane described the compliance question as urgent. Once the temporary order takes effect, manufacture, distribution, sale, and possession of the covered substances would be subject to criminal, civil, and administrative provisions of the CSA, according to the alert. The firm's attorneys characterized the window between now and the August 5 effective date as a period for companies to audit product lines, reformulate where necessary, and coordinate with counsel experienced in DEA enforcement matters.

The client alert also noted that the temporary scheduling order would last two years, with a pathway for permanent scheduling thereafter. Companies whose products fall within the scheduling definitions face the choice of reformulating, withdrawing affected products, or accepting enforcement risk, according to Spencer Fane's analysis.
