Georgia's new House study committee on kratom has held its first meeting, and its chairman has described two possible destinations for the panel's work. State Rep. Rick Townsend, who chairs the House Blue-Ribbon Study Committee on Youth Exposure to Kratom and Retail-Available Substances, told [The Brunswick News](https://thebrunswicknews.com/news/local_news/townsend-leads-kratom-committees-first-meeting/article_042aef42-e3ab-4b15-a5c3-11d44066d65a.html) that based on the first meeting, kratom "either needs to be under the watchful eye or purview of a healthcare professional or banned." In Tennessee, meanwhile, the [State Gazette reported](https://www.stategazette.com/news/kratom-ban-in-tennessee-now-in-effect/article_e6f46490-78e5-5d9c-9f2c-44e8952dd46e.html) that the state's kratom prohibition is now in effect, closing off retail sales statewide.

## Georgia committee opens with youth-exposure focus

The blue-ribbon committee held its first meeting June 17 in Atlanta, according to The Brunswick News. The panel's members include Reps. Jordan Ridley, Michelle Au, Steven Meeks, Tyler Paul Smith, Danny Mathis, and Dar'shun Kendrick. A second meeting is scheduled for August 20 at the Southeast Georgia Conference Center in Brunswick, running from 8 a.m. to 1 p.m.

The newspaper reported that the committee heard testimony describing kratom products marketed in packaging that resembles candy, rising pediatric exposures, and a national doubling of poison control center calls involving kratom. The youth-access framing echoes concerns that drove [Georgia's HB 181][georgia-kratom-law-connecticut-schedule-one], signed earlier this year, which added 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) to the state's statutory definition of kratom, restricted sales to buyers 21 and older, and required behind-the-counter or secured-display placement effective July 1.

Townsend has pushed for tighter controls before. He sponsored a bill in the last legislative session that would have required kratom to be sold only from pharmacy counters, The Brunswick News reported. That measure failed to pass, and the study committee is now examining what should replace it. Townsend's comment to the newspaper suggests the committee's recommendations could range from a pharmacist-supervised sales model to an outright prohibition.

The committee's work is unfolding against an active federal backdrop. The Drug Enforcement Administration is moving to [temporarily place concentrated 7-OH products and three synthetic kratom-derived compounds in Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], an action the agency has said targets products with elevated or synthetic alkaloid content rather than botanical kratom leaf with naturally occurring levels. The Brunswick News account referenced the DEA action in its coverage of the committee's first meeting.

## Tennessee prohibition now in force

Tennessee's kratom ban is in effect, the State Gazette reported this week. The prohibition, enacted as HB 1649 and known as Matthew Davenport's Law, passed the House 78-9 and the Senate 23-3 before taking effect July 1. The law makes kratom illegal statewide, covering natural leaf products as well as concentrates.

The State Gazette's coverage confirms the on-the-ground arrival of a deadline [KNT reported in June][tennessee-kansas-bans-take-effect-july-one], when Tennessee retailers, including gas stations, vape shops, and specialty stores, faced the loss of legal market access and possible criminal exposure after July 1. Tennessee's across-the-board approach differs from the concentration-based frameworks in states like Florida, which scheduled concentrated 7-OH formulations while leaving natural kratom leaf unregulated.

The two developments put Georgia and Tennessee at different points on the same spectrum. Tennessee has completed the move to prohibition. Georgia, which chose age-gated regulation in HB 181, is now studying whether to go further, with a committee chairman who has publicly narrowed the options to medical supervision or a ban.
