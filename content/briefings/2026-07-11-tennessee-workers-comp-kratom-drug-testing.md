Tennessee's kratom prohibition reaches into workplace drug testing, according to guidance published Friday by the state's workers' compensation agency. In its [2026 legislative update](https://www.tn.gov/workforce/injuries-at-work/bwc-newsroom/2026/7/10/2026-workers-compensation-legislative-changes-tennessee.html), the Tennessee Bureau of Workers' Compensation stated that Public Chapter 950, the kratom ban known as Matthew Davenport's Law, "classifies kratom as a regulated substance, extending workplace drug-testing policies to include kratom." The update describes how the ban, [which took effect July 1][tennessee-kansas-bans-take-effect-july-one], applies inside the state's workers' compensation system.

## What the Bureau's update says

The Bureau's summary describes Public Chapter 950, enacted as Senate Bill 1656 and House Bill 1649, as legislation signed May 7, 2026, and effective July 1, 2026. According to the update, the law criminalizes knowing possession or distribution of kratom, with penalties ranging from Class D to Class A felonies and enhanced penalties when minors are involved. The law is named for Matthew Davenport, who died in 2024, the Bureau noted.

For employers and insurers, the operative change in the Bureau's telling is the regulated-substance classification. By classifying kratom as a regulated substance, the update states, the law extends workplace drug-testing policies to cover kratom. The Bureau's update does not detail how a kratom-positive test would affect an individual workers' compensation claim, and it directs questions to the agency.

The update also states that the law mandates kratom testing in overdose autopsies and includes kratom in toxicology screenings for suspected overdose deaths statewide.

## A new front for a law already in force

Coverage of Matthew Davenport's Law to date has centered on its retail consequences. The prohibition ended legal kratom sales statewide on July 1, as the State Gazette reported and [KNT covered this week][georgia-kratom-study-committee-tennessee-ban-in-effect], closing off a market that had included gas stations, vape shops, and specialty retailers. The Bureau's update signals that the law's reach extends beyond retail into employment policy and public-health surveillance.

Tennessee's approach covers kratom across the board, natural leaf products as well as concentrates, a design that distinguishes it from concentration-based frameworks in states such as Florida and from the federal posture. The Drug Enforcement Administration's pending action would [temporarily place concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products and three synthetic kratom-derived compounds in Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] while leaving botanical kratom leaf, which contains only trace amounts of naturally occurring 7-OH, unscheduled at the federal level. Tennessee's statute draws no such line, and the Bureau's workers' compensation guidance likewise refers to kratom generally rather than to any product category.

For multistate operators, the update adds an employment-law dimension to a compliance map that has mostly tracked retail legality. Tennessee employers now have state guidance treating kratom like other regulated substances in drug-testing policy, according to the Bureau, and the autopsy-testing mandate means Tennessee will begin generating statewide toxicology data on kratom in suspected overdose deaths.
