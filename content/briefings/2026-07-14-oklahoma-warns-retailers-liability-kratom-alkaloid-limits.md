The Oklahoma Bureau of Narcotics (OBN) and the Oklahoma State Department of Health (OSDH) issued a joint warning to retailers on July 13, saying stores can be held liable for selling what the agencies described as "dangerous and intoxicating" products containing kratom alkaloids above the legal limits set by the state's Kratom Consumer Protection Act, [according to KTUL](https://ktul.com/news/local/oklahoma-warns-retailers-over-sales-of-illegal-and-intoxicating-kratom-products-07-13-2026). The limits at issue come from Senate Bill 891, the statute that established Oklahoma's regulatory framework for kratom, and enforcement of the act's retail requirements falls to the health department, the station reported. Kratom itself is not classified as a controlled dangerous substance in Oklahoma, according to the same report.

The warning arrived alongside a mortality figure. The Oklahoma State Medical Examiner's Office has attributed more than 50 deaths in the state to kratom products since January 2025, according to KTUL and [a separate account from Oklahoma City's KOKH Fox 25](https://okcfox.com/news/local/oklahoma-agencies-warn-retailers-over-illegal-high-potency-kratom-sales-after-deaths). Neither report specifies whether the products involved in those deaths were concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, which contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts naturally present in raw kratom leaf, or natural leaf products, a distinction that materially changes the products' risk profiles. Fox 25's account frames the enforcement warning around high-potency products exceeding the state's legal thresholds, and OSDH said in its statement that kratom "has become an emerging public health concern due to reports of severe adverse health effects associated with its use, particularly when products are adulterated, mislabeled, or used in combination with other substances."

OBN Director Donnie Anderson put the compliance burden on stores. "Stores have the responsibility to ensure they are in compliance with state law," Anderson said, adding that compliance requires "testing and thorough research on their part," according to KTUL. "If stores have any doubt at all about the legality of their products, they are encouraged to halt sales immediately," he said. In the Fox 25 account, Anderson said "state law is very clear, and anyone selling these products with reckless disregard for public safety could be held liable." The agencies directed consumers who believe state kratom laws have been violated to file complaints through the OSDH Consumer Health Services complaint form, KTUL reported.

The announcement makes Oklahoma the latest state to signal active retail enforcement under a Kratom Consumer Protection Act framework, the regulatory model that caps alkaloid content, sets labeling requirements, and keeps legal kratom on shelves rather than banning it. That approach contrasts with the prohibition model: [full kratom bans took effect in Tennessee and Kansas on July 1][tennessee-kansas-bans-take-effect-july-one], with both states placing kratom and its alkaloids in their state Schedule I. Oklahoma's agencies are instead policing the boundary between compliant products and those exceeding statutory limits, with liability rather than criminal scheduling as the lever described in this week's warning.

State-level enforcement is tightening as federal action narrows in on concentrated 7-OH specifically. The Drug Enforcement Administration [moved on July 1 to temporarily place high-concentration 7-OH products and several synthetic analogs in Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], and the notices [published in the Federal Register on July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule] set August 5 as the earliest the temporary order can take effect, with a comment period on the concentration threshold open through July 31. The DEA has said that action does not apply to botanical kratom products containing naturally occurring 7-OH below the specified thresholds. Oklahoma's warning operates on a different axis, targeting products that exceed the alkaloid limits in its state statute at retail, regardless of manufacturer or product format.
