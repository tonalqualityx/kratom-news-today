The Fountain Valley City Council voted 4-1 on Tuesday to approve an ordinance restricting concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) kratom products, a synthetic or highly concentrated compound distinct from the trace 7-OH that occurs naturally in kratom leaf, [according to the Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/socal/daily-pilot/news/story/2026-07-16/fountain-valley-to-restrict-sale-use-of-kratom-products). The ordinance prohibits the sale, distribution, or possession of kratom products whose alkaloid fraction contains more than 2 percent 7-OH within city limits.

The measure also bars sales to anyone under 21, requires child-resistant packaging, prohibits packaging designs attractive to children, and mandates age-verification systems for online retailers selling into the city, the Times reported. The ordinance requires a second reading before taking effect 30 days after adoption.

During the council debate, one member explicitly referenced the DEA's recent temporary scheduling of concentrated 7-OH products. "The DEA has spoken. They have said that their concern is the synthetic products and not natural leaf," the council member said, according to the Times, praising the ordinance for aligning with that federal distinction.

## Orange County trend

Fountain Valley joins a growing list of Orange County municipalities restricting kratom. Newport Beach banned sale and use of all kratom products in March 2024, according to the Times. The Orange County Board of Supervisors banned sale of synthetic kratom products in unincorporated areas in August 2024. Huntington Beach adopted a ban on sale, distribution, and possession of kratom in September 2024.

The Fountain Valley approach differs from some of its neighbors in an important respect: rather than banning all kratom, it targets products exceeding a specific 7-OH concentration threshold, preserving access to lower-potency natural leaf products. This tracks the distinction the DEA, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), and the FDA have drawn between concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products and botanical kratom leaf in the [federal scheduling action set to take effect on or after August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback].

The city's ordinance was discussed alongside California Assembly Bill 1088, which would regulate synthetic kratom products at the state level, the Times reported. Governor Gavin Newsom [said on July 15][california-claims-enforcement-lead-7oh-crackdown] that California had removed more than 6,700 kratom and 7-OH products from retail shelves and that approximately 98 percent of licensed alcohol retailers were in compliance with state restrictions.

## Medical association and policy debate

The local action comes as the American Medical Association (AMA) has expanded its policy on kratom-derived products. A July 16 article in [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20260716/a-wild-west-what-to-know-about-kratomderived-products) reported that the AMA now calls for a complete ban on the marketing, distribution, or sale of 7-OH products and similar derivatives, aligning with the DEA's plan to place concentrated 7-OH in Schedule I. The [AMA first adopted policy targeting synthetic 7-OH in June][ama-adopts-policy-targeting-synthetic-7oh-kratom].

The Healio article described the current landscape of kratom-derived products as a "wild west," citing the proliferation of concentrated 7-OH shots, gummies, and tablets sold in gas stations and convenience stores.

The regulatory push faces criticism from some policy analysts. A Reason Foundation commentary published July 16 [argued](https://reason.org/commentary/the-drug-enforcement-administrations-arbitrary-7-oh-ban-is-not-rooted-in-science/) that the DEA's emergency 7-OH ban is "not rooted in science," contending that available data, including evidence cited by the DEA, do not support an "imminent threat" finding. The commentary warned that scheduling could push people back to illicit opioids. Separately, a [Pain News Network column](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/7/16/no-one-called-kratom-an-opioid-until-they-wanted-it-regulatednbspnbsp) published the same day noted that the 7-OH molecule is chemically identical in natural kratom leaf and in concentrated products, with the DEA's Federal Register order acknowledging this while arguing that high-dose formulations "pose significant safety risk to unsuspecting consumers by exposing them to high doses of opioids."

The DEA's threshold for the temporary scheduling order covers products containing more than 0.05 percent 7-OH by dry weight or more than 1 milligram of 7-OH per unit, according to the [Federal Register notice published July 6][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback]. Natural kratom leaf with only trace, naturally occurring levels of 7-OH is not the target of the federal action, according to DEA, HHS, and FDA statements.
