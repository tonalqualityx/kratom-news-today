Rhode Island officials are reviewing applications from businesses seeking to manufacture, import, and distribute kratom products, [according to a commentary published July 20 in The Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/07/20/metro/commentary-ri-kratom-gas-station-drugs/). The applications are moving under a state law that took effect in April 2026 and allows licensed businesses to sell kratom products, reversing a ban the state had maintained since 2017, per the piece.

The licensing framework does not extend to high-potency 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, which remain banned in Rhode Island, the commentary notes. Those products contain 7-OH, an alkaloid present only in trace amounts in natural kratom leaf, at far higher concentrations, and concentrated and synthetic preparations are the target of a pending federal scheduling order from the Drug Enforcement Administration. The commentary refers to the 7-OH category by its "gas station heroin" nickname.

## A state moving against the current

Rhode Island's change of direction stands out against this year's broader state trajectory. Tennessee and Kansas bans [took effect July 1][tennessee-kansas-bans-take-effect-july-one], and Massachusetts towns including Tisbury and Edgartown [have banned local sales][tisbury-marthas-vineyard-kratom-ban] this summer. Rhode Island moved the opposite way, from a 2017 ban to a licensed retail market.

For operators, the practical detail in the Globe piece is that the application process is underway: state officials are currently reviewing submissions to manufacture, import, and distribute kratom-based products, according to the commentary.

## The public-health case against the law

The commentary reports that the change was heavily opposed by the Rhode Island Medical Society and other leaders in the state's public-health community, who argue that increased access will significantly raise the risk of abuse. The piece assembles the case those opponents make: the FDA has not approved kratom for any use and warns it can cause what the agency calls "serious adverse effects," including liver toxicity and seizures; the Mayo Clinic has described kratom as "unsafe and ineffective" for medical use; and the DEA lists kratom as a "Drug and Chemical of Concern," a designation that carries no federal controls but flags risk.

The commentary also points to rising poison-control activity. A research summary [carried by ScienceDaily in March](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/03/260330233401.htm) reported that kratom-related poison center calls rose from 258 in 2015 to 3,434 in 2025, an increase of roughly 1,200 percent over the decade, with a marked surge in 2025.

## Federal backdrop

The DEA [announced on July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] that it intends to temporarily place 7-OH and three related compounds in Schedule I, covering botanical material above 0.05 percent 7-OH by dry weight and processed products above that concentration or containing more than 1 mg per package. The temporary orders can publish [as early as August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback]. Federal officials have said the action targets concentrated and synthetic preparations rather than natural leaf, a line that Rhode Island's framework also draws: licensed sales for kratom products on one side, a continued ban on high-potency 7-OH on the other, per the Globe commentary.
