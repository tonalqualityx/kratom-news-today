WKRC-TV, the Sinclair station in Cincinnati, published a [national report](https://local12.com/news/nation-world/reports-of-addiction-and-deaths-linked-to-synthetic-7-oh-monster-products-grow-as-families-share-losses-federal-officials-target-concentrated-kratom-compounds-with-new-enforcement-and-health-warnings-across-the-country-cincinnati-nicholas-campana) on July 18, updated July 21, collecting accounts from families in at least four states who attribute deaths and addiction to kratom-derived products, most prominently concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) tablets and extracts. Those products contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts that occur naturally in kratom leaf, and they are the target of the DEA's pending temporary Schedule I order, which can take effect as early as August 5. Several of the deaths in the report were attributed by coroners to 7-OH, while others were attributed to mitragynine, the primary alkaloid in natural leaf, a distinction that matters for how each case bears on the federal action.

## The cases described in the report

According to WKRC, the Canyon County, Idaho coroner linked the February 2026 death of Kirsty Boswell, 50, to 7-hydroxymitragynine. Her daughter Morgan Boswell told the station the product "literally took away one of the most important people to me."

In Missouri, the family of Joshua Bradley, who died March 8, 2026 from what the report describes as the toxic effects of 7-OH, has filed a wrongful-death lawsuit seeking damages exceeding $25,000 against Bear Stop Convenience, where the product was purchased, and against the product's manufacturers, per WKRC. The suit adds a private litigation track in a state where the [attorney general has expanded an investigation][missouri-ag-expands-kratom-investigation-american-shaman-suspends-sales] into kratom and 7-OH marketing.

The report also describes an Oklahoma case in which a 20-year-old Yukon High School graduate died after taking what the station characterizes as a single pill of synthetic kratom.

Two other deaths in the report were attributed to mitragynine rather than 7-OH. WKRC reports that toxicology in the 2022 death of Wyatt Wheeler of Texas identified mitragynine toxicity as the primary cause, and that David Thacker died in May 2025 of kratom intoxication with a blood mitragynine concentration of 1,400 nanograms per milliliter. The station's reporting does not specify whether concentrated extract products or natural leaf preparations were involved in those two cases. National Institute on Drug Abuse materials cited in the DEA's scheduling notice state that 7-OH can cause opioid-like respiratory depression reversible with naloxone, and that mitragynine and kratom leaf have not shown the same respiratory-depression profile.

## Addiction and withdrawal accounts

The report pairs the death cases with accounts of dependence on 7-OH tablets. Christina Pollock of Ohio told WKRC she first bought the products at a vape shop and escalated to five tablets a day at a cost of roughly $50 daily, eventually completing a two-week inpatient detox after withdrawal symptoms that included shaking, sweating, and severe anxiety.

Nate Kehlmeier, director of the Ohio treatment provider Team Recovery, told the station that "people that come in on kratom are sick. It's no different than opiate withdrawal." Dr. Scott Hadland of Harvard Medical School said his clinic is seeing increasing use among young people, with patients "experiencing symptoms of opioid addiction." Robyn Shinaver, a toxicologist with the Lucas County Coroner's Office in Ohio, told WKRC that roughly 70 percent of 7-OH users the office encounters were self-treating depression, anxiety, ADHD, or PTSD.

## Marketing under scrutiny

The report highlights how concentrated 7-OH products are marketed. Nicholas Campana, a YouTuber who documents the category, showed the station product marketing that uses terms drawn from street opioid slang, including "perks," "syrup," "roxy," and "sippers only." One product cited in the report, a SEDA 7-Hydroxy Blend, is marketed with the phrase "sink into serenity."

The station also cites the FDA's 2025 letter to clinicians, which warned that some commercial products contain 7-OH at levels up to 500 percent higher than natural kratom and referenced research estimating 7-OH to be roughly 13 times more potent than morphine.

## Federal backdrop

The DEA [announced on July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] that it intends to temporarily place 7-OH and three related compounds in Schedule I, covering botanical material above 0.05 percent 7-OH by dry weight and processed products above that concentration or containing more than 1 mg per package, according to Reuters. Botanical kratom below those thresholds is outside the order's scope. The temporary order can take effect [as early as August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback]. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is quoted in the WKRC report calling the action on 7-OH "a critical step in the fight against opioid addiction."

For retailers and manufacturers, the report's litigation thread is the newer signal: the Bradley suit names a convenience store alongside manufacturers, and it lands as federal and state enforcement attention on concentrated 7-OH products intensifies ahead of the scheduling date.
