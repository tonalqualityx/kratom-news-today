Georgia and South Carolina are the only two states currently considering repeal of their Kratom Consumer Protection Acts in favor of controlled-substance scheduling, [according to a report from industry publication Kratom.news](https://kratom.news/kcpa-repeal-georgia-south-carolina-kratom-2026/). Both efforts, the outlet reports, are framed by sponsors as a response to high-potency products enriched with 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), the kratom alkaloid that occurs in trace amounts in natural leaf but appears at far higher concentrations in some extracts and synthetic preparations. The federal government's pending action targets those concentrated and synthetic forms, and state sponsors are citing it as support for broader measures.

## The Georgia bill

Georgia's House Bill 968 would repeal the state's Kratom Consumer Protection Act and instead place kratom or its key alkaloids into a controlled-substance framework, per Kratom.news. The bill has advanced past Second Reading, making it eligible for a floor vote, but had not cleared either chamber as of about July 20, according to the report.

The outlet reports that the bill's backers describe the shift as moving from consumer-protection regulation toward prohibition-style control, on the argument that kratom products can act like opioids, particularly when enriched with 7-OH. Commentary around the bill ties its timing to the DEA's July Notices of Intent on 7-OH, with the report suggesting state lawmakers view federal scheduling as justification or political cover for stricter state control.

## The South Carolina bills

South Carolina has two active bills that would repeal its Kratom Consumer Protection Act and replace it with controlled-substance scheduling, per Kratom.news. Sponsors argue that consumer-protection frameworks have failed to prevent high-potency "enhanced" products from reaching shelves, especially those involving concentrated 7-OH, according to the report. The outlet says supporters point to the compound's mu-opioid receptor activity and to the federal scheduling move as evidence for stricter state control.

## A shifting state map

The Kratom.news report places the two repeal efforts in a wider tally: nine states now enforce full kratom bans (Alabama, Arkansas, Connecticut, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana, Tennessee, Vermont, and Wisconsin), while more than 30 states regulate kratom under some form of KCPA-style law. Tennessee's "Matthew Davenport's Law" (HB 1649 and SB 1656) and Kansas's ban (HB 2635 and SB 430) both took effect July 1, per the report, and the two states are being cited as models for pending ban bills elsewhere.

Michigan's House passed a ban bill on March 18 that is pending Senate consideration, and Illinois, West Virginia, Wyoming, and Maryland all saw ban bills introduced in the 2026 session, according to Kratom.news. Rhode Island remains the counterexample: the [Dank Reports state tracker](https://tracker.dankreports.com/) describes it as the only state to move from full prohibition back to regulated sale, after the state [reversed its ban effective April 1 and began reviewing license applications][rhode-island-kratom-licensing-public-health-opposition].

## The federal backdrop

The DEA [announced on July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] its intent to temporarily place 7-OH above defined thresholds, along with mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, into Schedule I of the Controlled Substances Act, with the temporary order able to publish [as early as August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback]. The notices cover botanical material containing more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight and non-botanical or altered material with more than 0.050 percent 7-OH or more than 1 milligram per article. Natural leaf kratom below those thresholds is outside the scope of the temporary order, and mitragynine itself is not being scheduled.

The Georgia and South Carolina bills would go further than the federal action, per Kratom.news, since KCPA repeal paired with scheduling could reach kratom products generally rather than only high-7-OH preparations.

## Pushback in commentary

In a [July 23 column in Townhall](https://townhall.com/columnists/derekhunter/2026/07/23/government-needs-to-think-before-it-acts-n2679969), Derek Hunter argued that government "needs to think before it acts" on 7-OH, contending the DEA's move toward what he called the most extreme federal action is driven more by political and commercial pressure than by new science. The column warned that scheduling 7-OH and related compounds could push dependent users toward illicit opioids if the action is not paired with evidence-based treatment, and that overly broad thresholds might criminalize lower-dose products without reducing opioid harms. Those arguments are the columnist's; drug-policy researchers cited in recent coverage have similarly pressed for dose-specific and product-specific regulation rather than blanket bans.
