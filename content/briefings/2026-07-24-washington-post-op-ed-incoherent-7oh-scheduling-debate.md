Opinion writers at national outlets spent the past several days arguing over the design of the Drug Enforcement Administration's pending action on 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), the kratom alkaloid that occurs in trace amounts in natural leaf but appears at far higher levels in concentrated extracts and synthetic preparations. The federal action, [announced July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], would temporarily place above-threshold 7-OH products and three synthetic analogs into Schedule I, leaving natural leaf kratom below the thresholds outside its scope, and the [temporary order becomes eligible for publication on or after August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback]. The new commentary faults the approach from opposing directions: one side argues the scheduling is too narrow a tool for a largely unregulated category, and the other argues it leaves too much of that category untouched.

## The Post op-ed: regulate the category, don't carve out one alkaloid

An op-ed published July 21 in [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2026/07/21/theres-right-way-regulate-drugs-kratom-is-no-exception/) argues that scheduling only 7-OH while leaving other kratom products unregulated amounts to "incoherent" policymaking. The piece contends the action targets a single alkaloid without establishing age limits, manufacturing standards, labeling requirements, or dosage controls for the rest of the kratom category, and that consumers may respond by shifting to other potent preparations or unregulated products.

The op-ed describes what it calls the right way to regulate drugs as a set of balanced controls: evidence-based dosing information, clear product standards, and transparent risk communication. A narrow ban driven by interest-group pressure, the authors argue, delivers none of those.

## The counterpoint: overdue, and still not enough

A [viewpoint article published July 23 by the Genetic Literacy Project](https://geneticliteracyproject.org/2026/07/23/viewpoint-kratom-supplement-crisis-dea-sits-on-its-hands-while-patient-addictions-to-this-ineffective-drug-rise/) reaches a nearly opposite conclusion from similar premises. The piece describes a "kratom supplement crisis," arguing that the DEA allowed high-potency kratom products to proliferate for years and that the current move against 7-OH concentrates is overdue. In the author's view the action is also insufficient, because ordinary kratom leaf remains widely available and legal in most states, leaving the broader policy debate unresolved.

The two pieces disagree on the remedy while sharing a premise: both treat the scheduling action as addressing only one segment of a much larger market.

## The lobbying question

A third strand of commentary questions the action's origins. Townhall columnist Derek Hunter, in a [July 23 column](https://townhall.com/columnists/derekhunter/2026/07/23/government-needs-to-think-before-it-acts-n2679969) covered in [KNT's July 23 briefing][georgia-south-carolina-kcpa-repeal-bills], argued the government is weighing what he called the most extreme federal action against 7-OH because "powerful interests are demanding it" rather than because the science has changed. The column cites reporting in The Washington Examiner that, in the Examiner's words, "the kratom industry has been pushing bans like this because it doesn't like the 7-OH competition," and contends the scheduling leaves "many other more dangerous kratom products, including mixed beverages, shots and powders, on the shelves."

Parts of the established kratom industry have in fact backed the federal action. The American Kratom Association [endorsed the temporary scheduling in early July][aka-endorses-dea-7oh-scheduling-action], with senior fellow Mac Haddow calling concentrated 7-OH products "opioids, not kratom" and blaming their manufacturers for endangering the legal status of natural leaf.

## Why the debate matters now

The commentary arrives during the final stretch before the temporary order can take effect. The DEA's notices of intent state that the order may publish on or after August 5, and standard notice-and-comment procedures do not apply, [per the agency's Federal Register notice][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback]. Whether the arguments now circulating in opinion pages influence the final threshold definitions remains an open question; the disagreement itself, spanning outlets with very different audiences, indicates the scheduling has reframed the kratom policy debate without settling it.
