Online sellers of concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) tablets, gummies, and shots are discounting inventory ahead of the earliest date the DEA's temporary Schedule I order can be issued, [according to Pain News Network](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/7/27/7-oh-retailers-slash-prices-as-dea-deadline-looms). The products at issue are concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations, distinct from the trace amounts of the alkaloid naturally present in kratom leaf, and they are the target of the scheduling action the DEA [announced July 1][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products]. Botanical kratom products below the agency's proposed thresholds are outside the order's scope, [Medical Daily reported](https://www.medicaldaily.com/dea-7-oh-kratom-schedule-i-august-5-timeline-476378).

## A sell-through before the deadline

Pain News Network described retailers promoting last-chance sales and a fire-sale environment as companies try to move inventory before covered products carry federal criminal liability. Under the [rule text published in the Federal Register on July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule], the temporary order covers plant material with more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight and finished products containing more than 1 milligram of 7-OH, along with three synthetic compounds: mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16. Once the order takes effect, manufacture, distribution, sale, and possession of covered products would be federally prohibited, according to the notice.

The DEA has said the order [will not be issued before August 5][dea-7oh-order-august-timeline-patient-pushback] and would remain in force for at least two years, with a possible one-year extension, per Pain News Network's account of the Federal Register announcement.

## Comments top 24,000 ahead of a July 31 deadline

Pain News Network reported that public comments on the federal scheduling of 7-OH can still be filed, with a July 31 deadline, and that more than 24,000 comments have been submitted to the docket so far. The DEA's notice of intent stated that standard notice-and-comment rulemaking procedures do not apply to temporary scheduling, so the open docket, along with a Request for Information from the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Health seeking scientific data on the proposed 7-OH threshold and possible alternative measurement methods, has served as the main channel for outside input.

## Users describe reliance on the products

Wired [reported](https://www.wired.com/story/7-oh-users-brace-for-dea-ban/) that people who take 7-OH products for pain or to stay off conventional opioids are bracing for the ban, with some expressing anger at the prospect of being cut off. Harm-reduction advocates quoted in the piece warned that abrupt removal of the products without support systems could push some users toward illicit opioids. The DEA's notice, as quoted by Wired, cites a significant potential for abuse and describes a public-health threat from 7-OH products marketed as botanicals.

The federal action lands on top of an existing patchwork: twelve states have already enacted bans on 7-OH, per Wired, and California has seized thousands of kratom and 7-OH products in recent months, according to Pain News Network. HHS and the FDA have publicly supported the scheduling, describing concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products as a threat to public safety while distinguishing them from natural leaf, a line that has [drawn commentary from opposing directions][washington-post-op-ed-incoherent-7oh-scheduling-debate] as the deadline approaches.
