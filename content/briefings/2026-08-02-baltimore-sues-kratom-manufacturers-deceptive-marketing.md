The city of Baltimore has sued several kratom manufacturers, alleging the companies deceptively marketed their products as safe and natural despite serious risks and overdose associations described in the complaint, [according to the Baltimore Sun](https://www.baltimoresun.com/2026/07/30/kratom-manufacturers-lawsuit-baltimore/). The complaint asserts violations of consumer-protection laws, resting on allegedly misleading representations about the products' addiction potential, safety, and labeling, per the Sun's July 30 report.

The available reporting does not specify whether the products at issue are concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations or natural leaf kratom, a distinction that materially changes the products' risk profiles and that has shaped most of this year's regulatory activity. Concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products are the target of the DEA's pending temporary Schedule I order, which [can issue any day on or after August 5][7oh-comment-window-closes-dea-order-august-5], according to Kratom.news. Natural kratom leaf, which contains 7-OH only in trace amounts, falls outside that order's scope, according to the [rule text published in the Federal Register][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule].

## A deception theory in city hands

The suit adds a city government to a roster of public plaintiffs that has so far been dominated by state officials. Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton sued kratom retailers earlier this year over products' 7-OH content, [as KNT reported in May][senators-push-full-kratom-scheduling-states-crack-down]. The Federal Trade Commission, whose mandate covers deceptive marketing of dietary supplements and botanicals, has announced no kratom-specific enforcement action in its recent public filings. Deception claims against kratom companies have instead been pressed by state and local governments and by private litigants.

The Baltimore complaint's focus on safety representations and labeling echoes the framework of the state consumer-protection statutes that attorneys general have used elsewhere, based on the Sun's description of the filing. The Sun's report does not indicate a schedule for the case.

## A crowded week for sub-federal enforcement

The filing landed in the same window as two other state-level actions. On July 30, Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones announced a [Regulated Products Enforcement Unit][virginia-ag-regulated-products-enforcement-unit] that will police kratom, vapes, and hemp-derived products, with authority over civil enforcement, retail compliance, and product directories. In Utah, state officials [linked a death to mislabeled tablets][utah-death-mislabeled-synthetic-kratom-tablets] sold as "Buzzers Cat's Claw" that contained mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15, lab-made compounds derived from kratom alkaloids and distinct from natural kratom leaf.

The federal backdrop is now fixed. The comment period on the DEA's temporary scheduling proposal closed July 31, and the agency has indicated it intends to issue the order as soon as possible after August 5, [Kratom.news reported](https://kratom.news/dea-7-oh-ban-august-5-2026/). Once a temporary order takes effect, manufacture, distribution, sale, and possession of covered compounds would carry federal penalties, according to the outlet's account of the scheduling process.
