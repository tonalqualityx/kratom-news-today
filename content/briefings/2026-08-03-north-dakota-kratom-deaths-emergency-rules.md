Kratom, 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), or the related compound mitragynine pseudoindoxyl were part of the primary cause of death for 23 people in North Dakota between 2019 and June 2026, according to State Epidemiology Office data [reported by the North Dakota Monitor](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2026/07/31/growing-concerns-prompt-calls-to-address-kratom-substances-in-north-dakota/) on July 31. The death data as reported groups natural leaf kratom together with concentrated and synthetic compounds, and the reporting does not break down how many of the 23 deaths involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations versus natural leaf kratom, a distinction that materially changes the products' risk profiles.

## Pharmacy board moves toward emergency rules

In response to the deaths and to federal action, North Dakota's pharmacy board is pursuing emergency rules that would make 7-OH and related synthetic kratom compounds illegal at the state level, mirroring the DEA's pending temporary scheduling, per the Monitor. The rulemaking push follows [discussions among Gov. Kelly Armstrong and legislative leaders about a special session][warren-michigan-kratom-ban-north-dakota-special-session] that could take up a kratom ban. State lawmakers rejected a ban on a kratom compound in 2025, and a subsequent bill was amended into a study recommendation that legislative leaders ultimately opted not to pursue this interim, according to the Monitor.

The Monitor also reported that the city of Ray voted in July to ban the sale, possession, and distribution of products containing concentrated levels of 7-OH or similar synthetic compounds, and to restrict kratom sales within the city to people 21 and older. The local ordinance tracks the distinction in the pending federal action, which targets concentrated and synthetic 7-OH rather than kratom leaf broadly.

At a July press conference on state crime data, Attorney General Drew Wrigley criticized kratom products sold in smoke shops as "mislabeled and harmful," calling kratom "highly addictive," "highly dangerous," and "not tested or certified by the FDA," and urging that North Dakota "have serious proposals" to address it, per the Monitor.

## A state patchwork tightening around synthetic 7-OH

The Monitor, citing the National Conference of State Legislatures, reported that 22 states have imposed age restrictions on kratom sales. The article also noted that seven states have placed kratom or its components on Schedule I of their controlled substance lists, and that states including Oklahoma and Ohio distinguish synthetic 7-OH from natural kratom leaf, regulating the synthetic forms more strictly.

The state activity unfolds against a compressed federal timeline. The DEA's July 1 notices of intent, [published in the Federal Register on July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule], cover 7-OH above 0.050 percent by dry weight, or more than 1 milligram per finished unit, plus three synthetic compounds including mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, with natural leaf containing trace 7-OH carved out, according to the notices. [The public comment windows closed on July 31][7oh-comment-window-closes-dea-order-august-5], and the temporary Schedule I order can issue any day on or after August 5. A [News 12 New Jersey investigation](https://newjersey.news12.com/2026/08/03/kiyc-feds-look-to-ban-gas-station-heroin/WRau48bV82Gm8VhAPMcZx) published August 3 reported that the restrictions "could take effect as early as this week."

Deaths attributed to kratom products are drawing official attention beyond North Dakota. In Florida, [two adults were charged with aggravated manslaughter after a toddler's death was attributed to kratom poisoning][florida-toddler-death-kratom-manslaughter-charges], according to the New York Post, in reporting that likewise does not specify whether a concentrated 7-OH product or natural leaf kratom was involved.
