The Dare County Board of Commissioners unanimously approved an ordinance on August 3 regulating the sale of kratom products in the Outer Banks county, [Island Free Press reported](https://islandfreepress.org/outer-banks-news/dare-county-approves-kratom-regulations-targeting-synthetic-concentrated-products/). The ordinance prohibits sales to anyone under 21, establishes testing and labeling standards, and targets synthetic, adulterated, or highly concentrated kratom products rather than banning the category outright.

## What the Dare County ordinance requires

According to a [county news release](https://www.darenc.gov/Home/Components/News/News/9578/17), the ordinance establishes age restrictions, retailer verification requirements, product-labeling standards, and bans on products containing synthetic alkaloids, harmful contaminants, adulterants, or unsafe levels of certain active compounds. Retailers must verify age when they have reasonable grounds to believe a purchaser is under 30, per Island Free Press.

The ordinance requires laboratory testing and product traceability, so that compliant kratom products can be linked to specific production batches, and it updates enforcement provisions to give law enforcement clear authority to address violations, according to the county release.

On product composition, Island Free Press reported that compliant products must be derived solely from Mitragyna speciosa leaves and contain only naturally occurring kratom alkaloids. The ordinance expressly bars synthetic, semi-synthetic, chemically converted, biosynthetically produced, or otherwise manufactured kratom alkaloids, and it caps 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) content at two percent of the alkaloid fraction or 0.4 milligrams on a dry-matter basis of overall alkaloid composition. Concentrated and synthetic 7-OH preparations, which contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts found in natural kratom leaf, are the products those caps effectively exclude.

The composition rules arrive as labeling accuracy draws new scrutiny. A study covered this week by MedicalXpress and Pain News Network reported that [about 85 percent of tested kratom products did not match their label claims][study-finds-85-percent-kratom-products-mislabeled], the kind of gap that testing and traceability requirements like Dare County's are designed to address.

## Minnesota raises its purchase age, Blackfoot bans with a leaf exception

A new Minnesota law raises the legal age to buy kratom from 18 to 21, [KFF Health News reported](https://kffhealthnews.org/morning-breakout/state-watch-080426/) in its August 4 state roundup. The outlet described the measure as the state's first major step toward regulating kratom and tied it to an increase in kratom-related poison-control calls. Minnesota lawmakers had earlier [floated a Schedule II classification for kratom alongside the age limit][litigation-landscape-south-dakota-minnesota-restrictions], FOX 9 reported in June; the measure KFF Health News described addresses the purchase age.

In Idaho, the Blackfoot City Council approved a kratom ban that includes an exception for dried leaves containing less than 0.00500 percent 7-OH, the [Idaho State Journal reported](https://www.idahostatejournal.com/chronicle/news/blackfoot-city-council-bans-kratom/article_ca6ed291-2bd8-48df-aa0f-584fb480b3bb.html). The carve-out makes Blackfoot another jurisdiction drawing its legal line by 7-OH concentration rather than banning the botanical category wholesale.

## A widening sub-federal patchwork

The three actions extend a run of state and local kratom measures that has accelerated around the federal 7-OH scheduling timeline. The DEA's temporary Schedule I order for concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products [could not issue before August 5 by statute][7oh-comment-window-closes-dea-order-august-5], and jurisdictions have moved in different directions while awaiting it: North Dakota's governor [ordered a statewide ban on all kratom products effective August 5][north-dakota-executive-order-bans-all-kratom], reaching natural leaf as well as concentrated products, while [Lawrence, Kansas outlawed kratom and 7-OH within city limits in late July][north-alabama-kratom-seizure-lawrence-kansas-ban].

Local officials elsewhere have pointed to the gap the federal action leaves. Lewis and Clark County Public Health Officer Drenda Niemann told [Yellowstone Public Radio](https://www.ypradio.org/community/2026-08-04/gas-station-drug-gets-schedule-i-listing) that kratom itself remains unregulated nationally, with no legal requirements for age limits or accurate ingredient labeling despite the products' psychoactive properties.

The threshold-based approach that Dare County and Blackfoot adopted mirrors the structure of the federal order, though the specific cutoffs differ across jurisdictions. The R Street Institute, in a [public comment on the federal scheduling](https://www.rstreet.org/outreach/r-street-comments-on-placement-of-7-oh-on-schedule-i/), argued that current data do not establish any single numerical 7-OH threshold as scientifically defensible, while acknowledging that the record supports concern about concentrated and semisynthetic 7-OH products, inaccurate labels, and undisclosed potency.
