About 85 percent of tested kratom-derived products did not match their label claims, according to a study [reported by MedicalXpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-08-kratom-products-conflict.html) this week. [Pain News Network](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/8/4/study-finds-85-of-kratom-products-mislabeled), covering the same research on August 4, reported that the problems identified included inaccurate alkaloid content, missing disclosure of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) or its analogs, and misleading dosage information.

## What the study found

The MedicalXpress report described extensive mislabeling of ingredients, strengths, and alkaloid contents across the tested products, and said the results underscore how widely kratom products on the market vary. Consumers often lack accurate information about mitragynine, 7-OH, and other constituents, according to the outlet's coverage.

The missing-disclosure finding lands in a category that spans very different products. Natural kratom leaf contains 7-OH only in trace amounts, while concentrated extracts and synthetic preparations can contain the alkaloid at levels far above what occurs naturally, and the concentrated and synthetic products are the current focus of federal scheduling. When a label does not disclose 7-OH content or the presence of analogs, a purchaser cannot tell where on that spectrum a product falls. The coverage does not specify what share of the mislabeled products were natural leaf preparations versus concentrated or synthetic formulations.

## The finding enters the policy debate

Pain News Network reported that the mislabeling figure is being cited in policy and media discourse as evidence that self-regulation in the kratom industry has largely failed, supporting calls for stricter FDA oversight and DEA scheduling. The outlet also noted that documented mislabeling rates above 80 percent increase litigation and enforcement risk for manufacturers and retailers.

Label accuracy has been a recurring thread in recent regulatory arguments. The R Street Institute, in a [public comment](https://www.rstreet.org/outreach/r-street-comments-on-placement-of-7-oh-on-schedule-i/) on the DEA's 7-OH scheduling action, wrote that the scientific record supports concern about inaccurate labels, undisclosed potency, and unapproved medical claims, and endorsed immediate action on those fronts even as it questioned the order's specific numerical thresholds. Mislabeling has also figured in recent enforcement and coroner reporting, including a [Utah death that state officials attributed to mislabeled synthetic kratom tablets][utah-death-mislabeled-synthetic-kratom-tablets] in July.

The study's publication timing places it in the middle of the federal scheduling story. The DEA's temporary Schedule I order for concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products, which is distinct from any action on natural kratom leaf, [reached its earliest possible effective date this week amid what Pain News Network called enormous confusion][aka-warns-pseudoindoxyl-scheduling-could-reach-natural-leaf] among businesses about how its thresholds apply. At the local level, jurisdictions are writing testing and disclosure rules of their own: [Dare County, North Carolina approved an ordinance on August 3][dare-county-kratom-ordinance-minnesota-age-21] requiring laboratory testing, batch traceability, and labeling standards for kratom products sold in the county.
