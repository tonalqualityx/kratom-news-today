The North Carolina House declined to take up the bill that would ban most intoxicating hemp THC products and outlaw sales of synthetic kratom and xylazine during its session this week, and House Speaker Destin Hall said the measure will not get a vote until after the November election, [Marijuana Moment reported](https://www.marijuanamoment.net/north-carolina-bill-to-ban-hemp-thc-products-wont-get-a-house-vote-until-at-least-november/). The deferral means no new restrictions or formal regulation of kratom will take effect in North Carolina before November at the earliest, leaving the market in its current, largely unregulated state, [according to WFAE](https://www.wfae.org/2026-08-04/nc-house-decides-against-vote-hemp-product-ban).

The stand-down follows a late-July letter from top state law enforcement leaders [pressing the House to pass House Bill 328][north-carolina-law-enforcement-push-hb-328-kratom], the measure that pairs hemp THC controls with regulations for kratom, synthetic kratom, and xylazine.

## Stein faults the bill's kratom provisions

Governor Josh Stein criticized the bill as the House set it aside, WFAE reported, saying it would "legalize" kratom, which he called an "opioid-like substance sometimes called gas station heroin," and that it lacks "meaningful" age restrictions and regulatory structures in its current form.

As drafted, the bill would set 21 as the minimum age for kratom sales but without the permitting and regulatory requirements that would support enforcement, according to WFAE's account of Stein's objections. Stein's position, per the station, is that the bill expands legal access to kratom by explicitly allowing natural kratom products without robust permitting or enforcement mechanisms, and that it should be amended to add stronger protections for children and additional safeguards before passage.

The "gas station heroin" label Stein invoked has appeared most often in national coverage of concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, which contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts naturally present in kratom leaf. Those concentrated products are the subject of the DEA's temporary Schedule I order, [whose earliest effective date arrived this week][aka-warns-pseudoindoxyl-scheduling-could-reach-natural-leaf]; the agency's Federal Register notices state that botanical kratom below the order's 7-OH threshold is outside the federal action.

## Local rules keep moving while the state bill waits

The legislative pause leaves local governments as the most active regulatory layer in North Carolina for now. Dare County commissioners [approved an ordinance this week][dare-county-kratom-ordinance-minnesota-age-21] restricting kratom sales to buyers 21 and older, banning synthetic and chemically altered alkaloids, and capping 7-OH content in products sold in the county.

Similar local activity is playing out elsewhere. In Grant County, Washington, health district officials are urging city and county governments to ban kratom sales, [according to GCJ News](https://www.gcj.news/gc-health-district-officials-seek-kratom-sales-ban-within-county/). The officials said natural kratom is not federally scheduled, that product potency varies, and that long-term health effects have not been well studied, and argued that any regulation must therefore come at the state or local level, per the report. In Illinois, stores selling kratom products must pay a $300 licensing fee, with a $1.31 tax per unit sold, under the state's new framework for hemp and kratom retail, [oakpark.com reported](https://www.oakpark.com/2026/08/03/hemp-kratom-regulations/) on August 3.

The General Assembly's next opportunity to take up House Bill 328 comes after the November election, per Hall's comments reported by Marijuana Moment.
