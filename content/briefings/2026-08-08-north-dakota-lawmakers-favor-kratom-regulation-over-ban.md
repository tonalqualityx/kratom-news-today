Two days after North Dakota's ban on all kratom products took effect, some of the lawmakers charged with writing the state's longer-term policy signaled they would rather regulate kratom than prohibit it outright. A five-legislator workgroup met for the first time on Friday, August 7, at the State Capitol in Bismarck, and members who spoke appeared to agree that concentrated synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) pills are causing the state's problems while expressing concern about banning natural leaf kratom, which contains only trace amounts of the alkaloid, [InForum reported](https://www.inforum.com/news/north-dakota/north-dakota-lawmakers-call-for-regulation-of-kratom-over-flat-out-ban).

The meeting is the first legislative step since Governor Kelly Armstrong's [executive order banning the sale, use, and possession of all kratom products][north-dakota-executive-order-bans-all-kratom] took effect at 5 p.m. on August 5. The 30-day ban reaches natural leaf kratom as well as synthetic 7-OH pills, and Armstrong has called a special session beginning September 2, expected to last at least three days, to write a permanent framework, per InForum.

## Lawmakers separate the leaf from the pills

Representative Eric Murphy, a Republican from Grand Forks, told the workgroup the science behind kratom is complicated, noting that some call concentrated 7-OH "gas station heroin" and that synthetic 7-OH can cause opioid-like symptoms, while natural kratom acts as a stimulant in small doses. "We can't throw all these molecules in together," Murphy said, according to InForum.

Representative SuAnn Olson, a Republican from Baldwin, cited testimony from residents who said leaf kratom provided pain relief in a way nothing else did. "We can't just discount what that did for them and just say, 'Well, there must be something else they can use,'" she said, per the outlet. Senator Todd Beard, a Republican from Williston, said the problem appears to arise when natural products are adulterated, suggested synthetic products could be limited to prescription channels rather than sold over the counter, and said he does not want to ban all kratom if it benefits people.

Armstrong has defended the emergency action, describing kratom as unregulated, addictive, and deadly and saying children can buy it easily; North Dakota has received 50 reports of fatalities connected to kratom since 2019, according to InForum. The State Epidemiology Office has reported a lower figure with a different methodology: kratom or related substances were part of the primary cause of 23 deaths since 2021, plus one death as a contributing cause, [according to KFGO](https://kfgo.com/2026/08/06/1337644/), which also cited state data showing kratom-related Poison Control calls rising from seven in 2024 to 22 in 2025. That epidemiology data [underpinned the state's emergency rulemaking][north-dakota-kratom-deaths-emergency-rules] earlier this month. The reporting does not break down how many of the deaths or poison-control calls involved concentrated 7-OH products versus natural leaf kratom.

Other members questioned the process itself. Representative Lawrence Klemin, a Republican from Bismarck, noted that the Legislature heard extensive testimony on a failed kratom ban in 2025 and approved a study that was never conducted: "And yet here we are going into a special session to do the same thing all over again." Senator Michelle Axtman, a Bismarck Republican co-chairing the workgroup, argued the landscape has shifted since 2025, saying the products are different and calling the old testimony "incredibly outdated." Axtman also pointed to the Drug Enforcement Administration's move against 7-OH as changing the conversation. InForum described the DEA as having temporarily listed 7-OH as Schedule I last month; the DEA's July notices set August 5 as the [earliest date a temporary order could take effect][7oh-comment-window-closes-dea-order-august-5], and no published order had appeared in the Federal Register as of Kratom News Today's August 7 review.

Representative Bernie Satrom, a Republican from Jamestown, raised the ban's immediate human cost, saying he had heard kratom withdrawal "can be as bad as withdrawing from fentanyl" and worrying that people whose supplies run out will lack detox resources and turn to harder drugs. Satrom's characterization of withdrawal severity is his own; the reporting does not attribute it to a clinical source.

## Retailers and advocates press the same distinction

The ban stranded inventory with little warning. Zach Johnson, owner of Sports Vape in Fargo, told InForum that a couple thousand dollars' worth of kratom was en route to his store when Armstrong announced the ban on August 3, giving retailers about three days' notice; the product now sits in storage in another state. Johnson said his store carried natural leaf kratom rather than synthetic 7-OH products, which he said he has opposed from the start: "I've told everyone I know 7-OH is going to be the detriment to the natural kratom industry," he said, [per InForum's explainer](https://www.inforum.com/news/north-dakota/what-is-kratom-and-why-are-some-concerned-about-north-dakota-banning-it-with-little-notice).

Matthew Lowe, executive director of the Global Kratom Coalition, told the outlet that 7-OH products should be banned and kratom leaf age-restricted, and that North Dakota leaders are wrongly lumping the two together. "Do not allow synthetic opioids to hide behind the kratom name," Lowe said. "Don't let the harm that the synthetics have created be used to ban the natural product."

Meanwhile, the compliance machinery is running. Attorney General Drew Wrigley's office publicized disposal locations for kratom products, including law enforcement take-back sites and MedSafe pharmacies, and the state Board of Pharmacy's emergency rule classifying 7-OH as Schedule I remains in effect for about six months while a permanent rulemaking process, including hearings and public comment, unfolds, according to KFGO. The workgroup will take public input when it meets again on Wednesday at the Capitol, per InForum.

North Dakota's deliberations track a broader pattern of states acting ahead of the federal order, most recently Missouri, where the attorney general [barred a St. Louis-area distributor from kratom and 7-OH sales][missouri-ag-bars-green-dragon-kratom-7oh-sales] in an agreement covered in today's companion briefing.
