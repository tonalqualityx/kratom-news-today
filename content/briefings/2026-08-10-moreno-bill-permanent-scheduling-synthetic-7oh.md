Senator Bernie Moreno, a Republican from Ohio, introduced a bill on August 8 that would move federal restrictions on synthetic kratom-derived compounds from temporary regulation into permanent statute. S. 5383 would amend the Controlled Substances Act to place MGM-15 and MGM-16 in Schedule I and to schedule synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in Schedule I above a specific threshold, according to the bill's official title in the [status record published by the U.S. Government Publishing Office](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/s/BILLSTATUS-119s5383.xml). The bill was read twice and referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee the day it was introduced, with no cosponsors listed, [per LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/US/bill/SB5383/2025).

The bill's title identifies its target as synthetic 7-OH, the concentrated compound sold in tablets, shots, and pressed pills, as distinct from the trace amounts of 7-OH that occur naturally in kratom leaf. The title also calls for expanded enforcement actions against manufacturers and distributors of what it describes as "emerging synthetic opioids, commonly known as gas station heroin." The threshold levels the bill would set are not stated in the title, and the full bill text had not yet appeared in the Government Publishing Office's records as of August 10.

## From temporary order to statute

The bill follows the DEA's July 1 announcement that it would [temporarily place high-7-OH products and the same three lab-made compounds in Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], an action [formalized in Federal Register notices published July 6][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule]. Those notices covered botanical material containing more than 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight and processed or synthetic products above 0.050 percent or 1 milligram per unit, thresholds the agency said exclude natural leaf kratom with only trace 7-OH, according to Reuters. The DEA's notices described the temporary scheduling as lasting two years, with a possible one-year extension while permanent scheduling is considered.

A statute would remove that expiration date, making the restrictions independent of the permanent-scheduling review the DEA's notices described as still ahead. The formal status of the temporary action itself remains unsettled: a Kratom News Today review of the Federal Register on August 7 [found no published temporary scheduling order][pennsylvania-7oh-enforcement-san-diego-smoke-shop-lawsuit], even though the DEA's notices established August 5 as the earliest date an order could take effect.

One apparent difference between the bill and the DEA's action sits in the threshold language. The bill's title applies the "above a specific threshold" qualifier to both synthetic 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, while the DEA's companion notice placed pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in Schedule I without threshold qualifiers. The American Kratom Association warned earlier this month that [scheduling pseudoindoxyl without a workable measurement standard could in practice reach natural leaf kratom][aka-warns-pseudoindoxyl-scheduling-could-reach-natural-leaf], arguing there is no reliable way to measure the compound in botanical material. Whether the bill's threshold language speaks to that concern will not be clear until the text is published.

## Congress engages from both directions

The bill arrives as congressional attention to kratom policy builds along separate tracks. Senator Ron Wyden, an Oregon Democrat on the Finance Committee, [opened an inquiry earlier this month][wyden-inquiry-kennedy-kratom-vape-policy] into how the administration's recent kratom and flavored-vape decisions were made, requesting documents and written answers from the Department of Health and Human Services and from Botanic Tonics by the end of August. Wyden's inquiry examines the process behind the federal decisions; Moreno's bill would harden one of their outcomes into law.

In Moreno's home state, an Ohio Board of Pharmacy rule prohibiting the sale of processed kratom products remains in effect after a Franklin County judge [declined to block it][ohio-judge-declines-block-processed-kratom-ban] earlier this month, leaving that restriction in place while the case proceeds to trial.

The Judiciary Committee's [legislation docket](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/committee-activity/legislation) lists no scheduled action on the measure. As introduced, the bill would need to pass both chambers before any of its scheduling provisions took effect.
