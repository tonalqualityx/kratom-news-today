Georgia's legal kratom market includes synthetic, highly concentrated kratom-derived products with misleading potency labels, according to an [investigation published August 10 by The Current](https://thecurrentga.org/2026/08/10/potent-mislabeled-and-easy-to-buy-inside-georgias-kratom-market/), a nonprofit investigative newsroom covering coastal Georgia. Products purchased in the state were tested at the University of Florida's Clinical and Translational Science Institute, and many allegedly violated Georgia's potency laws, the outlet reported. One product tested at 24 times the state's legal limit of one milligram of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) per serving. The products at issue are concentrated or synthetically boosted 7-OH preparations, a category distinct from the trace amounts of 7-OH that occur naturally in kratom leaf.

The investigation lands in a state that chose regulation over prohibition. In 2025, Georgia raised the minimum purchase age to 21, required kratom products to be moved behind counters or into locked cases, banned kratom vaping products, and set the state's first per-serving limits for mitragynine and 7-OH, according to The Current. The outlet reported that kratom remains widely available in gas stations and smoke shops across the state, and that some products on shelves exceed the serving limits the 2025 rules established.

The report's central finding concerns enforcement. Georgia has no dedicated regulatory body and no funding mechanism to test kratom products for compliance with its potency limits, The Current reported, leaving the state's rules largely unverified in practice. The outlet also described an ongoing lawsuit against a Georgia manufacturer over a synthetic kratom product, and characterized the products flowing into the state as part of a roughly $1 billion national industry facing tightening state limits.

On the public health side, The Current reported that Georgia medical examiners detected kratom in more than 740 accidental overdose victims from 2019 to 2025. The reporting describes detection rather than cause of death, and it does not specify how many of those cases involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations versus natural leaf kratom. The outlet also cited Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data showing a 1,200 percent increase in kratom-related poison-center incidents nationally between 2015 and 2025.

Industry advocacy drew the same line the state's potency limits attempt to draw. Mac Haddow, senior fellow on public policy at the American Kratom Association, told The Current that the organization believes there is no credible evidence linking natural kratom to any death, while products with synthetically boosted 7-OH "pose a real risk" and "should be banned." Haddow said those products are "in fact, opioids" with "the same addiction profile," and estimated that synthetically boosted products may make up about 50 percent of the market. The mislabeling findings echo a [national study reported earlier this month that found about 85 percent of tested kratom-derived products did not match their label claims][study-finds-85-percent-kratom-products-mislabeled].

The state-level scrutiny runs parallel to federal action aimed at the same product category. The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) announced July 1 that it would [temporarily place kratom products with elevated 7-OH, along with three synthetic analogs, in Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], an action federal officials described as targeting concentrated and synthetic products rather than natural kratom leaf, according to Reuters. States have diverged on how far to go beyond that line: North Dakota [banned all kratom products under an emergency executive order][north-dakota-executive-order-bans-all-kratom] this month ahead of a September special session, [MPR News reported](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/08/09/north-dakota-banned-kratom-and-set-a-special-session-for-september), while Georgia's framework permits sales within potency limits that, per The Current's testing, the state currently has no mechanism to enforce.
