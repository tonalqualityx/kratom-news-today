The American College of Medical Toxicology (ACMT) published a [position statement](https://www.acmt.net/news/acmt-position-statement-public-health-concerns-associated-with-7-hydroxymitragynine-and-other-kratom-compounds/) on August 11 urging that nonprescription sale of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) be restricted and that use of the compound be limited to monitored clinical research with appropriate oversight. The statement's concern is concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products, sold as tablets, gummies, shots, and strips, which it distinguishes from the trace amounts of 7-OH that occur naturally in kratom leaf.

On the pharmacology, ACMT said 7-OH and related kratom compounds act on opioid receptors, citing animal studies in which 7-OH produced opioid-like effects including respiratory depression. The statement said clinical experience links 7-OH to intoxication, respiratory depression, dependence, and withdrawal, and that concentrated 7-OH products have been linked to reports of overdose and death. By contrast, the statement described mitragynine, the primary alkaloid in kratom leaf, as a relatively weak mu-opioid receptor agonist. ACMT said safe-dose data for 7-OH are lacking and that no proven therapeutic benefits have been established for 7-OH or other kratom-associated compounds.

The statement pairs its policy recommendation with clinical guidance directed at health professionals. ACMT recommended naloxone access, advised against combining 7-OH with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other opioids because of the potential for compounded respiratory depression, and said suspected 7-OH toxicity should be treated as a medical emergency, with naloxone and ventilatory support when needed. The organization also called for clinicians to report toxicity cases to poison centers and the Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) MedWatch program, noting that United States poison-center exposures involving kratom and 7-OH have risen sharply.

ACMT's statement situates itself in the current federal docket. It notes that the FDA recommended in 2025 that 7-OH be placed under the Controlled Substances Act, said the compound is not lawful in dietary supplements or conventional foods, and sent warning letters to companies marketing 7-OH consumer products rather than natural leaf kratom. The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) followed with notices of intent, [announced July 1 and published in the Federal Register days later][federal-register-publishes-dea-7oh-scheduling-rule], to temporarily place 7-OH products above specified thresholds into Schedule I along with mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16. ACMT noted that traditional kratom products containing no more than 0.05 percent 7-OH by dry weight are excluded from the proposed federal action, whose [30-day statutory waiting period ended August 5][7oh-comment-window-closes-dea-order-august-5]. Kratom itself remains unscheduled federally, the statement said, with states applying a patchwork of regulations and bans.

The position statement arrives in a week of state and local movement on the same product category, including [Dutchess County, New York's unanimous vote to ban concentrated and synthetic kratom products][dutchess-county-bans-concentrated-synthetic-kratom-products].
