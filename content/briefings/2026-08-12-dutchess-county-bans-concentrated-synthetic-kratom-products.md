Dutchess County legislators voted unanimously on August 10 to ban the sale of concentrated and synthetic kratom products, according to the [Poughkeepsie Journal](https://www.poughkeepsiejournal.com/story/news/local/2026/08/11/high-potency-lab-enhanced-kratom-products-to-be-banned-in-dutchess/91253458007/). The products covered by the resolution are concentrated or lab-made 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, a category distinct from the trace 7-OH that occurs naturally in kratom leaf. The outlet reported that the measure names specific derivatives and extends to future analogues designed to evade regulation, and that it targets products commonly sold in convenience stores, gas stations, and smoke shops.

The enforcement mechanism is civil, according to the [Times Union](https://www.timesunion.com/health/article/dutchess-county-bans-kratom-sales-22382405.php). Sellers face fines of $1,500 for a first violation and up to $3,000 for subsequent violations, with an additional $500 penalty for sales to anyone under 21. The measure also bars online platforms from shipping the targeted products to addresses in the county, a provision that reaches e-commerce sellers with no physical presence in the Hudson Valley. [News 12](https://longisland.news12.com/2026/08/11/dutchess-county-lawmakers-vote-to-ban-high-potency-kratom/666luRFkO9AN7ktPXVkSFz) reported the vote as part of a broader county effort against what local officials called "gas station kratom."

The county acted against a backdrop of escalating state attention. The Times Union reported that state officials have linked kratom to at least 100 deaths in New York; the reporting does not specify how many of those cases involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations rather than natural leaf kratom. New York enacted a statewide under-21 sales ban and warning-label requirements in December 2025, and the state legislature voted in June to ban kratom entirely, though Gov. Kathy Hochul had not signed that broader bill as of the Times Union's reporting.

Industry reaction in the coverage focused on the line between leaf products and enhanced ones. The Poughkeepsie Journal reported that the Global Kratom Coalition (GKC) has praised New York's 2025 kratom laws as "prudent regulation" grounded in science, a position consistent with the group's support for restricting high-potency and synthetic 7-OH products while defending access to natural leaf.

The county resolution tracks the same product category the federal government is moving against. The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) [announced July 1 that it intends to temporarily place 7-OH above specified thresholds, along with mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, into Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], an action the agency described as excluding traditional kratom products below the threshold. Local governments have increasingly written that distinction into their own rules: Dare County, North Carolina [approved an ordinance capping 7-OH content earlier this month][dare-county-kratom-ordinance-minnesota-age-21], and the [American College of Medical Toxicology this week urged restricting nonprescription 7-OH sales nationally][acmt-position-statement-restrict-nonprescription-7oh-sales]. North Dakota has gone further than the concentrated-product line, with [a statewide ban on all kratom now in its first weeks of enforcement][north-dakota-kratom-ban-no-violations-education-push].
