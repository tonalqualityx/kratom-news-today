Physicians in Chicago have documented opioid-like withdrawal in a 43-year-old man who abruptly stopped taking high-dose 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, according to a case report in the Journal of the American Pharmacists Association [described August 16, 2026 by ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/doctors-just-documented-what-happens-when-you-quit-high-dose-7-oh-kratom-cold-turkey). The products at issue are concentrated 7-OH preparations, which ScienceAlert describes as "chemically enriched or semi-synthetic" and which deliver the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts that occur naturally in kratom leaf. Such products bear "little resemblance to traditional kratom leaf or tea, despite frequent marketing," the outlet reported.

According to the case report as summarized by ScienceAlert, the patient had been using approximately 360 milligrams of 7-OH daily and presented about 48 hours after stopping, with nausea, diarrhea, abdominal cramping, restlessness, chills, clamminess, and anxiety. Urine testing ruled out typical opioids as a cause, per the report. The treating clinicians administered buprenorphine and naloxone at a 4-1 milligram dose, along with clonidine, antiemetics, antidiarrheals, muscle relaxants, and over-the-counter pain relievers.

## A pending federal order shapes the clinical backdrop

The report arrives while the federal status of concentrated 7-OH remains unsettled. The Drug Enforcement Administration announced its intent in July to temporarily place 7-OH above specified concentration thresholds into Schedule I, along with three synthetic kratom-derived compounds, and the [statutory earliest date for the final order arrived August 5][7oh-comment-window-closes-dea-order-august-5]. As of mid-August, ScienceAlert reported, the Federal Register listing still showed the 7-OH action as a proposed temporary placement rather than a published final order. The agency's proposal targets concentrated and synthetic products above its thresholds, and botanical leaf below those thresholds is excluded, according to the DEA's published notices.

The case report adds a documented clinical account to concerns the medical community has already raised. The American College of Medical Toxicology, in a position statement covered here earlier this month, [linked 7-OH to opioid-like effects including dependence and withdrawal][acmt-position-statement-restrict-nonprescription-7oh-sales] and urged restrictions on nonprescription sales of potent preparations.

## Ripples beyond the clinic

The same pending order is registering in corporate disclosures. LFTD Partners, which counts kratom-derived products as one of its two major lines of business, [told investors in an August 14 quarterly filing][lftd-partners-10q-7oh-scheduling-revenue-risk] that the proposals, if finalized, could materially reduce its revenue, "potentially by approximately half or more," according to the company's 10-Q [published via StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/LIFD/10-q-lftd-partners-inc-quarterly-earnings-report-59ff2cdbf166.html). On the clinical side, ScienceAlert reported that the DEA's scheduling announcement has raised concerns among healthcare workers about the management challenges that lie ahead for patients dependent on these products, challenges the Chicago case documents at the level of a single patient.
