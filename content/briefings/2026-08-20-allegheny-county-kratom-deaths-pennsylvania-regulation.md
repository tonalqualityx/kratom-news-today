The [Pittsburgh Post-Gazette reported on August 20](https://www.post-gazette.com/news/health/2026/08/20/kratom-allegheny-county-deaths/stories/202608170048) that Allegheny County has documented a second kratom-associated death and that county officials describe multiple recent death cases in which mitragynine, kratom's primary alkaloid, appeared on death certificates. The paper reported that Pennsylvania lawmakers are grappling with how to regulate a substance that remains legal in the state but largely unregulated. The reporting does not specify whether the deaths involved concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, which are distinct from the trace 7-OH naturally present in kratom leaf, or natural leaf products.

The Post-Gazette described kratom as "touted as a lifesaver by some and cautioned as a villain by others," a divide the paper drew between consumers who report using it for pain or opioid withdrawal and the officials tracking the county's death investigations. The paper explained that 7-OH is extracted from kratom to create potent commercial products, a process it compared to THC extraction in cannabis, and noted the Drug Enforcement Administration's (DEA) July move against 7-OH products above a 0.05 percent threshold. The agency signed notices of intent on July 1, but the [temporary scheduling order itself had not appeared in the Federal Register as of August 18][dea-7oh-temporary-scheduling-order-still-unpublished].

## An enforcement posture without a state statute

According to an [August 18 analysis](https://cannabislawpa.com/2026/08/18/when-convenience-stores-sell-7-hydroxy-or-7-0h-to-customers-they-are-selling-potent-opioids-the-federal-government-just-made-this-illegal-by-judith-cassel/) by attorney Judith Cassel at Cannabis Law PA, Pennsylvania has not enacted a kratom-specific statute. The analysis reports that the Pennsylvania Attorney General's Office has publicly stated it is prepared to enforce the federal Schedule I treatment of concentrated 7-OH, a position [the office signaled earlier this month][pennsylvania-7oh-enforcement-san-diego-smoke-shop-lawsuit], and anticipates criminal enforcement where concentrated 7-OH products are manufactured, distributed, or sold in the state once the federal order is effective. Cassel's commentary warns convenience stores that manufactured or extracted products containing more than 0.05 percent 7-OH, or more than one milligram per unit, would be illegal under the pending federal action, while plain leaf below the threshold would remain unscheduled federally.

Clinical commentary in the state is striking a similar note. [Penn State Health's "Medical Minute"](https://pennstatehealthnews.org/2026/08/the-medical-minute-what-is-7-oh-the-dangerous-addiction-risk-sold-at-convenience-stores/), published August 19, describes 7-OH as a high-potency alkaloid that occurs naturally in trace amounts in the kratom plant and carries addiction risk especially when isolated or synthesized at high concentrations. The commentary states that the DEA's action targets concentrated, synthetic 7-OH and related compounds, and that the kratom plant itself is not being placed into Schedule I under the federal move.

Warnings tied to 7-OH products are surfacing beyond Pennsylvania. In Colorado, [Steamboat Radio reported](https://www.steamboatradio.com/2026/08/18/craig-and-steamboat-springs-police-warn-of-deaths-from-7-oh-and-kratom-products/) that police in Craig and Steamboat Springs warned on August 18 of deaths linked to 7-OH and kratom products, citing the Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) identification of risks including addiction, withdrawal, and seizures, and pointing to the DEA's July 1 notice of intent. The outlet's account of the warnings does not specify whether the Colorado deaths involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations, natural leaf kratom, or both. The local warnings land amid new national data: a study in the journal Addiction reported a [65-fold rise in U.S. poison center kratom cases][addiction-study-poison-center-kratom-cases-surge] between 2010 and 2023, a figure the Post-Gazette's reporting places alongside the county's death investigations in the policy debate.
