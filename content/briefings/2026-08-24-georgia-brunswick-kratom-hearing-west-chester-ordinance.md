Georgia's House Blue-Ribbon Study Committee on Youth Exposure to Kratom and Retail-Available Substances held a public meeting in Brunswick on Friday, August 21, drawing policymakers, doctors, and community members to debate whether kratom needs tighter regulation in the state, [WSAV reported](https://www.wsav.com/news/local-news/georgia-news/kratom-regulations-pros-and-cons-discussed-amongst-georgia-representatives/). The session ran from 9 a.m. to 1 p.m. at the Southeast Georgia Conference Center on the College of Coastal Georgia campus, according to The Brunswick News. According to the station, presentations covered links between adolescent brain development and addiction, kratom's therapeutic potential, the absence of FDA approval, fatality statistics, federal safety warnings, and the product's availability in retail locations such as gas stations. Presenters did not converge on a single recommendation, per WSAV: some urged limiting kratom sales in convenience stores and gas stations while making unadulterated kratom products available through healthcare professionals, and others advocated banning kratom in Georgia altogether.

Testimony reflected the same split, according to WSAV. Cammie Wolf Rice, who supports regulation, told the committee that "there may be wonderful things Kratom can do, but selling it in a gas station is terrifying," per the station. Hilary Tesluck, executive director of the group End Kratom Addiction, described beginning regular use on the assumption that a natural supplement carried minimal risk, WSAV reported. Christian Warren, a kratom user, told the committee the product let him manage pain while continuing to function, according to the station. Mac Haddow of the American Kratom Association (AKA) directed his remarks at adulterated products, per WSAV: "Spiked Kratom products that use industrial-grade solvents are dangerous; let's get them off the marketplace."

The Brunswick meeting followed a contentious week for the committee. The AKA [filed a formal complaint with Georgia House Speaker Jon Burns on August 21][aka-formal-complaint-georgia-kratom-study-committee] alleging bias in how Chairman Rick Townsend is administering the committee. Ahead of the meeting, dozens of people sent coordinated emails to the committee on Wednesday and Thursday, [The Brunswick News reported](https://thebrunswicknews.com/news/local_news/kratom-supporters-send-email-blitz-ahead-of-committee-meeting-in-brunswick/article_3e829006-82ef-41cc-a8b5-c3d4f7435226.html), writing that "at the committee's first kratom meeting, Chairman Townsend did not allow kratom consumers or advocates the opportunity to testify." Townsend, a Republican from St. Simons Island, chairs the committee, which was formed to study youth exposure to kratom and other retail-available substances in a state where [reporting has documented potency and labeling gaps in the kratom market][georgia-kratom-investigation-7oh-limits-testing-gap].

In Pennsylvania, West Chester's borough council sent a proposed kratom ordinance back to committee for further refinement, the [Philadelphia Inquirer reported on August 21](https://www.inquirer.com/news/pennsylvania/west-chester-smoke-vape-shops-kratom-20260821.html). The draft would have prohibited the sale and public use of kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) in the borough while leaving possession and private use untouched, according to the paper. Council member Brian Travis objected to the measure's scope, per the Inquirer: "I think this is a big lane violation, quite frankly. We are not the FDA." Council member Bernie Flynn said incoming West Chester University students he spoke with had not heard of kratom, the paper reported. At the same meeting, the council approved zoning limits on where smoke shops can open, a step the Inquirer reported came after neighborhood pushback over a shop's location.

The Inquirer noted that the National Institutes of Health has said kratom can have effects similar to opioids and stimulants, and that the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) announced plans in July to temporarily place 7-OH in Schedule I of the Controlled Substances Act. That federal action targets concentrated and synthetic 7-OH preparations rather than natural leaf kratom, which contains the alkaloid only in trace amounts, and the [temporary scheduling order had not yet been published in the Federal Register as of August 18][dea-7oh-temporary-scheduling-order-still-unpublished].

Both deliberations add to the list of state and local bodies studying kratom rules rather than enacting them outright, a pattern seen last week when [Naperville, Illinois debated a retail ban while Marion County, Florida paused its own][naperville-council-debates-kratom-retail-ban] to await federal action. In Pennsylvania, the West Chester referral follows [reporting earlier this month on kratom-involved deaths recorded by Allegheny County's medical examiner][allegheny-county-kratom-deaths-pennsylvania-regulation] and the statewide regulation debate that coverage described.
