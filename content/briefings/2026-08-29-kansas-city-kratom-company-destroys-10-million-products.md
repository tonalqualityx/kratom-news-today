A Kansas City-area kratom company destroyed roughly $10 million in kratom-related products after the Drug Enforcement Administration's emergency scheduling order took effect, [KCUR reported](https://www.kcur.org/health/2026-08-29/kansas-city-kratom-company-american-shaman-cbd-dea) August 29. The destroyed inventory relates to the [DEA's temporary Schedule I order for three synthetic kratom-related compounds][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] (mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, also called MGPI, along with MGM-15 and MGM-16), lab-derived substances the agency distinguishes from natural leaf kratom, which the order does not schedule. KCUR, whose reporting identifies the company as American Shaman, a name known for its CBD line, said the destruction reflected attorney-guided risk assessments that certain products might contain MGPI or other newly scheduled compounds at levels that could be considered manufactured or enriched rather than merely incidental.

The [Missouri Independent reported](https://missouriindependent.com/2026/08/28/missouri-kratom-company-destroys-10m-in-products-after-dea-crackdown/) August 28 that the company's move followed the federal crackdown on what the Department of Justice has called deliberately manufactured and concentrated opioid products, with the two-year temporary order now in place. According to the Missouri Independent, the episode illustrates the business uncertainty the order has created around testing, product formulation, and inventory management, particularly where synthetic enhancement or contamination is possible.

## The gap between the news release and the order text

The American Kratom Association (AKA), which supports the scheduling of the three synthetic compounds while opposing any application of the order to natural leaf, pointed to the destruction as evidence that the order's wording needs clarification. AKA senior fellow Mac Haddow told [KRCG](https://krcgtv.com/news/local/missouri-kratom-company-destroys-10m-in-products-after-dea-crackdown) that the Justice Department explains enforcement discretion for incidental trace amounts in its news release, but that the discretion is not stated in the temporary order itself. Haddow said the AKA supports what he called aggressive enforcement against chemically manipulated MGPI, MGM-15, and MGM-16 products, while seeking legal certainty that trace, natural presence of these compounds in kratom leaf does not result in Schedule I treatment. That gap is the basis of the [federal lawsuit the AKA filed in Washington][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order], which asks a court to confirm that natural kratom products with only incidental trace amounts of MGPI fall outside the order.

The KRCG coverage frames the Missouri destruction as an example of how ambiguity in the order's scope can carry major economic consequences, the argument Haddow and the AKA are making in court.

## Companies reposition while the 7-OH proposal stays open

KCUR reported that the destruction fits a broader compliance pattern: divestment or destruction of high-risk inventory and reformulation toward pure leaf or low-potency products that fall clearly outside the DEA order's scope. Companies are positioning against the pending federal action on 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) as well as the finalized synthetic-compound order, per KCUR. The DEA's separate proposal would place 7-OH above certain thresholds into Schedule I, and the Missouri Independent reported the proposed lines at more than 0.05 percent 7-OH by dry weight or more than 1 milligram of 7-OH per dose. That proposal has not been finalized, and the [public comment period runs through September 10][hhs-extends-7oh-comment-period-september-10], per the Missouri Independent.

KCUR's report emphasized the science underlying the distinction regulators are drawing: mitragynine is the main alkaloid in kratom leaves, 7-OH is present naturally only in small amounts, and the newly scheduled compounds are concentrated or synthetic derivatives rather than ordinary leaf material. The DEA has said its action targets deliberately manufactured and concentrated opioid products, not traditional botanical kratom, per KCUR. For manufacturers, the practical question the $10 million destruction raises is where that line sits in a warehouse: the answer, for now, is coming from company lawyers rather than the order's text.
