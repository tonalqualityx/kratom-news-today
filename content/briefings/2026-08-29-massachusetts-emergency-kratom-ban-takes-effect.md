Massachusetts' emergency order temporarily placing all forms of kratom into Schedule I under state law took effect Friday, August 28, [Governor Maura Healey's office announced](https://www.mass.gov/news/reminder-governor-healeys-order-protecting-massachusetts-residents-from-dangerous-kratom-products-goes-into-effect-today). The order, [issued by Public Health Commissioner Robbie Goldstein on August 13][massachusetts-emergency-order-all-kratom-schedule-one], covers natural leaf kratom as well as concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, and it authorizes local boards of health and municipal officials to enforce the ban against retailers for up to one year, per the governor's office and prior state announcements.

[WWLP reported](https://www.wwlp.com/news/massachusetts/massachusetts-emergency-restriction-on-kratom-goes-into-effect/) that the state characterized the action as a response to widespread sale of unregulated kratom products in smoke shops, gas stations, and convenience stores. Local boards of health [had been preparing enforcement][massachusetts-boards-of-health-prepare-kratom-enforcement] since the order was announced, with some towns ordering retailers to clear shelves ahead of the Friday deadline.

The order took effect with litigation unresolved. [Four retailers of natural kratom leaf products sued][massachusetts-retailers-sue-emergency-kratom-ban] in Suffolk County Superior Court to block the ban, and [WBUR reported](https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/08/27/kratom-ban-massachusetts-lawsuit-retailers) that their attorneys argue the emergency order did not meet procedural and substantive requirements set by the Legislature and improperly lumps traditional botanical kratom together with highly concentrated synthetic products. None of the coverage reviewed for this briefing reports a court ruling on the retailers' challenge before the effective date.

## Researchers and health officials split on the blanket approach

Coverage on the effective date shows real disagreement about the order's scope. [The Boston Globe reported](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/28/metro/kratom-ban-pushback/) that some advocates argue the blanket ban is more dangerous than certain forms of the drug itself, on the theory that prohibition could drive use underground and reduce quality control. The Globe cited Oliver Grundmann, a clinical professor at the University of Florida's College of Pharmacy and a long-time kratom researcher, who described dose-dependent risk and said whole-leaf kratom is very unlikely to cause serious adverse effects in most users, while acknowledging risks he called non-trivial, including drug interactions and insufficient research on use during pregnancy or breastfeeding. Grundmann's position, as the Globe presented it, favors expanded, structured research over prohibition, and he distinguished traditional leaf products, which primarily contain mitragynine with only trace 7-OH, from highly concentrated extracts and synthetic derivatives that he said carry substantially higher risk.

[Northeastern Global News reported](https://news.northeastern.edu/2026/08/28/kratom-ban-massachusetts/) the other side of that split: more than 100 deaths in Massachusetts since 2020 have been cited as public-health justification for the emergency action, and some health officials argue that even leaf products have been implicated in deaths and support broad bans like the state's. The Northeastern piece also noted that the order aligns the state's Schedule I definition with the federal standard of no currently accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse.

## A broader sweep than the federal action

The Massachusetts order remains broader than the federal activity it partly references. The Drug Enforcement Administration's [temporary Schedule I order that took effect August 26][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] covers three synthetic kratom-related compounds, and the Department of Justice has said that action targets deliberately manufactured and concentrated opioid products rather than traditional botanical kratom. Massachusetts, by contrast, now treats natural leaf and synthetic 7-OH products alike under state Schedule I. That distinction between product categories is the central factual dispute in the retailers' lawsuit, and with the order now in force, the state courts will decide whether it survives.
