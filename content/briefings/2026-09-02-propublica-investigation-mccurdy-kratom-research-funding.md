A [ProPublica investigation](https://www.propublica.org/article/kratom-legalization-christopher-mccurdy-research-conflict-of-interest-aka) published September 1, 2026, examines whether Christopher McCurdy, a University of Florida professor and one of the country's most prominent kratom researchers, properly disclosed financial support from the American Kratom Association (AKA) and its nonprofit arm. According to the report, McCurdy accepted travel reimbursements, donations to the UF Foundation, and funding for an annual kratom research symposium from the AKA and the Center for Plant Science and Health, and those relationships did not appear in his conflict-of-interest statements or on scientific papers he has published.

ProPublica reports that the AKA, through its nonprofit arm, has donated more than $100,000 to the University of Florida to support kratom research activities, including the annual symposium, which the article says brings together about 100 scientists to study kratom's therapeutic potential. The story also reports that McCurdy estimated about $1 million in industry donations to a foundation supporting his work, funding he did not fully disclose in certain contexts. The article frames the disclosure questions as a matter of research independence and regulatory capture in the kratom policy process. McCurdy's work is central to current kratom science: University of Florida researchers are running the [first human trial of purified mitragynine as an experimental opioid use disorder treatment][nih-clears-mitragynine-opioid-use-disorder-trial] with the NIH, and a UF team [published an analysis of more than 40 commercial kratom products in August][university-of-florida-study-mislabeled-kratom-products] that found widespread mislabeling.

The AKA disputed that the arrangements required disclosure. Mac Haddow, the organization's senior fellow on public policy, told ProPublica that beneficiaries are not typically required to disclose travel-related payments and that the AKA has never commissioned a study from McCurdy that would trigger formal disclosure obligations. "We think that science should dictate what the public policies are," Haddow said in the article, adding that the organization relies on McCurdy and other scientists to give policymakers accurate information but has no control over what they say. The report notes that McCurdy has publicly sounded alarms about synthetic derivatives including MGM-15, MGM-16, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, positions that align with the AKA's push to ban those compounds, [three of which the DEA temporarily placed in Schedule I on August 26][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds]. The AKA [has separately sued over that order][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order], asking a federal court to confirm it does not reach botanical kratom. ProPublica's reporting does not include a formal institutional response from the University of Florida.

The investigation arrives during an active stretch of kratom policymaking that draws directly on the research record. State officials are moving against the products, with [Iowa's governor calling for a total statewide ban this week][iowa-governor-reynolds-calls-total-statewide-kratom-ban] and [Albany County, New York's retail sales ban taking effect September 1][albany-county-kratom-sales-ban-takes-effect], while federal review of 7-hydroxymitragynine scheduling thresholds remains open through a comment period ending September 10.
