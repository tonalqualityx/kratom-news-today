The North Dakota Senate voted 46-1 on September 3, 2026, to pass Senate Bill 2408, a bill that as amended would ban kratom and its derivatives statewide, covering natural leaf kratom, which contains 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) only in trace amounts, as well as concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products, [KFGO reported](https://kfgo.com/2026/09/03/n-d-senate-passes-a-bill-banning-kratom-in-a-46-1-vote/). Republican Sen. Jeff Magrum of Hazelton cast the only no vote, according to the station. The same day, the House passed a competing measure, House Bill 1628, by 86-6, [InForum reported](https://www.inforum.com/news/north-dakota/kratom-ban-bill-easily-advances-in-north-dakota-special-session). That bill would allow natural leaf kratom for adults 21 and older under a licensing and labeling regime. The two chambers now swap the bills for second votes on Friday, September 4, 2026, per InForum.

The floor votes came a day after a joint House-Senate policy committee [advanced both bills with do-pass recommendations][north-dakota-committee-advances-permanent-full-kratom-ban] following Governor Kelly Armstrong's State of the State address.

## What each bill does

SB 2408 started as a bill targeting synthetic kratom derivatives, according to KFYR-TV. As amended by its sponsor, Sen. Michelle Axtman, R-Bismarck, it classifies kratom alkaloids and derivatives as controlled substances at the same level as heroin and LSD, which Axtman described as "essentially a blanket ban on all kratom and kratom derivatives," [Prairie Public reported](https://news.prairiepublic.org/local-news/2026-09-03/kratom-bills-advance-including-a-permanent-full-ban-in-north-dakota). That scope reaches natural leaf and the concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products that have been the focus of federal action alike.

Under the amended bill, a first offense is an infraction carrying a fine of up to $1,000, a second offense is a Class A misdemeanor with up to a year in jail and a $3,000 fine, and three or more possession offenses rise to a Class C felony punishable by up to five years in prison and a $10,000 fine, according to Prairie Public. KFGO reported that the bill also allows judges to let people convicted of kratom possession participate in drug court.

HB 1628, sponsored by Rep. Pat Heinert, R-Bismarck, takes the regulatory path. According to [KFGO's account of the September 2 hearing](https://kfgo.com/2026/09/02/public-lawmakers-debate-kratom-regulation-versus-full-ban-in-north-dakota/), it would restrict natural leaf kratom to adults 21 and older, license processors and retailers, set labeling and lab testing requirements, prohibit products that look like candy, require ingredient lists and health disclaimers, give the attorney general enforcement authority, and fund a $20,000 public health campaign. A fiscal note attached to the bill estimates $3.7 million in licensing and testing costs through 2029, per InForum. InForum also noted that HB 1628 does not regulate synthetic kratom, so concentrated 7-OH products would remain legal under state law if the ban bill fails.

That gap sits against a federal backdrop. As KNT has reported, the Drug Enforcement Administration's [temporary Schedule I order for three synthetic kratom-related compounds][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] took effect August 26, 2026, while 7-OH itself remains subject to a pending threshold proposal. Armstrong separately approved an emergency pharmacy rule on 7-OH last month, [Valley News Live reported](https://www.valleynewslive.com/2026/09/02/governor-urges-permanent-ban-all-kratom-north-dakota/).

## Floor debate

Supporters of the ban framed the vote as regional leadership. Sen. Kristin Roers, R-Fargo, told the chamber that being on an island means having one's head above water and that North Dakota has a chance to lead its region, according to KFGO. Sen. Janne Myrdal, R-Edinburg, rejected any distinction between forms of the plant, saying, "There isn't God's kratom and Lucifer's kratom," and calling the drug highly addictive, per InForum.

Skeptics focused on the criminal penalties. Sen. Ryan Braunberger, D-Fargo, said he was concerned about the consequences of possession charges because he did not want to criminalize people with addiction, KFGO reported. Magrum told KFGO host Joel Heitkamp that he personally uses kratom. In the House, Rep. Eric Murphy, R-Grand Forks, a University of North Dakota biomedical sciences professor, voted for the regulation bill and argued that kratom is safer than THC products the Legislature already permits at levels well above the FDA limit, [Y94 reported](https://y94.com/2026/09/03/north-dakota-house-passes-ban-on-the-sale-of-natural-kratom-to-those-under-age-21/). InForum quoted Murphy warning that a ban would "do a lot of harm to an awful lot of people." Rep. Ben Koppelman, R-West Fargo, said lawmakers had not had enough time to know what the outcome of such a bill would be, per Y94.

The six House no votes on HB 1628 came from Democrats Gretchen Dobervich, Collette Brown, and Lisa Finley-Deville and Republicans TJ Brown, Dwight Kiefert, and Nico Rios, according to Y94.

## The governor's position

Armstrong asked the Legislature to ban kratom "in all its forms" during his September 2, 2026, address, according to Valley News Live. The station reported that he pointed to overdoses and deaths from products sold off the shelf with no regulation or age restriction, and that he said he wanted verifiable data rather than anecdotal testimonials. He also urged care on penalties: "I don't want to make someone a felon in September for something they could buy easily over the counter in July," per the station.

The State Epidemiology Office reported that kratom or related substances were the primary cause of 23 deaths in North Dakota since 2021 and a contributing cause in one more, according to KFGO. That reporting does not break out how many of those deaths involved concentrated 7-OH products versus natural leaf kratom. Armstrong's [executive order banning all kratom products][north-dakota-executive-order-bans-all-kratom] took effect August 5, 2026, as a temporary measure, [KFYR-TV reported](https://www.kfyrtv.com/2026/09/02/north-dakota-lawmakers-consider-total-kratom-ban-during-special-session/), and the governor called the special session to replace it with statute.

## What happens Friday

Each bill needs passage by the other chamber and the governor's signature to become law. Rep. Todd Porter, R-Mandan, flagged the risk of banning both natural and synthetic kratom in one bill when the chambers' votes were uncertain, Prairie Public reported. InForum wrote that it is unclear how much support exists in the House for a total ban. KFYR-TV reported that no bill becomes law until Friday's final votes.

North Dakota's vote lands as local enforcement against 7-OH products continues elsewhere: Michigan regulators [seized hundreds of kratom and 7-OH products from a Canton tobacco shop][canton-michigan-inspection-seizes-kratom-7oh-products] in a joint inspection reported this week.
