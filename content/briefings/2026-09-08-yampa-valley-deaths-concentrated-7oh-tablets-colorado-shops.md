Two men died in Colorado's Yampa Valley this spring and summer after taking concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) tablets, products sold as 60 mg and 80 mg tablets and distinct from the trace 7-OH that occurs naturally in kratom leaf, the [Steamboat Pilot reported](https://www.steamboatpilot.com/news/synthetic-kratom-7-oh-led-to-two-yampa-valley-deaths/) on September 7, 2026. The paper's account, by Suzie Romig, found that similar tablets were still available by request at a Steamboat Springs shop as of September 3.

## The two deaths

Max Cowell, a 29-year-old Denver resident, died in Steamboat Springs in May 2026 after consuming one-fourth of a 60 mg grape-flavored Pocket Sevens tablet purchased at The Y-Not Shop downtown, according to the Pilot. The Routt County Coroner's Office ruled the death accidental, citing "complications of combined Alprazolam and 'kratom' toxicity," per the paper. The Pilot reported that Cowell had also consumed alcohol and was taking anxiety medication. The coroner's wording refers to kratom, while the product the paper identified was a concentrated 7-OH tablet; the article does not indicate that the coroner's office distinguished between the two.

The second death involved a 23-year-old Westminster man found June 21 in a McDonald's parking lot in Craig, according to the Pilot. A Grand Junction medical examiner attributed the death to "drug 7-hydroxymitragynine intoxication," the paper reported. The product was an 80 mg Dopium-brand tablet in a grape slushie flavor, purchased at a shop in Dinosaur; the packaging indicated a serving of one-half tablet per 24 hours, and two tablets were missing, per the Pilot. The man had been working in the oil industry in Utah, according to the paper.

The Pilot reported that the Pocket Sevens packaging instructed users to "start with 1/16 or 1/8 of tablet" and carried a QR code and website address that did not function. Current 7-OH products, the paper noted, carry extensive warnings on the reverse of the packaging, and the tablets are distributed by companies based in Wyoming and California.

## What clinicians and police said

Tracey Wall, a physician assistant at Porch Light Health in Craig, told the Pilot that the primary cause of death in these overdoses is respiratory depression, in which victims "stop breathing essentially." Wall said that "synthetic kratom is highly addictive and can lead to withdrawal symptoms," and that a product "can seem innocent, and it can be advertised as organic and healthy, but really it is more powerful than morphine," according to the paper.

Craig Police Lt. Anthony Fandel told the Pilot, "It's a very dangerous product. Stay away from it."

David Kroll, Ph.D., of the University of Colorado Anschutz, said the compounds "have great health risks when combined with any other depressant of the central nervous system," per the Pilot. Kroll also pointed to a resource gap: "The problem with all of these temporary rescheduling efforts is that they come with no additional funding."

Cowell's mother, Stephanie Rausser, told the paper, "The fact that this substance is so toxic and readily available, there is not even room for all that rage yet," and said she did not want her son's death "dismissed as simply another young person making a bad choice."

## Still on sale

When the Pilot visited The Y-Not Shop on September 3, natural kratom products were displayed on open shelves, and three types of synthetic 7-OH were available "by request" and stored out of public view, the paper reported. The article did not include a statement from the shop or from either product's distributor.

The Pilot framed the continued availability against the federal picture. The Drug Enforcement Administration's [temporary Schedule I order][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] placed mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 under federal control in late August, but the paper noted that the order does not cover 7-OH itself, which remains unscheduled federally. A separate federal proposal to set a 7-OH concentration threshold is [open for public comment through September 10][hhs-extends-7oh-comment-period-september-10]. The Pilot's reporting does not describe any Colorado state restriction on 7-OH products, and it describes natural kratom as legal in the state for adults 21 and older.

State action elsewhere moved this week. North Dakota's governor [signed a law on September 4 scheduling concentrated 7-OH and related derivatives][north-dakota-armstrong-signs-hb-1628-synthetic-kratom-ban-house-rejects-total-ban] under state law while licensing natural leaf sales.
