The Pennsylvania Department of Health is "reviewing the situation and considering all options" after two county prosecutors asked it to use emergency powers against 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), a department spokesperson told the [Pennsylvania Capital-Star](https://penncapital-star.com/health-care/health-experts-lawmakers-push-for-pa-kratom-regulation-proponents-warn-theyll-fight-a-ban/) in a report published September 8, 2026. The article, which surveys the state's regulatory debate, reported that kratom and more potent related products remain legal in Pennsylvania while multiple bills to regulate or ban them move through the legislature.

## The state's numbers

According to the Department of Health, as reported by the Capital-Star, there have been 17 overdose deaths in Pennsylvania this year in which kratom was a contributing factor, a count that includes cases where kratom was found alongside other substances. There were 33 such deaths in 2025, and the department described the data as preliminary. The figures do not separate concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products from natural leaf kratom, so the reporting does not establish which products were involved.

The Pennsylvania Poison Control Center's kratom-related cases do break out 7-OH, per the Capital-Star. The center logged 114 cases last year, including 25 involving 7-OH, and 106 cases through June 30 of this year, including 41 involving 7-OH. Natural kratom leaf contains 7-OH only in trace amounts; the concentrated and lab-made products that carry far higher levels of the alkaloid are the focus of the pending federal scheduling process.

## What clinicians and researchers told the paper

Sarah Kawasaki, an addiction medicine specialist at Penn State and medical director of its associated opioid treatment program, told the Capital-Star she is treating 10 to 20 patients in central Pennsylvania exclusively for kratom use, and that about 80 percent of them had previously sought treatment for opioid addiction. "Some people know that it can cause dependence and harm, and are intentionally using it that way," Kawasaki said. "But some people don't. Then they get started on it and don't realize they're dependent until they stop." Asked about relative risk, she said: "Is it orders of magnitude less dangerous than fentanyl? Probably. But we don't really know the definitive answer to that."

Laura Nettuno, a researcher at the RAND Corporation's drug policy research center, told the paper the product category occupies "a very complex space in the substance use landscape." In her words, for some people it functions as harm reduction because it helps manage opioid cravings and withdrawal symptoms, while at the same time "it's creating its own issue." She pointed to extract products with larger amounts of the plant's alkaloids and to synthetic products with higher 7-OH concentrations. "More potent synthetic products are coming out of these labs, and they're tweaking them faster than regulation can keep up with," Nettuno said, adding that "any researcher who is doing their job well should tell you we really have no idea" about use prevalence at the state level.

The Capital-Star also cited a University of Michigan study that found more than half of reported kratom users had struggled with substance use disorder, with roughly a quarter reporting major depression.

## The bills in Harrisburg

None of the kratom bills introduced in recent years has reached the governor's desk, the Capital-Star reported. The measures with the most momentum would allow sales of natural leaf products to adults over 21 while banning synthetic kratom products and some high-potency extracts.

Rep. James Prokopiak, a Bucks County Democrat, is co-sponsoring multiple bills that would ban synthetic products, set labeling standards, and bar sales of natural products to anyone under 21, per the paper. Neither bill would criminalize possession or use; both put liability on sellers. They differ on enforcement, with one assigning testing and regulation to the Department of Health and the other creating a new board. "From what we've heard, the synthetic product is really the one that can be made much more potent than a naturally occurring kratom," Prokopiak told the Capital-Star. "There are no packaging requirements. There are no safety warnings. There are no labelling requirements. It's the wild west for kratom, really."

In the Republican-controlled Senate, Sen. Tracy Pennycuick of Montgomery County is sponsoring a bill that would ban synthetic kratom products and any kratom product with more than two percent 7-OH concentration, the paper reported. "As an adult, I don't care what you do," Pennycuick said. "But I do want to make sure that if you're buying a product, you know what you're buying."

## The AKA's position and plans

Lawmakers who spoke with the Capital-Star all said they had met with lobbyists from the American Kratom Association, which the paper described as having had a hand in crafting legislation in many states. The association reported $4.7 million in revenue in 2024, according to IRS filings cited by the paper, and Mac Haddow, its senior fellow on public policy, said that money comes primarily from the roughly 250 businesses he estimates manufacture and sell kratom products in the country.

Haddow said the association agrees with lawmakers that kratom needs regulation but strongly opposes an all-out ban, and that it has advocated in Pennsylvania for banning synthetic products and hyper-potent extracts while restricting sales to adults 21 and over. "Back in 2018 when we saw, from a consumer perspective, that there were companies out there creating products that were dangerous, we started promoting our Kratom Consumer Protection Act," he told the paper. Haddow, who said he has used kratom for 10 years, added that the association has worked in Pennsylvania for three years and intends to expand its effort: "We're going to step it up here now that we see there's a pathway. So you're going to see a lot of activity there."

## Prosecutors, the health department, and the federal backdrop

The Capital-Star's report follows the [August 31 letters from Bucks County District Attorney Joe Khan and his Chester County counterpart asking the Department of Health to schedule 7-OH and other kratom derivatives on an emergency basis][bucks-chester-das-urge-pennsylvania-emergency-7oh-scheduling], a step the paper noted would make possession a crime, unlike most of the pending bills. "We have an emergency in Bucks County," Khan told the paper, describing smoke shops and gas stations that he said are "luring children into stores and selling them all kinds of things," including kratom. Khan [obtained an emergency injunction last week against a Buckingham Township smoke shop][bucks-county-da-injunction-zaza-vape-kratom-minors] he said was selling kratom to children. Asked what lawmakers should do long term, Khan said he was "not in a position to really make recommendations" for adults 21 and over and that his immediate concern is minors.

The department spokesperson told the Capital-Star that DOH is "working to raise awareness and educate the general public," that retail availability "can make people assume they are regulated and safe when they are not," and that the department is doing additional outreach to health care providers while seeking feedback on the best course of action.

The paper placed the state debate against federal and out-of-state moves: the [DEA's emergency Schedule I placement of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] for two years, an open comment period on a proposed Schedule I classification for 7-OH, [North Dakota's new law banning synthetic kratom while regulating natural leaf][north-dakota-armstrong-signs-hb-1628-synthetic-kratom-ban-house-rejects-total-ban], and [Iowa's governor calling for a total ban][iowa-governor-reynolds-calls-total-statewide-kratom-ban].
