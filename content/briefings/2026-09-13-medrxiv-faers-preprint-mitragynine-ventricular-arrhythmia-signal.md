A preprint posted to medRxiv on September 10, 2026 reported that mitragynine, the primary alkaloid in kratom leaf, showed disproportionate reporting of ventricular arrhythmia and QTc prolongation in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), according to the [abstract published on the preprint server](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.09.08.26362577v1). The reports analyzed name mitragynine as a substance and do not indicate whether the product involved was natural leaf kratom, an extract, or a concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparation; the abstract does not mention 7-OH. The paper, titled "Cardiac Arrhythmia Associated with Psychoactive Drugs," has not been peer reviewed. medRxiv labels all posted manuscripts as preliminary reports that should not be used to guide clinical practice.

## Who wrote it and why

The listed authors are Mori J. Krantz, Mark C. Haigney, Mary Ross Southworth, Norman Stockbridge, and David P. Kao. According to the preprint's author information, Krantz is affiliated with the Division of Cardiology and Nephrology in the FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, Haigney with the Uniformed Services University, Southworth and Stockbridge with S & S Consulting Partners in Silver Spring, Maryland, and Kao with the University of Colorado School of Medicine. Kao and Krantz are listed as corresponding authors. The disclosure section lists consulting relationships for Stockbridge with a large number of pharmaceutical companies and states that Southworth's relationships overlap with his because both work for the same consulting firm.

The authors framed the analysis around a recent presidential executive order directing federal agencies to accelerate development of and access to psychedelic drugs, writing that studies of these substances have "focused disproportionately on efficacy over safety" and that "rigorous evaluation of cardiovascular safety, including potential arrhythmia liability, is essential." The abstract groups mitragynine with the psychedelics under study as a substance "available over-the-counter in some states and illegal in others."

## What the analysis found

The researchers analyzed FAERS reports from 2000 through 2024 that involved MDMA, mitragynine, ibogaine, LSD, mescaline, psilocybin, THC, and DMT, using FDA-approved drugs as reference points: dofetilide, an antiarrhythmic the authors used as a positive control, and naltrexone as a negative control. The measure was the proportional reporting ratio (PRR), which compares how often a given adverse event appears in reports mentioning a drug against how often it appears in all other reports. Among 18,499,626 unique cases, 61,961 mentioned at least one of the drugs of interest.

For mitragynine, the abstract reports:

- A PRR of 8.8 for the composite of ventricular arrhythmia and cardiac arrest, based on 84 of 1,171 mitragynine reports.
- A PRR of 4.4 for ventricular arrhythmia alone, based on eight of 1,171 reports, after excluding cardiac arrests that may not have been arrhythmic in origin.
- A PRR of 15.9 for QTc prolongation, based on 38 of 1,171 reports, which the authors described as comparable to dofetilide (PRR 30.9) and methadone (PRR 12.4).

The abstract describes mitragynine and ibogaine as producing the strongest ventricular arrhythmia signals in the set. Ibogaine's signals were markedly larger (a PRR of 142.0 for ventricular arrhythmia alone) but rested on 25 reports in total. MDA and LSD did not show a ventricular arrhythmia signal, and MDMA was associated with disproportionate reporting of QTc prolongation only. The authors concluded that "ibogaine and mitragynine demonstrated disproportionate reporting of ventricular arrhythmia similar to or exceeding established proarrhythmic drugs" and that the data "suggest that robust cardiac safety evaluations will be a regulatory priority."

## What the analysis does not establish

FAERS, which the FDA [now calls the Adverse Event Monitoring System](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/surveillance-post-drug-approval-activities/fda-adverse-event-monitoring-system-aems) and describes as a database of adverse event reports, medication error reports, and product quality complaints submitted to the agency, is a spontaneous reporting system. The preprint's own framing treats the results as signals for further evaluation rather than as evidence of causation, and the authors' stated conclusion is that cardiac safety evaluation should be a regulatory priority, not that mitragynine has been shown to cause arrhythmia. The abstract does not describe how the analysis handled reports listing mitragynine alongside other drugs, and the full methods were not reviewed for this briefing.

The analysis is of mitragynine as a reported substance, and, as noted above, the reports do not identify the product type. That leaf-versus-concentrate distinction sits at the center of the [federal scheduling proposal whose comment period closed September 10][hhs-7oh-threshold-comment-period-closes-30000-comments]. The signal reported here therefore attaches to the alkaloid as recorded in adverse event reports, not to any product category.

The preprint adds to a run of surveillance-data studies on kratom published this summer. A study in the journal Addiction reported in August that [U.S. poison center cases involving kratom rose from 19 in 2010 to 1,242 in 2023][addiction-study-poison-center-kratom-cases-surge], with the same limitation that the underlying data do not distinguish leaf from concentrated products. No trade or advocacy organization had responded to the medRxiv preprint in the coverage available at publication.
