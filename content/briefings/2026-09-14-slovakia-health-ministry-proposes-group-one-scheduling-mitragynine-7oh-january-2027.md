Slovakia's Ministry of Health has proposed adding mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) to the country's list of Group I psychotropic substances, the Slovak daily [Pravda reported](https://www.pravda.sk/uzitocna/ako-vybavit/clanok/2036949-volne-dostupne-latky-mozu-zabijat-slovensko-ich-chce-zakazat-na-zoznam-pribudnu-dalsie-nebezpecne-drogy) on September 12, 2026. The change would come through an amendment to Slovakia's law on narcotic and psychotropic substances, and the submitted material sets an effective date of January 1, 2027, according to the paper's account of the proposal. The listing names mitragynine, the main alkaloid in kratom leaf, alongside 7-OH; Pravda reported that the state wants to move both substances into a much stricter regime, and the paper's report does not spell out how the listing would apply to natural leaf products as sold.

## What the ministry's explanatory report says

The explanatory report describes 7-OH as a pharmacologically active alkaloid of Mitragyna speciosa and a metabolite of mitragynine, per Pravda. "The use of 7-hydroxymitragynine may be associated with a risk of dependence, the development of tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and intoxication," the ministry's material states, in the paper's quotation. The report attributes similar risks to mitragynine, which it calls kratom's main psychoactive alkaloid acting on the opioid system, according to Pravda.

Pravda reported that kratom products in capsule, powder, drink, and extract form are currently available to Slovak consumers both online and in brick-and-mortar shops, mostly as ground powder and capsules. The paper added its own caution that the explanatory report only sets out the intent and justification for scheduling the two substances; the concrete legal consequences for possession, sale, and other handling would follow from the text of the law and related regulations, which the paper did not have.

## Products enriched with 7-OH, and products relabeled as other Mitragyna species

The ministry's case leans on market monitoring by the Slovak Ministry of the Interior, as cited in the explanatory report. According to Pravda, that monitoring identified products "containing or enriched with 7-hydroxymitragynine" on the market, including soluble drinks, extracts, strips, tablets, and other consumer formats. Products enriched with 7-OH are concentrated or synthetic preparations, a category distinct from the trace 7-OH found in natural kratom leaf; the Pravda report does not give concentrations or say how many products were found.

The Interior Ministry also flagged increased interest among children and adolescents, per the report. Pravda quoted the explanatory report as saying that discussions about recommended dosing, signs of dependence, and combining the products with other substances to boost their effects had been identified on social networks and messaging forums, and that the products were turning up in schools, gyms, and clubs.

On mitragynine, the ministry described a pattern it says circumvents the existing legal framework: products declared as Mitragyna hirsuta or Mitragyna javanica, or sold as isolated alkaloids, according to Pravda. The paper's report does not say whether those relabeled products were tested for mitragynine content.

## Two nitazenes in the same amendment

The kratom alkaloids are one part of a broader list. Pravda reported that the same amendment would add N-desethyl etonitazene and N-pyrrolidino isotonitazene, also known as isotonitazepyne, to the Group I narcotic substances list. The explanatory report describes N-pyrrolidino isotonitazene as having "at least five times" the potency of fentanyl at the mu-opioid receptor and says it appeared on illicit markets in Australia and the United States in 2024, per the paper. For N-desethyl etonitazene, the ministry's document says available pharmacological data point to properties typical of strong opioids, with potency that "may be significantly higher than fentanyl," according to Pravda.

Pravda's report does not say whether the ministry treats the kratom alkaloids and the nitazenes as comparable in risk; they share an amendment, and the report presents them in sequence.

## How the Slovak line differs from the Czech and U.S. approaches

The Slovak proposal, as Pravda describes it, would place the two alkaloids themselves on a controlled list rather than setting a concentration threshold, and the paper's report does not address how that would apply to natural leaf products. Two days after the Pravda report, the neighboring Czech government was set to discuss a different model: [a package that would raise the purchase age to 21, ban online sales, and add an excise tax while keeping licensed sales in place][czech-government-weighs-kratom-age-21-online-sales-ban-excise-tax], as KNT reported on September 14.

In the United States, the federal scheduling effort has so far targeted concentrated and synthetic 7-OH rather than mitragynine or the plant. As KNT reported on September 12, the [Department of Health and Human Services' comment period on the proposed 7-OH scheduling threshold closed September 10][hhs-7oh-threshold-comment-period-closes-30000-comments] with more than 30,000 comments, and no DEA temporary order had appeared in the Federal Register as of that date. Pravda's report does not mention the U.S. process or any European Union-level action on kratom.
