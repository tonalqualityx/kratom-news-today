Two UMass Dartmouth nursing professors who spent part of this year visiting vape shops along New England's Route 1 corridor to study how kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products are marketed and sold have concluded that addressing kratom dependence will take more than Massachusetts' August 28 reclassification of the substance as a Schedule I drug, [The Herald News reported](https://www.heraldnews.com/story/news/2026/09/16/umass-dartmouth-researchers-say-mass-needs-more-than-kratom-ban/91692180007/) on September 16, 2026. Martha Whitfield and Kristopher Jackson have two papers from the project under review, according to the outlet, which did not name the journals.

"The floodgates here was opened. These were marketed as unregulated supplements sold in vape shops and convenience stores. And so now we've turned the spigot off," Jackson told The Herald News. The state's emergency order [placed all forms of kratom in Schedule I for one year][massachusetts-emergency-order-all-kratom-schedule-one], and local health departments have been using it to [clear products from retail shelves][massachusetts-kratom-ban-quiet-compliance-retailers-clear-shelves].

## What the mystery shopping found

Whitfield and Jackson entered vape shops across New England with a script, per The Herald News: a friend is struggling with opioid addiction and wants to know whether kratom or 7-OH could help. The responses varied. Some staff sold kratom without a second thought. Others expressed concern about using kratom for opioid addiction and recommended the fictional friend go to a methadone clinic instead. Many recommended kratom and 7-OH "in a measured way," offering dosing advice in a style Jackson compared to a physician's, according to the outlet.

"The idea of being like, 'Take two and call me in the morning,' it felt a little bit like that at times," Jackson said, per The Herald News.

The researchers described "a genuine willingness to try to help" customers with opioid addiction among retail staff, and Jackson said an appreciation that opioid addiction is a serious problem was "sort of a universal finding," according to the outlet. They asked whether public health initiatives could channel that goodwill by working with vape shop staff to share information about quitting drugs. The project also examined online kratom and 7-OH forums, which Whitfield said clinicians should perhaps be reading to understand what users are asking about and what needs they feel are unmet in conventional settings, per The Herald News.

## The treatment-access argument

The researchers argued that access to established addiction treatments such as buprenorphine and methadone must improve to keep people from turning to unregulated substances, and that cutting off the kratom supply without that access risks steering users toward more dangerous drugs such as fentanyl, The Herald News reported. Whitfield, who works at an addiction clinic in Fall River, said many patients do not disclose kratom use unless asked, and the outlet noted there is no official diagnosis for kratom addiction. "There's no great way to measure the number of users that existed. We know this product sold because it was on shelves across New England," Jackson said. The Herald News cited a Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration estimate of 1.7 million Americans who used a kratom product in 2021.

The outlet, citing the state public health department, described kratom as a plant that can mimic stimulant or opioid effects depending on the amount used, and described 7-OH as the plant's "active chemical" that can be isolated into stronger synthetic products. That framing compresses a distinction the research itself turns on: 7-OH occurs naturally in kratom leaf only in trace amounts, and the concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products sold in vape shops are a separate category from leaf-based kratom. The Herald News also stated, without attributing the claim to a named source, that kratom and 7-OH are addictive and can cause liver toxicity, seizures, and overdoses, especially when combined with other drugs, while noting there is little science on their health effects.

The Herald News placed the research against the DEA's July announcement of its intent to temporarily place concentrated 7-OH in Schedule I and Massachusetts' one-year emergency order. Fall River health director Tess Curran was still planning how to enforce that order as of an August 31 Board of Health meeting, the outlet reported.
