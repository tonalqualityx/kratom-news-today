Knox County, Tennessee, has recorded 18 deaths involving kratom so far in 2026, and in five of them kratom alkaloids were the sole cause of death, [WVLT reported](https://www.wvlt.tv/2026/09/15/banned-form-kratom-is-being-mixed-into-cats-claw-supplements-sold-shelves-gas-stations-across-tennessee-heres-what-we-know/) on September 15, 2026, citing the Knox County Regional Forensic Center. Twelve of the 18 were ruled drug-related deaths, per the station. Chris Thomas, the center's chief administrative officer and director, said the products involved are man-made synthetic compounds sold in mislabeled cat's claw supplements, and that natural kratom leaf is not what people are dying from, according to WVLT. The report does not name the specific synthetic compounds identified in the Knox County cases; the Tennessee Bureau of Investigation [said this week][tennessee-tbi-warns-cats-claw-tablets-contain-mgm-15-kratom-18-lab-submissions] that some cat's claw tablets submitted to its crime lab contained MGM-15, a synthetic kratom-derived compound in federal Schedule I.

## What the forensic center said

"What we're seeing is not the naturally occurring leaf plant. That's not what people are dying from. It is the alkaloids. It's the synthetics. It's the chemical compound concoctions that people are taking that are not safe," Thomas said, per WVLT. He compared the products to a Diet Coke can emptied and refilled with Mountain Dew and sold under the original label, the station reported.

Thomas said the deaths attributed solely to the products were a particular concern. "Knowing that we have had deaths solely attributed to what's being sold at a gas station legally is concerning for us," he said, according to WVLT. Kratom itself has been [illegal to possess or sell in Tennessee since July 1][tennessee-kansas-bans-take-effect-july-one]; cat's claw, a South American vine, remains legal, and the TBI has said the mislabeled products are turning up on convenience store shelves. WVLT's report does not say how many of the 18 deaths occurred before or after the ban took effect.

Mark Delaney, special agent in charge of the TBI Drug Investigation Division, told the station that people are buying the products directly off store shelves and that the deaths span all demographics. "There's no one age bracket. There's no one sex. It's hitting everything. It's coming from teenagers all the way to 70- and 80-year-old individuals," Delaney said, per WVLT. The forensic center and the TBI said people should not take a supplement without first speaking with a health care provider and knowing what it contains, the station reported.

The compounds the TBI has identified, including MGM-15, are among the three the Drug Enforcement Administration [placed in federal Schedule I on August 26][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds]. WVLT's report does not say whether any of the Knox County deaths involved 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, which are a separate category of concentrated preparation and are not covered by that federal order.

## Treatment centers report a surge

In a separate report on September 17, [NewsChannel 5 in Nashville](https://www.newschannel5.com/news/tennessees-kratom-ban-brings-wave-of-withdrawal-patients-to-treatment-centers) said facilities across Tennessee have reported a significant increase in patients seeking treatment for kratom withdrawal in the 77 days since the ban took effect. Dr. Chapman Sledge, quoted by the station alongside staff of the Cumberland Heights treatment center, said, "We're seeing more people seeking treatment from seven-hydroxy-mitragynine or Kratom addiction than we do fentanyl addiction these days," and put the ratio at four to one. Sledge said withdrawal symptoms can feel similar to those of other opioids but that people are more likely to survive them, per the station.

Sledge said he supports the state ban but that patients are already finding the substance under other names, including products marketed as cat's claw, NewsChannel 5 reported. Ben Taylor, a former patient who now works at Cumberland Heights, described withdrawal symptoms including sleeplessness and body aches, and Alumni Director Casey Hyatt said some patients had returned to use after treatment, per the station.

The NewsChannel 5 report describes kratom as "also known as 7-OH" and as a concentrated, often man-made substance. Natural kratom leaf and concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations are distinct products, and the station's account does not separate the admissions it describes between people who used leaf products and people who used concentrated 7-OH products. Reports of rising withdrawal admissions have surfaced in other states as well; a Pittsburgh hospital [described an uptick in kratom withdrawal cases][ahn-wexford-pittsburgh-hospital-uptick-kratom-withdrawal-icu] earlier this month.
