The Quincy City Council in central Washington voted 5-0 to schedule a public hearing on a proposal to regulate kratom sales within the city, the [Columbia Basin Herald](https://columbiabasinherald.com/news/2026/sep/18/quincy-schedules-hearing-on-kratom-ban/) reported on September 18, 2026. The resolution passed without discussion; council members Andrew Royer and Dylan Kling did not attend the meeting, according to the paper. The Herald's lede places the hearing at 6 p.m. on October 6, while a later reference in the same article calls it "the Oct. 8 hearing." KNT was unable to reconcile the two dates from the reporting available.

City Clerk Stephanie Boorman emphasized that the resolution only gathers public reaction to a possible ban and does not enact one, the Herald reported. "It is not regulating the sale or distribution of kratom products; it is merely outlining the concerns surrounding kratom products and moving forward with setting the public hearing," she said. "There is nothing specifying what type of a ban at this point." The details of any ban would be up to the council, according to the paper.

## The health district's pitch

The council's consideration follows a presentation by the Grant County Health District at its September 1 meeting, the Herald reported. Health district officials have urged cities throughout Grant County to enact kratom product bans within their own jurisdictions, according to the paper.

Reece Leavitt of the district's Healthy Communities and Families program told the council that kratom is a naturally occurring substance derived from a Southeast Asian plant. "Just because it's natural doesn't mean it's safe," he said, according to the Herald. Leavitt said the district's concern is with products containing high doses of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and its synthetic derivatives rather than with the leaf at low doses. "They take that chemical and they synthetically up the levels in these products," he said, adding that the most common example the district has seen in the county is a shot-style energy drink. Those concentrated and synthetic products are a category distinct from natural leaf, in which 7-OH is present only in trace amounts; the resolution the council adopted, as the Herald described it, does not say whether a Quincy ban would cover leaf products, concentrated products, or both.

Leavitt told the council that the products act on the same parts of the brain as opioids and that overdoses respond to Narcan, the Herald reported. He said there is little monitoring of product quality. "It's not tested at all," he said. "There's no regulation on this. Nobody's testing." Leavitt said there is almost no way to know the 7-OH content of any product on the market or what else it might contain, and that because the products are unregulated they can be purchased by customers of any age, including children, according to the paper.

## Grant County's cities have mostly acted already

Ephrata, Mattawa, Warden, Royal City, and George have all approved total bans, and Moses Lake has restricted sales by age, the Herald reported. Othello's council has also banned kratom, and Leavitt said Spokane and Spokane Valley have banned it as well. KNT reported in June that [Mattawa became the third Grant County city to ban kratom][mattawa-washington-bans-kratom-third-grant-county-city], treating violations as civil infractions.

The activity fits a pattern KNT has tracked since spring, in which [Washington local governments have moved on kratom][washington-local-governments-fill-kratom-regulatory-vacuum] in the absence of a statewide framework. On the other side of the state, the Bellingham City Council [voted 7-0 on September 14][bellingham-washington-city-council-approves-kratom-sales-ban-7-0-gross-misdemeanor] to adopt an emergency ordinance banning kratom sales as a gross misdemeanor. The Herald's report does not include comment from Quincy retailers or from kratom industry or advocacy organizations.
