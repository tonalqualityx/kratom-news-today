Kratom has been found in products sold as cat's claw across Virginia without appearing on the label, according to the Blue Ridge Poison Center, [Charlottesville station WVIR reported](https://www.29news.com/2026/09/18/unlabeled-kratom-found-cats-claw-products-across-virginia/) on September 18, 2026. Dr. Chris Holstege, the center's director, told the station the mislabeling came to light through patients. "There are symptoms associated with it that should not have occurred based on historically how it was used," Holstege said of cat's claw, a South American vine sold as an herbal supplement. "That's when it was found out and detected that there was indeed another plant that was in it. And that was kratom."

The report does not identify the products or brands involved, say how many cases the poison center has handled, or describe the symptoms in detail beyond the station's general references to breathing trouble, depression, and dependence risk at high doses. It also does not say what form the kratom took. Whether the products contained natural leaf material, concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), or a synthetic kratom-derived compound is not stated, and the distinction changes the risk profile considerably. When the [Tennessee Bureau of Investigation warned about cat's claw tablets on September 14][tennessee-tbi-warns-cats-claw-tablets-contain-mgm-15-kratom-18-lab-submissions], its crime lab reported that some of the 18 submissions since July contained MGM-15, a synthetic kratom-derived compound the DEA placed in federal Schedule I on August 26. Virginia's poison center has not published comparable lab findings, and WVIR's report attributes the detection to the center's clinical work rather than to a state laboratory.

## "We have to have quality control"

Holstege framed the issue as one of product integrity. "This is a repetitive problem that we're seeing and we have to have quality control," he told WVIR. "If I'm a consumer and buying one of these products, I should know that I'm getting cat's claw and not another plant." The station noted that neither cat's claw nor kratom products are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which it said makes such products harder to track.

The report adds Virginia to the short list of states where officials have publicly linked cat's claw labeling to kratom. Tennessee's warning is the other, and it came from a law enforcement agency in a state where kratom possession and sale have been illegal since July 1. Virginia's position is different: the state [adopted sales restrictions, with rules that took effect July 1, and did not ban kratom][virginia-kratom-restrictions-take-effect-july-one], so a cat's claw product containing undisclosed kratom in Virginia raises a labeling question first and a legality question second. WVIR's report does not mention any enforcement action by Virginia agencies.

Clinicians detecting kratom exposure through unexpected symptoms is also the theme of a [systematic review published this month in the Journal of Medical Toxicology][journal-medical-toxicology-systematic-review-kratom-pulmonary-toxicity-24-reports-five-categories], whose authors urged physicians to consider kratom and 7-OH when patients present with unexplained lung injury or opioid-type respiratory depression.
