A resting-state functional MRI study published online on September 18, 2026, in the [Journal of Psychoactive Drugs](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.2026.2729619) reported that regular kratom users showed reduced low-frequency brain activity in regions the authors associate with the default mode network, cognitive control, and self-referential processing, along with increased connectivity between salience and cerebellar networks. The study compared 22 regular kratom users with 21 healthy controls, for a total of 43 participants, according to the abstract. The authors describe the work as exploratory.

The paper's lead author is Suzana Mat Isa of Universiti Sains Malaysia, with co-authors Aini Ismafairus Abd Hamid, also of Universiti Sains Malaysia, Muhamad Zabidi Ahmad of KPJ Perlis Specialist Hospital, Muhammad Riddha Abdul Rahman of Universiti Tun Hussein Onn Malaysia, Mohammad Farris Iman Leong Abdullah of Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin, and Darshan Singh of Universiti Sains Malaysia, according to the journal's listing.

## What the scans showed

Imaging data were acquired on a 1.5T GE Signa HDxt scanner and analyzed with Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 and the CONN toolbox, the [abstract states](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42757738/). The researchers compared the two groups on low-frequency fluctuations, a measure of spontaneous activity in resting-state fMRI, and on functional connectivity between brain networks.

The authors reported that kratom users showed reduced low-frequency fluctuations in regions tied to the default mode network, cognitive control, and self-referential processing, including the precuneus, superior frontal gyrus, and cerebellar and cingulate cortices. The same group showed increased connectivity between salience and cerebellar networks, particularly in the posterior cerebellum, and in the left rostral prefrontal cortex, according to the abstract.

## How the authors interpret the findings

The authors wrote that chronic kratom consumption may alter brain activity in areas linked to the default mode network, cognition, and reward processing. They suggested the increased connectivity between salience and cerebellar networks "may reflect compensatory mechanisms or adaptations to kratom's effects," and called for further research into the underlying mechanisms and the implications for cognitive function and addictive behaviors.

The abstract does not report how long participants had used kratom, how much they consumed, or in what form. It does not describe the alkaloid content of the products involved, and it does not address concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, the category the Drug Enforcement Administration has proposed scheduling above a concentration threshold in a [federal action that remains pending][hhs-7oh-threshold-comment-period-closes-30000-comments]. The abstract does not state where participants were recruited; all six authors are based at Malaysian institutions. The design compares two groups at a single point in time, the authors describe the work as exploratory, and the abstract makes no causal claim.

## Where the study sits in recent kratom research

The paper adds to a run of kratom studies published this month. A [medRxiv preprint posted September 10, 2026][medrxiv-faers-preprint-mitragynine-ventricular-arrhythmia-signal] from FDA, Uniformed Services University, and University of Colorado cardiologists reported disproportionate ventricular arrhythmia and QTc-prolongation reporting for mitragynine in FDA adverse event data. An [observational study in Frontiers in Pharmacology reported earlier in September][frontiers-observational-study-mitragynine-20mg-focus-energy] described participant-reported focus and energy improvements at a roughly 20 mg mitragynine dose and more side effects at higher doses. And a [systematic review in the Journal of Medical Toxicology][journal-medical-toxicology-systematic-review-kratom-pulmonary-toxicity-24-reports-five-categories] sorted 24 reports of kratom-associated lung injury into five provisional categories. None of those papers used neuroimaging, and the Journal of Psychoactive Drugs study does not cite them.
