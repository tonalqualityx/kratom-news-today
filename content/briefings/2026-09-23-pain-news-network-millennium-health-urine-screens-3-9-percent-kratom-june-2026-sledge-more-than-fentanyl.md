[Pain News Network reported](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2026/9/22/kratom-and-7-oh-bans-create-new-patients-for-addiction-treatment) on September 22, 2026 that Millennium Health, a drug testing company that works with addiction treatment centers nationwide, found 3.9% of its urine drug screens tested positive for kratom alkaloids in June 2026, up from 3% in 2025. The outlet's Pat Anson described the increase as marginal and set it against positivity rates of 13% for fentanyl, 11% for stimulants, and about 2% for prescription opioids in the same screens. The report ties the numbers to a wave of state and local bans on kratom and on concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, and it follows [STAT's September 21 report on addiction clinics seeing more kratom and 7-OH withdrawal cases][stat-addiction-clinics-rising-kratom-7oh-withdrawal-boulder-care-14-percent-new-patients], which described those products as containing 7-OH at concentrations far above the trace amounts in natural kratom leaf.

Eric Dawson, a pharmacist and vice president of clinical affairs at Millennium Health, told the outlet he does not know whether more clinicians are ordering kratom tests, but that positivity rates run higher among the patients who are tested for it. The report does not give the number of screens behind the percentages or say whether the 2025 figure is a full-year average.

## Treatment providers describe different pictures

"We're seeing more people seeking treatment from 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) or kratom addiction than we do fentanyl addiction these days," Dr. Chapman Sledge, who runs an addiction treatment center in Nashville, told Pain News Network. Sledge made a similar point in [Knox County, Tennessee coverage earlier this month][knox-county-tennessee-18-kratom-involved-deaths-2026-five-sole-cause-synthetics-treatment-surge], where he described kratom-related admissions outnumbering fentanyl admissions four to one.

Dr. Robert Levy, an addiction specialist in Minneapolis and past president of the Minnesota Society of Addiction Medicine, gave the outlet a more measured account. He said his metro area sees some kratom and 7-OH use disorder but that fentanyl still accounts for most cases, with more kratom showing up in rural areas, per the report. Levy said polysubstance use disorder remains the most common intake, that patients often do not mention kratom when listing the substances they use, and that he records such patients under opioid use disorder because "kratom use disorder isn't a diagnosis," according to Pain News Network.

Dr. Andrew Kolodny, president of Physicians for Responsible Opioid Prescribing, told the outlet that a growing number of addiction specialists are treating patients who have been using kratom with buprenorphine. He said not everyone addicted to kratom will need that medication. A substantial subset with severe opioid use disorder, he said, is ending up on the same treatment used for other opioids, per the report. Kolodny also said there is no good surveillance data yet on addiction involving kratom, and that he believes it is rising as consumption rises.

Pain News Network also reported that nearly one-third of new patients prescribed Suboxone by a telehealth addiction clinic were using kratom or 7-OH. Boulder Care's head of medical affairs, Dr. Ayesha Appa, called that figure "quite an explosion" and said clinics are seeing what happens when people lose access to a product and seek care in large numbers, per the outlet. Appa made the same case to STAT, which reported that most of Boulder Care's kratom-reporting patients had been using highly concentrated 7-OH products rather than leaf kratom.

## Bans, illicit products, and the pending federal action

The report says illicit products appeared after Maryland banned 7-OH in July and after Connecticut banned kratom and 7-OH products in March. According to Pain News Network, Maryland health officials found three illicit 7-OH products, two of which were laced with fentanyl and methamphetamine, and Connecticut authorities arrested smoke shop employees for selling cannabis gummies containing 7-OH and other kratom alkaloids. That Connecticut case matches [the Wallingford raid covered here on September 17][wallingford-connecticut-puffcity-raid-two-arrested-7oh-gummy-sold-as-cannabis], in which police said a gummy sold as cannabis contained 7-OH and 7-hydroxymitragynine pseudoindoxyl. The report closes on the observation that regulating a substance, as Maryland did with 7-OH sales, typically disrupts the unregulated drug market.

Anson wrote that he expects reports of withdrawal and treatment-seeking to increase once the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) places 7-OH products in Schedule I, which he said could happen any day. The DEA's proposal to schedule 7-OH above a concentration threshold has not been finalized; the agency's August order [placed three synthetic compounds in Schedule I][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] and did not reach 7-OH or natural leaf kratom. The Pain News Network report treats bans on leaf kratom and bans on concentrated 7-OH as one trend. Kratom advocacy organizations have not treated them that way: the American Kratom Association's Mac Haddow [told the Pennsylvania Capital-Star this month][pennsylvania-health-department-reviewing-7oh-request-lawmakers-push-regulation] that the group supports regulation and opposes an outright ban on leaf kratom. The report does not quote the association or any kratom trade group.

The data in the report come from one testing company's client base and from individual clinicians' observations, and Levy and Kolodny both told the outlet that measurement is complicated by the absence of a diagnosis code and of surveillance data.
