Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes sued Botanic Tonics LLC on September 23, 2026, alleging that the maker of the kratom-and-kava tonic "feel free" violated the Arizona Consumer Fraud Act by promoting its two-ounce shots as energy drinks, obscuring the product's kratom content, and failing to adequately disclose risks of addiction and withdrawal, [according to the attorney general's office](https://www.azag.gov/press-release/attorney-general-mayes-sues-kratom-maker-botanic-tonics-first-nation-consumer). The office's release described the complaint, filed in Maricopa County Superior Court, as a first-in-nation consumer protection lawsuit against a kratom maker.

The release and the coverage reviewed by KNT describe feel free as a product containing kratom and kava. None of them characterize it as a concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparation, and none report a 7-OH concentration for the product. The complaint's stated theory concerns marketing and disclosure rather than the alkaloid content of the shot.

Mayes said in the release that Botanic Tonics has repeatedly and willfully violated the Consumer Fraud Act and endangered the health and safety of Arizonans, including minors, by improperly marketing a kratom-containing beverage. She said the product's marketing hides what she called a real danger of addiction and other mental and physical harm.

## What the complaint alleges

The attorney general's office said Botanic Tonics has sold feel free throughout Arizona at gas stations, convenience stores, other mainstream retail locations, and online, while marketing it as a wellness tonic, energy product, kava drink, and alternative to alcohol. The release said the company has sold millions of kratom shots in Arizona since 2020 through what it called a youth-focused advertising campaign that has included the use of colleges and college students.

According to the release, a review of the company's practices allegedly shows feel free was designed to appear innocuous, and the office listed four categories of conduct: obscuring the product's kratom content and failing to disclose risks of addiction, dependence, and withdrawal; marketing it as a benign wellness and energy product inconsistent with the risks the company knew it presented; promoting it as an alternative to alcohol, including to consumers seeking sobriety, without disclosing that it substituted one substance carrying dependence risk for another; and using sales practices designed to encourage repeated purchases, including recurring subscriptions, subscription discounts, and a rewards program that increased benefits as consumers spent more.

[The Arizona Capitol Times reported](https://azcapitoltimes.com/news/2026/09/23/arizona-ag-sues-over-kratom-kava-in-feel-free-drink/) that the beverage is marketed as a "kava drink" made with kava and "other ancient plants," and that kratom is not mentioned as an ingredient. "Describing 'feel free' as a kava drink is akin to describing a gin and tonic simply as tonic," the complaint states, according to the Capitol Times. The outlet also reported that the complaint takes issue with packaging it says is designed to resemble caffeine-based products such as 5-Hour Energy, and that Mayes alleged company owner Jerry Ross knew some consumers experienced problems with the product and at one point early on put his personal telephone number on packages.

[Arizona's Family reported](https://www.azfamily.com/2026/09/23/arizona-sues-popular-feel-free-kratom-drink-maker-alleging-it-hid-health-risks/) that the drink's packaging claims to provide a "clean energy boost" and "a mood lift," and states that the product "has not been shown to cause any serious physical harm." The station reported that the complaint alleges the defendants failed to adequately warn Arizona consumers even as they became aware that consumers were reporting addiction, withdrawal, and other serious adverse effects.

## What the state is asking for

The lawsuit does not ask the court to take feel free off the market, the Capitol Times reported. Mayes instead wants Maricopa County Superior Court Judge Adele Ponce to order Botanic Tonics to stop the acts the complaint describes as deceptive, misleading, or unfair, including omitting the two ingredients, when the product is sold in Arizona, and she is seeking financial compensation for Arizona purchasers who suffered side effects, according to the outlet.

Mayes said in the release that the office will not tolerate companies selling these products without safeguards to ensure consumers understand the risks, and that the state is taking Botanic Tonics to court to stop the practices and hold the company accountable under state law. The office said it will set up a forum for consumers to share their experiences with feel free and other kratom products.

Richie Taylor, a spokesperson for the attorney general's office, told the Capitol Times that the state Department of Health Services has counted 146 kratom-related deaths between 2020 and 2023, a figure the release also cites. Taylor called it a public health threat, the outlet reported. Neither the release nor the coverage says what products or alkaloid concentrations were involved in those deaths, or whether they involved concentrated 7-OH products, natural leaf kratom, or other substances.

The company did not immediately respond on Wednesday, the Capitol Times reported, and Arizona's Family said it had reached out to Botanic Tonics for comment. None of the coverage reviewed included a response from kratom trade or advocacy organizations.

## The legal theory in a state without a kratom statute

The Capitol Times reported that Arizona lawmakers have rejected efforts to impose tighter restrictions on kratom and that there are no restrictions on kava. Mayes is suing under the Consumer Fraud Act, and the outlet reported her position is that companies still cannot sell products containing these ingredients without disclosing that to customers. As KNT reported in May 2026, the Arizona Senate [passed HB 2415][arizona-kratom-bill-delaware-weighs-ban], a bill that would raise the purchase age to 21 and classify high-concentration 7-OH products as narcotic drugs. The Capitol Times report on the lawsuit does not name that bill or describe its status.

[Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/arizona-sues-kratom-drink-maker-false-advertising-2026-09-23/) the suit as a false-advertising case and noted that kratom remains federally legal while federal authorities have separately moved against higher-concentration products. The approach differs from the enforcement track in Colorado, where [Attorney General Phil Weiser announced a $152,000 settlement on September 21][colorado-attorney-general-weiser-152000-settlement-dnb-distro-million-smoke-n-vape-7oh-limit-labeling] with a smoke shop chain under a state statute that caps 7-OH at 2% of total alkaloids. Arizona's complaint, as described in the coverage, rests on disclosure and marketing law rather than a potency limit.

## Botanic Tonics' litigation record

Arizona's Family reported that Botanic Tonics settled a class action lawsuit last year for $8.75 million, and that the settlement required the company to add a warning that kratom "can be habit-forming and harmful if not consumed as directed" and should not be used "if you have a history of substance abuse." That suit claimed the company failed to warn consumers about the drink's addictive properties, the station reported.

The company has appeared in KNT's coverage several times this year. The federal government [dismissed its enforcement case against Botanic Tonics in June 2026][botanic-tonics-case-dismissed-new-york-alabama-kratom], and the parents of a 32-year-old Florida man [filed a wrongful-death suit over feel free the same month][idaho-falls-kratom-ban-botanic-tonics-wrongful-death]. In May, The Daily Beast reported that Department of Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin [holds between $500,000 and $1 million in Botanic Tonics shares][dhs-secretary-mullin-holds-stake-in-kratom-company]. The Arizona release does not mention any of those matters.
