The American Kratom Association announced a virtual press conference for 9:30 a.m. Eastern on Friday, September 25, 2026, titled "Reporting Before the Results: A Response to Media Coverage of the Ole Miss Deaths," [according to a release the association issued](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/american-kratom-association-hold-live-021300406.html) on September 24. The association said news reports had attributed the deaths of two University of Mississippi students to kratom before autopsy and toxicology results were complete, and that the briefing would address the difference between confirmed causation and the presence of a packaged product at a scene.

The announcement arrived as officials in two states issued their own statements about the deaths, and the statements do not agree on what is known. South Carolina Attorney General Alan Wilson described the deaths as overdoses in a public safety advisory, while the Mississippi investigators handling the cases have said they have no confirmed information that kratom or any other substance contributed to either death. None of the releases or reports reviewed by KNT specifies whether the packaged kratom found in Oxford was natural leaf or a concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparation, and the Mississippi State Department of Health's warning this week concerns concentrated 7-OH products specifically.

## What the association says it will argue

The AKA's release quoted the Lafayette County Metro Narcotics statement that investigators "do not have confirmed information indicating that kratom or any other substance was a contributing factor in either death," and said there was no confirmed evidence the two deaths are connected, that the cases are being investigated separately, and that autopsy and toxicology results had not been released. [KNT reported the Metro Narcotics statement and the initial coverage on September 23][two-university-of-mississippi-students-dead-oxford-packaged-kratom-found-metro-narcotics-no-confirmed-link].

According to the release, the briefing will cover the distinction between natural kratom leaf and what the association called concentrated or chemically manipulated substances: 7-OH, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16. The association characterized those as substances the Drug Enforcement Administration moved to schedule on July 1. [The DEA announced its intent to temporarily place high-concentration 7-OH products in Schedule I on that date][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] and [finalized emergency scheduling of the three synthetic compounds in late August][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds]. The release also cited guidance from NMS Labs that routine toxicology panels may not detect novel substances, urged what it called comprehensive quantitative toxicology, and referred to unverified reports of alternative synthetic opioids circulating in the university community. The release did not name the speakers beyond "the AKA and experts," and listed Mac Haddow, the association's senior fellow on public policy, as the press contact.

The press conference builds on [a condolence statement the association issued September 23](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/american-kratom-association-expresses-condolences-172000148.html). "The responsible course at this point is precisely what law enforcement and the university have requested: allow the investigations and toxicology testing to determine the facts before conclusions are drawn about what substances, if any, contributed to these deaths," Haddow said in that release. He said the federal scheduling actions make it more important than ever that investigators identify exactly what substances were present rather than applying the generic term kratom, and urged investigators to test for mitragynine, 7-OH, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, MGM-16, and other novel synthetic opioids or illicit substances. If a dangerous substance contributed to the deaths, he said, it is critically important that it be accurately identified.

The association's position tracks the stance it has taken since the summer: it has supported federal action against concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products while opposing bans on natural leaf kratom. Haddow told the Associated Press, in a story [carried by Hawaii News Now](https://www.hawaiinewsnow.com/2026/09/25/what-is-kratom-deaths-university-mississippi-students-cast-spotlight-substance) on September 25, that regulators confuse natural kratom with synthetic versions, and that many people have told lawmakers kratom has improved their lives. The AP identified the two students as Aidan Hamilton, a freshman from Colorado, and Robert Strang, a junior from Georgia, and reported that toxicology results were pending with no confirmed link between kratom and the deaths.

## South Carolina's attorney general calls the deaths overdoses

Wilson's office issued [a public safety advisory](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/944956275/public-safety-advisory-attorney-general-alan-wilson-warns-public-after-multiple-overdose-deaths-on-college-campus) on September 24 stating that two University of Mississippi students died from overdoses and that kratom was found with both. The advisory said messages among students had mentioned cychlorphine, a synthetic opioid, though law enforcement had not confirmed its presence. That characterization goes further than the Mississippi investigators' own statements, which have described the products as an investigative finding and stopped short of calling the deaths overdoses.

The advisory said kratom is widely legal, is available in convenience stores in South Carolina, and can be bought there by anyone 21 or older. It said that while kratom is regulated in the state, the products can be laced with synthetic opioids such as fentanyl and cychlorphine without the user's knowledge, and it described those synthetic opioids as often manufactured overseas and designed to be more potent and harder to detect than older drugs. Wilson described the drugs as poison and said a single exposure can be fatal, according to the advisory. The office urged people to avoid unknown or illicit substances, asked parents to talk with their children, and asked the public to report suspicious activity to law enforcement.

[Fox Carolina reported](https://www.foxcarolina.com/2026/09/25/south-carolina-ag-urges-drug-awareness-after-suspected-overdoses-ole-miss/) on September 25 that Wilson cautioned that unregulated kratom supplies "can be contaminated or laced with lethal substances like fentanyl," and that officials had not confirmed the cause of either death or whether the two cases are connected. The station's account did not mention cychlorphine. The advisory does not say whether the kratom found in Oxford was a natural leaf product or a concentrated 7-OH preparation, and it does not report any test result on those products.

## Mississippi's health department warns about concentrated 7-OH

The Mississippi State Department of Health posted [a notice titled "Beware of Counterfeit and Unregulated Drugs"](https://msdh.ms.gov/page/23,31118,341.html) on September 23. The notice relays Food and Drug Administration warnings about concentrated 7-OH products sold as tablets, gummies, drink mixes, and shots through gas stations, smoke shops, and corner stores, and states that 7-OH is not approved for medical use and cannot lawfully be marketed as a dietary supplement or added to conventional foods, according to the department. The AKA's September 23 release described the DEA's temporary scheduling action as targeting concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products while excluding botanical kratom with naturally occurring 7-OH below specified thresholds, and the department's warning is directed at that concentrated category rather than at leaf products.

The department also noted the Department of Justice's emergency scheduling of three manufactured compounds, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, which it said the department described as a response to a rapidly developing market for manufactured opioid compounds. [Tennessee's Bureau of Investigation warned last week that MGM-15 had turned up in tablets sold as cat's claw][tennessee-tbi-warns-cats-claw-tablets-contain-mgm-15-kratom-18-lab-submissions]. The Mississippi notice pairs the kratom-related warnings with a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention caution about counterfeit prescription pills that may contain fentanyl.

"The most important message for Mississippians is that you cannot determine what is in an unregulated product simply by looking at it," Jon Hubanks, director of the department's Center for Injury Prevention and Recovery, said in the notice. The department did not connect the notice to the Oxford investigations by name, and it does not say what products were found there.

## Where the accounts diverge

Three official or organizational voices have now spoken to the same two deaths, and they describe them differently. The AKA and the Mississippi investigators both say cause has not been established, with the association adding that a product labeled kratom may not contain natural leaf. Wilson's office calls the deaths overdoses and raises the possibility of adulteration with fentanyl or cychlorphine, a claim its own advisory notes law enforcement has not confirmed. The Mississippi health department warns about concentrated 7-OH products without tying them to the cases. What would settle the question, toxicology and product testing, had not been released by any of them as of September 25.
