Georgia's House Blue-Ribbon Study Committee on Youth Exposure to Kratom and Retail-Available Substances held a hearing on the University of Georgia campus in Athens on September 25, 2026, [WSB-TV reported](https://www.wsbtv.com/news/local/lawmakers-hold-hearing-dangers-some-kratom-products/RUK6ODOLKRG5LOQERH6OMXCCCY/). Chairman Rep. Rick Townsend, a Brunswick Republican, told the station that Georgia already has restrictions on potent synthetic kratom products and that the open question is who pays to enforce them.

"There's restrictions now. It's all about enforcement. Really, it has to be set up where the Department of Agriculture and the Department of Revenue take over enforcement. And that's going to take money," Townsend said, according to WSB-TV.

WSB-TV reported that the committee's focus was on potent synthetic forms of kratom and on pills sold at gas stations, and that natural kratom came up as a standardization question. The station's account does not use the term 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), and it does not specify the alkaloid content of any product discussed, so the report does not establish whether the products at issue were concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations, a category distinct from natural leaf kratom, which contains 7-OH only in trace amounts.

## The restrictions Townsend was referring to

WSB-TV reported that Townsend spearheaded passage of new laws restricting the sale of potent synthetic forms of kratom. The station does not name the legislation. Georgia's HB 181, which took effect July 1, 2026, [added 7-OH to the state's kratom definition, restricted sales to adults 21 and older, and required behind-the-counter placement][georgia-kratom-law-connecticut-schedule-one], as KNT reported in June. An August investigation by The Current [reported that products in Georgia's legal market were measuring far above the state's one-milligram-per-serving 7-OH limit][georgia-kratom-investigation-7oh-limits-testing-gap], a gap that the investigation attributed in part to the absence of state testing.

Townsend's proposal, as reported by WSB-TV, would route enforcement through the Department of Agriculture and the Department of Revenue. The station's report does not connect the proposal to The Current's findings, and it does not say whether the committee has drafted legislation to that effect or when it might report its recommendations.

## The witness testimony

Nick Morgan, whom WSB-TV described as a man who said he became addicted to synthetic kratom, told the committee he had been buying kratom, mostly in pill form, at a gas station, spending around $50 a day for about three weeks before trying to stop. Morgan said the withdrawal lasted three days and felt severe enough that he initially thought he was having a heart attack, and that it sent him to the hospital, according to the station. "To me it's a poison. I almost died," Morgan said, per WSB-TV, adding that he wanted people to know how dangerous he considers the products.

WSB-TV's report does not identify the brand or the labeled contents of the pills Morgan used, and it does not say whether they were concentrated 7-OH products or another synthetic preparation. The station reported his account as his own.

## The Ole Miss deaths

The hearing took place three days after [two University of Mississippi students were found dead in separate incidents in Oxford][two-university-of-mississippi-students-dead-oxford-packaged-kratom-found-metro-narcotics-no-confirmed-link], with packaged retail kratom found at both scenes and Lafayette County Metro Narcotics saying it had no confirmed information that kratom contributed to either death, as KNT reported. WSB-TV identified one of the students as Robert Strang, a 20-year-old junior who grew up in Atlanta, and reported that investigators in Oxford said toxicology results could take several weeks. Neither WSB-TV's report nor the earlier Mississippi coverage specifies whether the packaged kratom found at the scenes was a concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparation or natural leaf kratom.

"It's tragic that a young person should die like that. It's like Russian roulette," Townsend said, according to the station. Two UGA students interviewed by WSB-TV, Lena Almer and Nick Polumeyz, described the deaths as upsetting and said the products worried them. The station's report does not establish what the Mississippi students consumed or whether any kratom product was a cause of death, and the toxicology results it references were not available at the time of the hearing.

## The committee's track record

The Athens hearing is at least the third public session for the committee, which [met in Brunswick on August 21, 2026][georgia-brunswick-kratom-hearing-west-chester-ordinance], where presenters split between banning kratom outright and moving unadulterated products into healthcare channels, WSAV reported at the time. That meeting came one day after the American Kratom Association [filed a formal complaint with House Speaker Jon Burns][aka-formal-complaint-georgia-kratom-study-committee] alleging bias in how Townsend was administering the committee, according to a report carried by Yahoo Finance. WSB-TV's report on the Athens hearing does not mention the complaint or include testimony from industry or advocacy representatives.

Local governments in the state have started moving on their own. [Fulton County commissioners backed a resolution on September 17, 2026][fulton-county-georgia-commissioners-back-resolution-synthetic-kratom-ban-natural-kratom-behind-pharmacy-counters] calling for a state ban on synthetic kratom and for natural kratom products to be kept behind pharmacy counters, the Sandy Springs Record reported, a request that the General Assembly would next be able to act on in its 2027 session. The study committee's recommendations, whenever they come, would feed the same session.
