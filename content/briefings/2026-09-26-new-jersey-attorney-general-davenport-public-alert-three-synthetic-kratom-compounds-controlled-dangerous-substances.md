New Jersey Attorney General Jennifer Davenport and the state's Division of Consumer Affairs issued a public alert on September 25, 2026, stating that three synthetic kratom-related compounds, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, are being listed as controlled dangerous substances in New Jersey, [according to the attorney general's office](https://www.njoag.gov/ag-davenport-division-of-consumer-affairs-issue-public-alert-three-types-of-synthetic-kratom-to-be-listed-as-controlled-dangerous-substances-in-new-jersey/). "Any individual or business found violating this ban could face criminal charges," Davenport said in the release.

The three compounds are synthetic derivatives related to 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), and they are distinct from natural kratom leaf, which contains 7-OH only in trace amounts. The office's alert draws that line explicitly: natural kratom and synthetic kratom-related compounds that have not been scheduled remain legal in New Jersey, according to the release.

## How the state listing works

The state action is a consequence of federal scheduling rather than a separate New Jersey rulemaking. Under New Jersey law, when the federal government schedules a drug and the director of the Division of Consumer Affairs does not object, the drug automatically becomes scheduled in the state, the attorney general's office said. The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) temporarily classified mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 as Schedule I substances in August 2026, and the federal order remains in effect until at least August 26, 2028, unless it is modified, according to the release.

That federal order is the one the [Justice Department announced on August 25 and published in the Federal Register on August 26][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds], covering what the department described as 7-OH-related substances manufactured and concentrated into opioid products. The DEA's separate proposal to temporarily schedule concentrated 7-OH products above specified thresholds, announced in July, [had not been published as a final temporary order as of KNT's last check][dea-7oh-temporary-scheduling-order-still-unpublished], and the New Jersey alert does not address it.

[KYW Newsradio reported](https://www.audacy.com/kywnewsradio/news/local/some-synthetic-kratom-products-schedule-1-drug-nj) on September 25 that the three compounds join LSD and heroin on the state's Schedule I list, and that the temporary ban runs for roughly two years. The station's report does not describe any prior New Jersey enforcement actions or pending state legislation on kratom.

## What the officials said about kratom products generally

Christopher Peterson, the division's acting director, used the release to caution consumers about the wider category. "Consumers should utilize utmost caution with all kratom and related products," Peterson said, according to the attorney general's office.

Davenport said kratom products lack "quality control standards regarding contaminants or concentration" and often resemble candy with unclear labeling, which makes it difficult for consumers to understand what they are buying, KYW reported. The station described kratom as an herb that at high doses can produce opioid-like effects, and noted that kratom products are not approved for medical use in the United States. Those characterizations are the officials' and the station's; the alert itself does not change the legal status of natural kratom in New Jersey.

## Where the industry's advocacy groups stand

The federal order that New Jersey is now enforcing has had support from the two largest kratom advocacy organizations, with a caveat from one of them. The Global Kratom Coalition applauded the DEA's scheduling of the three synthetic compounds when it was announced, [as KNT reported in August][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds]. The American Kratom Association also publicly welcomed the order and then, one day later, [filed a federal lawsuit][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order] warning that scheduling mitragynine pseudoindoxyl could sweep up natural kratom products unless the order is clarified to exclude them, Bloomberg Law reported at the time. The association's position in both venues, as KNT summarized it then, is support for scheduling the synthetic compounds paired with an effort to keep the order away from natural leaf.

The New Jersey release does not describe how the state will test products for the three compounds. That question has come up elsewhere: the Tennessee Bureau of Investigation [warned this month that tablets sold as "cat's claw" contained MGM-15][tennessee-tbi-warns-cats-claw-tablets-contain-mgm-15-kratom-18-lab-submissions], based on laboratory submissions, and the American Kratom Association's lawsuit argued there is no reliable field method for measuring pseudoindoxyl levels. Neither the attorney general's release nor KYW's report addresses testing or laboratory capacity in New Jersey.
