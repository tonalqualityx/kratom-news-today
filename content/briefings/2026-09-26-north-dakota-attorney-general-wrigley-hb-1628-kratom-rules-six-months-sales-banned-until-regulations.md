North Dakota Attorney General Drew Wrigley said his office expects the process of writing regulations under the state's new kratom law to take approximately six months, and that all kratom sales remain banned statewide until those regulations are implemented, [KFGO reported](https://kfgo.com/2026/09/25/writing-kratom-regulations-for-new-law-is-expected-to-take-the-nd-ags-office-six-months/) on September 25, 2026. "I'm not going to drag my feet about it. We take the law and the law is the law," Wrigley said, according to the station.

The law in question is House Bill 1628, which [Governor Kelly Armstrong signed on September 4][north-dakota-armstrong-signs-hb-1628-synthetic-kratom-ban-house-rejects-total-ban] after a special legislative session, as KNT reported at the time. KFGO's September 25 report does not cite the bill number. It describes the law as imposing strict testing and labeling requirements on kratom products, requiring retailers to be licensed, banning synthetic kratom, and prohibiting sales to anyone under 21. The synthetic products the law bans are concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) derivatives, a category distinct from natural leaf kratom, which contains 7-OH only in trace amounts and which the law would allow for adults once the rules exist.

## What the six months covers

Under the framework as KNT reported it earlier this month, the law places synthetic and semi-synthetic kratom derivatives, including concentrated 7-OH, MGM-15, MGM-16, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, into Schedule I under state law, while allowing natural leaf kratom for adults 21 and older under licensing, laboratory testing, and labeling rules with a 0.05 percent 7-OH dry-weight limit.

The attorney general's office is responsible for writing the rules that make the natural-leaf side of that framework operational. Until it does, Wrigley said, the statewide ban on kratom sales continues, KFGO reported. Wrigley made the same point when the bill was signed, telling reporters then that kratom remained banned in all forms until rulemaking was complete, and lawmakers at the time estimated the process could run until March 2027, [as KNT reported][north-dakota-armstrong-signs-hb-1628-synthetic-kratom-ban-house-rejects-total-ban]. The six-month estimate Wrigley gave KFGO on September 25 points to roughly the same window.

## Wrigley's caveat on compliance

Wrigley also raised the possibility that the regulated market the law contemplates may not materialize. "It remains to be seen whether anybody in the kratom industry can produce a product that can comply with those regulations," he told KFGO. The station's report does not elaborate on which requirements he had in mind, and it does not include a response from industry operators or advocacy organizations.

## How North Dakota got here

The state has been without legal kratom sales since August 5, when [Armstrong's executive order declaring a public health emergency][north-dakota-executive-order-bans-all-kratom] took effect and prohibited the sale, use, and possession of all kratom products, natural leaf included, as KNT reported. The legislature then took up competing approaches in special session. [The Senate passed Senate Bill 2408, a permanent ban on all kratom, by 46-1 on September 3, while the House passed HB 1628 by 86-6 the same day][north-dakota-senate-passes-full-kratom-ban-house-passes-regulation-bill], as KNT reported at the time. The House then rejected the Senate's total ban 46-47, and Armstrong signed HB 1628, according to KNT's earlier coverage.

The result, based on Wrigley's comments to KFGO and the legislative record KNT reported earlier this month, is a state that has chosen a regulated natural-leaf market over a permanent ban on paper, while sales remain prohibited in practice until the attorney general's office finishes writing the rules. KFGO's report gives no date for when a draft of those rules might be published for comment.
