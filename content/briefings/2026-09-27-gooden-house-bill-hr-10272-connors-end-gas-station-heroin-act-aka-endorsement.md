Rep. Lance Gooden, a Republican from Texas, has introduced the House version of the End Gas Station Heroin Act, a bill aimed at synthetic opioids sold in products marketed as kratom, [the Washington Reporter reported](https://washingtonreporter.news/exclusive-lawmakers-introduce-legislation-to-crack-down-on-synthetic-gas-station-heroin/) on September 24, 2026. The bill would permanently schedule synthetic opioids and their analogs found in synthetic kratom products, target the manufacturers and distributors that introduce them, and create a specialized federal enforcement mechanism for new and emerging substances, according to the outlet's account of the measure.

The products at issue are concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations and related lab-made compounds, which the bill's backers distinguish from natural kratom leaf, where 7-OH occurs only in trace amounts. The Washington Reporter wrote that the legislation draws that line, treating synthetic kratom products as addictive and potentially fatal while leaving naturally occurring kratom outside its target.

"Dangerous synthetic opioids powerful enough to fuel addiction and destroy lives should not be sitting on convenience store shelves next to snacks and energy drinks," Gooden said, according to the Washington Reporter. "My bill permanently schedules these drugs, closes loopholes, and targets the entities putting them into our communities."

## Sponsors in both chambers

Senator Bernie Moreno, an Ohio Republican, is the Senate sponsor, and the House cosponsors are Reps. Mariannette Miller-Meeks of Iowa, Rich McCormick of Georgia, Barry Moore of Alabama, and Randy Fine of Florida, all Republicans, the Washington Reporter reported. The outlet did not publish a bill number. The American Kratom Association's September 26 release identified the two measures as S. 5383 and H.R. 10272.

Moreno's Senate bill is the one he [introduced on August 8][moreno-bill-permanent-scheduling-synthetic-7oh], which would amend the Controlled Substances Act to place MGM-15 and MGM-16 in Schedule I and to schedule synthetic 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in Schedule I above a specific threshold, according to the bill status record KNT reviewed at the time. The outlet's account does not say whether the House text mirrors the Senate version, and KNT was unable to retrieve the House bill text from congress.gov on September 27.

The bills would give statutory permanence to restrictions the Drug Enforcement Administration has so far pursued through temporary scheduling. The DEA [placed mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in Schedule I][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] by a temporary order published August 26, which runs for two years. Its separate proposal to schedule 7-OH above a concentration threshold [had not been published as a final order as of KNT's last check][dea-7oh-temporary-scheduling-order-still-unpublished].

## The American Kratom Association's endorsement

The American Kratom Association said on September 26 that it "strongly supports" the bill, [according to the association's release](https://www.americankratom.org/news/aka-strongly-supports-connors-end-gas-station-heroin-act), which was distributed the same day [through ACCESS Newswire](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/american-kratom-association-strongly-supports-154000882.html). The release calls the measure "Connor's End Gas Station Heroin Act" and says it is named for Connor Neeleman, who died at 26 of a 7-OH overdose after using what the association described as a highly concentrated synthetic 7-OH product deceptively marketed as kratom and purchased at a vape shop in Texas. His parents, David and Eva Neeleman, went on to advocate publicly for stricter rules on 7-OH and synthetic kratom products, the release says. The Washington Reporter did not mention the Neelemans or the "Connor's" name in its report.

"Congress cannot wait for more families to lose someone they love," Mac Haddow, the association's senior fellow on public policy, said in the release. "Dangerous synthetic opioids are being sold in everyday retail settings where consumers can mistake availability for safety." Haddow added that "natural kratom leaf must not become a marketing disguise for powerful synthetic opioid products."

The endorsement is consistent with the association's position through this year. It [welcomed the DEA's temporary scheduling of concentrated 7-OH][aka-endorses-dea-7oh-scheduling-action] in July and has supported scheduling the three synthetic compounds, while [warning that scheduling pseudoindoxyl without a workable measurement standard could reach natural leaf][aka-warns-pseudoindoxyl-scheduling-could-reach-natural-leaf] and [suing the DEA over the emergency order][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order] in August to press for that clarification. The September 26 release does not repeat the pseudoindoxyl measurement concern in connection with the bill.

## What the association wants added

The association said removing the products now on shelves "is only part of the job," and asked Congress to add provisions to the bill: independent testing requirements, manufacturing standards, accurate ingredient labeling, product traceability, clear regulatory authority to recall noncompliant products, and what it called meaningful penalties for manufacturers and distributors that adulterate products or mislead consumers, according to the release. "Congress must also stop companies from introducing the next chemically manipulated product under deceptive labeling," Haddow said.

The release names synthetic 7-OH, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 as the substances it wants removed from the market. It does not mention the deaths of two University of Mississippi students, which were the subject of the association's [press conference the day before][american-kratom-association-reporting-before-the-results-press-conference-ole-miss-south-carolina-wilson-mississippi-health-warning], and it does not address the DEA's pending 7-OH threshold order.

As introduced, both bills would need to pass their respective chambers and be signed into law before any scheduling provision took effect. Neither the Washington Reporter nor the association's release describes a committee referral or scheduled action on the House measure.
