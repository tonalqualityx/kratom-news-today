Two physicians have published a case report describing serotonin syndrome in a 38-year-old man who had been taking concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) chewable tablets many times a day while on a stable regimen of psychiatric medications, [according to the report](https://www.psychiatrist.com/pcc/serotonin-syndrome-kratom-use-stable-psychotropic-regimen/) in The Primary Care Companion for CNS Disorders, published September 24, 2026. The authors are Talya Kapenstein of the psychiatry residency program at Westchester Medical Center Health Network in Valhalla, New York, and Alëna A. Balasanova of the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Nebraska Medical Center in Omaha.

The product was a concentrated 7-OH preparation, which the report identifies by its labeling as 30-milligram chewable tablets containing "super strength" 7-hydroxymitragynine. Tablets of that kind contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts present in natural kratom leaf, a distinction the Drug Enforcement Administration drew in July when it [moved to schedule 7-OH above a concentration threshold][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] while stating that botanical kratom with naturally occurring trace 7-OH was outside the action. The report's title refers to "kratom use," and its mechanism discussion centers on mitragynine, the principal alkaloid in kratom leaf.

## What the patient was taking

The man had diagnoses of depression and anxiety disorders and a home medication list that included duloxetine 120 milligrams, levomilnacipran 80 milligrams, quetiapine 200 milligrams, dextroamphetamine/amphetamine 15 milligrams, propranolol 40 milligrams, eszopiclone 3 milligrams, clonazepam 0.5 milligrams as needed, and benztropine 0.5 milligrams daily, according to the report. Duloxetine and levomilnacipran are both serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and quetiapine is an antipsychotic.

He had been using the 7-OH tablets daily and, in the week before he came to the hospital, had escalated to 30 milligrams "up to 8-10 times per day," the authors wrote.

## How he presented

The patient arrived with disorganized thoughts, psychomotor restlessness, and hypervigilance, along with a heart rate in the 120s, blood pressure in the 140s over 90s, and sweating, according to the report. The authors wrote that his symptoms were initially interpreted as psychosis rather than serotonin toxicity. He later developed rhabdomyolysis, a breakdown of muscle tissue, with a creatine kinase level of 20,373 units per liter, the report states.

His symptoms resolved within 48 hours after the serotonergic medications were stopped, and he was discharged on the fourth hospital day with a prescription for buprenorphine-naloxone for a newly diagnosed kratom use disorder, according to the report. Buprenorphine-naloxone was also the treatment described in a [Journal of the American Pharmacists Association case report][case-report-documents-opioid-like-7oh-withdrawal] on opioid-like withdrawal in a man who stopped using about 360 milligrams of 7-OH a day, as KNT reported in August.

## The mechanism the authors proposed

The authors attributed the toxicity to a drug interaction. "Mitragynine is thought to inhibit drug metabolism by CYP3A4 (in both liver and intestinal cells) and CYP2D6 (in liver cells)," they wrote. In their account, that inhibition raised the serum levels of duloxetine, levomilnacipran, and quetiapine enough to precipitate serotonergic toxicity in a patient whose regimen had otherwise been stable. CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 are cytochrome P450 enzymes that metabolize a large share of prescription drugs.

The report does not describe laboratory analysis of the tablets, and it does not report measured blood levels of the patient's medications or of kratom alkaloids, based on the text available to KNT. The mechanism is the authors' proposed explanation for one patient.

## What the authors concluded

Kapenstein and Balasanova wrote that clinicians should "consider kratom use as a possible precipitant of serotonin toxicity in patients with symptoms of SS who were otherwise stable on their psychotropic medication regimen," and that "prompt withdrawal of the offending serotonergic agents is necessary for symptom resolution." Those are the authors' recommendations to other clinicians. A single case report cannot establish how often 7-OH products or kratom interact with serotonergic medications in this way, and the report does not make population-level claims.

Wills Eye Hospital physicians [described visual snow-like symptoms and retinal changes][retinal-cases-brief-reports-wills-eye-70-year-old-daily-kratom-visual-snow-ellipsoid-zone-disruption-case-report] in a 70-year-old daily kratom user in a report that drew attention this week, as KNT reported. That report did not specify what form of kratom the patient used. The serotonin syndrome report does, and the product it names is a concentrated 7-OH tablet.
