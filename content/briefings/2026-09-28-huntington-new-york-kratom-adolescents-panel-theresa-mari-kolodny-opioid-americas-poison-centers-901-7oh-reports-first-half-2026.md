Health and addiction speakers on Long Island told parents that kratom drinks, gummies, and other products can cause dependence and serious illness, during a panel titled "Kratom and Adolescents: Emerging Drug Trends" presented by Huntington, New York Councilwoman Theresa Mari, [NTD reported](https://www.ntd.com/new-york-panel-warns-of-risks-to-teens-from-potent-kratom-drinks-gummies_1175264.html) on September 26, 2026. NTD said the panel took place last week and did not report a date or venue.

The speakers, as NTD described them, urged parents to recognize warning signs, to question products marketed as natural, and to press for stronger safeguards. The article did not report any statement from a kratom manufacturer, retailer, or advocacy organization at the event.

## What the panelists said

"Kratom is an opioid, and because it's an opioid, that means that everything we know about opioids applies to kratom," said Andrew Kolodny, medical director of the Opioid Policy Research Collaborative, according to NTD. Kolodny said regular use can cause dependence and disrupt people's lives, and he urged policymakers to address local sales to protect children.

Jada Pollock, a licensed mental health counselor, said plant-based marketing should not be mistaken for proof of safety. "One thing I would want parents and everyone here to walk away with is the fact that natural does not mean risk free," Pollock said, per NTD. She called for clearer information about product contents and risks.

Barry Zaks, director of the Huntington Drug & Alcohol Counseling Center, said kratom has become a multibillion-dollar business that is already part of a culture in which people use mind-altering substances for recreation, pain, or coping, and that communities "cannot afford to ignore it," NTD reported. Linda Oristano, executive director of AWARE, who moderated, said prevention takes more than warning adolescents about another drug, and that families need accurate information, early recognition of emerging trends, and communities that keep young people connected and supported.

Kolodny's characterization of kratom as an opioid is his position. The American Kratom Association, in a separate release the next day about the Ole Miss death investigations, restated its view that natural kratom leaf, which contains only trace 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), is a different product category from concentrated and synthetic 7-OH preparations, [as KNT reports today][american-kratom-association-asks-lafayette-county-sheriff-joey-east-identify-independently-test-packaged-kratom-ole-miss-cychlorphine]. NTD's account of the panel does not record whether any speaker addressed that distinction beyond the FDA's own description, summarized below.

## The numbers NTD cited

NTD attributed its poison-center figures to a March report in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, the same report [KNT covered in June][cdc-kratom-poison-center-calls-surge]. Poison centers recorded 14,449 kratom exposure reports involving people 12 and older from 2015 through 2025, NTD reported, with annual reports rising about 1,200 percent from 258 in 2015 to 3,434 in 2025. Multiple-substance cases accounted for 38 percent of reports but 184 of the 233 kratom-associated deaths, or 79 percent, according to the article's account of the CDC data. Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, cannabis, stimulants, and antidepressants were among the substances reported alongside kratom.

For 2026, NTD cited a July 8 health advisory from America's Poison Centers, which said poison centers received 3,190 reports involving kratom or its derivatives through June 30, a pace the organization said would represent a 67.8 percent increase over 2025 if it held. Reports involving 7-OH reached 901 in the first half of 2026, compared with 593 during all of 2025, per the advisory as reported by NTD. Reported symptoms included vomiting, agitation, confusion, rapid heart rate, breathing trouble, seizures, loss of consciousness, dependence, and withdrawal.

Poison center reports are exposure calls, not confirmed diagnoses or a count of products in circulation, and the CDC report as KNT summarized it in June found that outcomes were worse when kratom was combined with other substances. NTD's article does not say whether the 7-OH reports distinguish concentrated products from natural leaf exposures.

## The FDA framing in the article

NTD wrote that the Food and Drug Administration describes enhanced 7-OH products as potent opioid products that have not been shown to be safe or effective for any use, citing the agency's consumer warning. The article noted that 7-OH occurs naturally in trace amounts in kratom leaf and that the FDA's warning is directed at products with added or concentrated levels, which the agency says can be dangerous and should be avoided. No 7-OH product has an FDA-approved medical use, NTD reported, and tablets, gummies, drink mixes, and shots remain available online and at smoke shops, gas stations, and corner stores.

The article did not report any ordinance, resolution, or pending local law in the Town of Huntington, and Mari was not quoted. Huntington is in Suffolk County. Elsewhere in New York, Saratoga County's public safety committee [advanced a countywide ban on all kratom sales][saratoga-county-new-york-committee-advances-total-kratom-ban-board-of-supervisors] on September 19, and Rensselaer County Executive Steve McLaughlin [was weighing whether to sign a countywide sales ban][rensselaer-county-executive-mclaughlin-weighs-kratom-ban-local-law-t-4-public-hearing] as of September 25, as KNT reported.
