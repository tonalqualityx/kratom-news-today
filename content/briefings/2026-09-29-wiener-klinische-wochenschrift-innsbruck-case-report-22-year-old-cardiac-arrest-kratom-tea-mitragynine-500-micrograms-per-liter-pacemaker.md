Intensive care physicians in Innsbruck, Austria, have published what they describe as the country's first case of cardiac arrest associated with isolated kratom poisoning, a previously healthy 22-year-old man who collapsed after drinking kratom tea and whose blood plasma contained 500 micrograms of mitragynine per liter, the [Kronen Zeitung reported](https://www.krone.at/4312155) on September 28, 2026. The case report, ["Sudden cardiac arrest in a young healthy man following kratom tea consumption: a case report and brief literature review,"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00508-026-02826-5) appears in Wiener klinische Wochenschrift, the Vienna clinical weekly published by Springer, with Jonas Dunz of the Medical University of Innsbruck's Department of Anaesthesia and Intensive Care Medicine as first author, according to the [Tiroler Tageszeitung](https://www.tt.com/artikel/30943251/22-jaehriger-erlitt-herzstillstand-durch-kratom-tee).

The product involved was tea prepared from kratom leaf powder, the Krone and [ANSA](https://www.ansa.it/trentino/notizie/2026/09/28/innsbruck-22enne-in-arresto-cardiaco-dopo-aver-bevuto-infuso-di-kratom_4e910367-403b-4315-8e60-d0a2a6dc6572.html) reported. None of the accounts describe a concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) product, and the alkaloid the authors measured was mitragynine rather than 7-OH. The reports do not say whether the tea itself was tested or where the leaf powder was purchased, though the Krone noted that kratom is readily available in Austria at specialty herb shops and vending machines.

## The case as the authors describe it

"We are reporting the first case of cardiac arrest in Austria associated with isolated kratom poisoning in a young man," Dunz and his co-authors wrote, according to the Krone's account of the paper. The authors wrote that toxicological blood analysis confirmed the suspicion by showing significantly elevated mitragynine concentrations, and that kratom, though considered a low-risk substance, can cause severe and potentially fatal side effects, the Krone reported.

The man collapsed suddenly in his apartment, and his roommate found him unconscious, convulsing, and without a pulse and began basic life support, the authors wrote, per the Krone. An emergency physician confirmed cardiac arrest, according to the report. Circulation returned after about four minutes of chest compressions and an adrenaline injection, then the heart stopped a second time and was restarted with intensified resuscitation, the Krone reported. The patient was intubated and transported to University Hospital Innsbruck on mechanical chest compressions, the newspaper reported.

Extensive examination found no evidence of a serious underlying condition, and the man was otherwise healthy, the Krone reported. Initial toxicology showed no alcohol, sedatives, sleeping pills, or antidepressants, and a urine screen was negative for amphetamines, cocaine, and opiates except for fentanyl, which the report described as present as a pain medication, according to the newspaper. The patient then reported drinking kratom tea. "Targeted tests following the reported consumption of kratom tea revealed elevated mitragynine levels of 500 micrograms per liter of blood plasma," the authors wrote, as quoted by the Krone. The journal abstract, as indexed by Springer, describes the arrest as asystolic and the measurement as targeted mass-spectrometric testing.

The patient repeatedly developed a heart rate of 25 to 30 beats per minute and was fitted with a pacemaker, and he had transient mild neurological problems, the Krone reported. He was discharged after two weeks without further complications, according to the Krone and ANSA.

## How the authors read the mitragynine level

The authors compared the patient's plasma concentration with a global analysis of 156 kratom-associated deaths in which mitragynine levels were about 398 micrograms per liter, the Krone and Tiroler Tageszeitung reported. The two papers characterized that figure differently: the Krone described it as an average and the Tiroler Tageszeitung as a median. Neither report identified the analysis by author or journal.

"Mitragynine poisoning is the most likely cause of the cardiac arrest in this otherwise healthy young man and underscores the potential for serious side effects from kratom," the Innsbruck physicians wrote, according to the Krone. "The actual frequency of such events is likely underestimated, as mitragynine is not routinely included in standard toxicological tests." The paper's abstract calls for increased clinical awareness, public education, and further investigation of the cardiovascular safety of mitragynine, per Springer's listing.

The finding is a single case report, a limitation the authors acknowledged, according to the Krone. The reports do not describe how much tea the man drank or over what period, do not identify the 156-death analysis by author or journal, and do not say whether that analysis reflects postmortem or antemortem samples.

## Context the authors and the coverage supplied

The authors placed the case against United States poison-center data, writing that kratom is increasingly popular in the U.S., where they said an estimated two million adults consume it annually, according to the Krone. National Poison Data System figures cited by the authors show kratom-related poisoning cases increased twelvefold between 2015 and 2025, peaking at 3,434 cases in 2025, with 233 deaths reported over the period, the Krone reported. Those are the same figures the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published in March 2026, [as Kratom News Today (KNT) reported][cdc-kratom-poison-center-calls-surge]; the CDC analysis found that most of the deaths involved other substances alongside kratom.

The Krone also cited a July 2025 statement from the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices warning patients against using kratom for medical purposes because its safety and efficacy "have not yet been sufficiently tested," and a December 2025 statement from a German consumer protection agency, which the Krone did not name, that the newspaper quoted as: "Not a legal dietary supplement: Kratom powder can be addictive and have dangerous side effects." The newspaper said its own test purchase in Innsbruck last year found a package labeled "not intended for human consumption and is addictive."

KNT has covered other recent kratom case literature. A [systematic review in the Journal of Medical Toxicology][journal-medical-toxicology-systematic-review-kratom-pulmonary-toxicity-24-reports-five-categories] that KNT covered on September 20, 2026, catalogued 24 published reports of pulmonary toxicity, and a [case report in The Primary Care Companion for CNS Disorders][primary-care-companion-case-report-serotonin-syndrome-38-year-old-super-strength-7oh-tablets-psychotropic-regimen] covered on September 27, 2026, described serotonin syndrome in a man taking concentrated 7-OH tablets. The Innsbruck case differs from the latter in that the product was leaf tea and the alkaloid measured was mitragynine. The full text of the Wiener klinische Wochenschrift paper is behind Springer's paywall, and KNT has not reviewed it beyond the abstract and the newspaper accounts.
