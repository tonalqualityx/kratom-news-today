The Global Kratom Coalition is circulating a peer-reviewed clinical review that argues physicians cannot assess a kratom-related case without first establishing whether the patient used natural leaf or a concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) product, according to a [release the coalition distributed through GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/30/3371512/0/en/global-kratom-coalition-highlights-new-medical-review-on-natural-vs-synthetic-kratom-products.html) on September 30, 2026. A version of the same release carrying a September 29 Washington dateline [ran in the Rutland Herald](https://www.rutlandherald.com/features/health/new-medical-review-urges-physicians-to-distinguish-natural-kratom-leaf-from-high-potency-synthetic-products/article_5b65bee4-7fef-5526-80f8-b8833117dbfd.html) and other outlets a day earlier.

The paper, ["Kratom in Clinical Practice: What Physicians Need to Know,"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42757335/) appears in the July-August 2026 issue of Missouri Medicine, the journal of the Missouri State Medical Association, at volume 123, issue 4, pages 292 to 297, according to its PubMed record, which lists a first publication date of July 1, 2026 and a free full text in PubMed Central. The authors are Mark S. Gold, a professor of neuroscience and psychiatry at the University of Florida College of Medicine, and Nicole M. Avena, an associate professor of neuroscience at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The review has been available for roughly three months; the coalition's promotion of it is the new development, and it follows [law enforcement records in the Ole Miss death investigations][lafayette-county-affidavit-smax-pseudo-dhm-tablets-both-ole-miss-scenes-7oh-preliminary-mgm-15-untested-aka-students-should-have-been-warned] in which, according to a Lafayette County Metro Narcotics affidavit, the products found at the scenes were packaged tablets labeled as pseudo-DHM, with preliminary testing finding 7-OH in some pills, and not natural leaf kratom; investigators have said no link to the deaths has been confirmed.

## What the review says

The abstract frames the paper around product identity. "Kratom use is increasingly encountered in clinical practice, yet products marketed under this name encompass markedly different substances with distinct pharmacologic and risk profiles," Gold and Avena wrote, according to the PubMed record. The abstract says leaf contains a complex mixture of alkaloids dominated by mitragynine, with effects the authors describe as stimulant or sedative depending on serving size, while many modern products labeled as kratom contain concentrated, semi-synthetic, or synthetic alkaloids, particularly 7-OH, that the authors say carry substantially greater opioid-like potency.

The authors state that the review summarizes pharmacology, clinical effects, and safety data on natural leaf, contrasts those findings with evidence on high-potency alkaloid products, and examines how exposure misclassification and polysubstance use limit adverse event surveillance. Their central claim, in the abstract's words: "Clinical and toxicologic data suggest that severe acute harms attributed to 'kratom' disproportionately involve concentrated alkaloid formulations rather than whole-leaf products." The paper offers guidance "to assist physicians in screening, counseling, and documentation, emphasizing the importance of distinguishing product type to support risk assessment, harm reduction, and patient safety."

The coalition's release adds figures the abstract does not. According to the release, the review cites poison center data from 2017 through 2023 reporting 23 deaths associated with "kratom," more than a third of which involved documented polysubstance use, a figure the release contrasted with more than 2,000 opioid-related deaths. The release also says the review describes products falsely labeled as kratom that contain isolated 7-OH and derivatives such as MGM-15 at levels exceeding natural leaf by more than 100 times, and that it cites a 2024 toxicology review finding insufficient evidence linking natural kratom leaf alone to severe acute adverse effects. Those characterizations are the coalition's summary of the paper; the review's full text was not independently reviewed for this briefing.

## What the coalition and an author said

"Physicians, regulators and consumers need to know whether they are discussing natural kratom leaf or a concentrated synthetic 7-OH product," Avena said in the release.

Matthew Lowe, the coalition's executive director, framed the paper as a clinical tool. "This review gives clinicians a clear framework: ask what product a patient used, document it precisely," Lowe said. He added that "consumers deserve care informed by facts, not labels that flatten important scientific differences."

The release states the coalition's policy position alongside the science: target concentrated synthetic products while preserving access to traditional kratom leaf. That is the same line the coalition took when it [applauded the Drug Enforcement Administration's August order][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] placing mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in Schedule I, an order the coalition said at the time does not reach unaltered leaf products.

## How the review fits the surveillance debate

The paper's emphasis on exposure misclassification matches a theme in other recent literature. A [systematic review in the Journal of Medical Toxicology][journal-medical-toxicology-systematic-review-kratom-pulmonary-toxicity-24-reports-five-categories] earlier this month sorted kratom-associated lung injury reports into provisional categories and noted that most case reports did not characterize the product involved. The Gold and Avena abstract likewise identifies exposure misclassification as a limitation in adverse event surveillance.

The review is a narrative review in a state medical society journal rather than a systematic review or a clinical trial, and the abstract describes its safety conclusions as what the data "suggest." Neither the PubMed record nor the coalition's release describes funding for the paper or any relationship between the authors and the coalition, and the release does not say whether the coalition had any role in the review's preparation.
