Three sheriffs in Florida's western Panhandle are urging residents to stay away from kratom products after the District 1 Medical Examiner's Office counted nine kratom-related overdose deaths this year across Escambia, Santa Rosa, Okaloosa, and Walton counties, [WEAR reported](https://weartv.com/news/local/northwest-florida-sheriffs-warn-against-kratom-use-after-9-deaths-reported-in-area) on September 29, 2026. The sheriffs singled out concentrated products such as gummies and oils with high levels of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), which WEAR distinguished from raw leaf kratom, and Okaloosa County Sheriff Eric Aden said the Florida Department of Law Enforcement (FDLE) is working on a field test to identify high concentrations of it. Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier's June emergency rule placed concentrated 7-OH in Schedule I under state law, according to the [attorney general's office](https://www.myfloridalegal.com/newsrelease/attorney-general-james-uthmeier-signs-emergency-rule-immediately-scheduling-dangerous-7), which said at the time the rule targets concentrated and copycat 7-OH formulas and not natural kratom leaf.

The WEAR report does not say how many of the nine deaths involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products and how many involved natural leaf kratom, whether other substances were present, or what toxicology sits behind the medical examiner's count. The station described raw kratom as legal in Florida and more concentrated forms with higher 7-OH levels as illegal, without attaching that distinction to any individual death.

## What the medical examiner and the sheriffs said

The nine deaths span the four counties covered by the District 1 Medical Examiner's Office, according to WEAR. Santa Rosa County Sheriff Randy Tifft told the station his office believes about half of them happened in his county and cited his office's own tally of overdoses and deaths through September 28, figures the report does not reconcile with the district-wide total.

Escambia County Sheriff Chip Simmons drew the line between leaf products and concentrates. "And then there's the concentrated version, or the gummies or oils and that sort of thing, which has a high level of what they call 7-OH," Simmons said, according to WEAR.

Aden, the Okaloosa County sheriff, described the compound itself. "It's an alkaloid, and it is 13 times stronger than morphine," he told the station. The figure is Aden's characterization, and the WEAR report does not attribute it to a study.

## A field test and pending charges

Aden said FDLE is developing a field test that would let officers identify products with high concentrations of 7-OH, WEAR reported. The station did not report a timeline for the test or say whether it would distinguish concentrated products from leaf material. Aden also said criminal charges are already pending in some cases, which WEAR characterized as charges against dealers, without naming defendants or specifying the counts.

WEAR did not cite a statute or rule for its statement that raw kratom is legal and concentrated forms are illegal. The attorney general's office said in its [June announcement](https://www.myfloridalegal.com/newsrelease/attorney-general-james-uthmeier-signs-emergency-rule-immediately-scheduling-dangerous-7) that the emergency rule scheduled concentrated 7-OH and six related compounds with concentration thresholds, as [covered at the time][florida-emergency-rule-7oh-schedule-one].

## Advice from the sheriffs, and what the report leaves open

The sheriffs' public message went beyond concentrated products. According to WEAR, they advised against all kratom use and urged residents to understand the difference between legal and illegal forms. WEAR did not report the sheriffs' reasoning for extending the advisory to leaf products, and it did not include comment from trade groups, retailers, or public health researchers.
