The American Medical Association (AMA) has updated its kratom issue brief and is circulating it to state medical societies and lawmakers ahead of 2027 legislative sessions, [the association said in its October 2, 2026, State Advocacy Update](https://www.ama-assn.org/health-care-advocacy/advocacy-update/oct-2-2026-state-advocacy-update). The brief, titled "Unregulated kratom is a dangerous substance and presents serious public health concerns," sets out four positions, the first of which supports legislation "banning synthesized, purified or derivative products from kratom for marketing, distribution, promotion and sale including but not limited to the unregulated mitragynine along with the 7-hydroxymitragynine and MGM-15 market."

That sentence is the one state legislators and kratom trade groups are likely to parse most closely. It names 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), the alkaloid at the center of the Drug Enforcement Administration's pending scheduling proposal, and MGM-15, a manufactured derivative the DEA placed in Schedule I in August. It also names mitragynine, the primary alkaloid in natural kratom leaf, in a list of products the AMA wants banned when sold in synthesized, purified, or derivative form. The brief's own framing refers to "unregulated kratom" and uses the phrase "gas station heroin," which is also the name of [Rep. Lance Gooden's House bill][gooden-house-bill-hr-10272-connors-end-gas-station-heroin-act-aka-endorsement] to schedule MGM-15, MGM-16, synthetic 7-OH, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, a bill that does not reach mitragynine.

## The four positions

According to the advocacy update, the AMA supports:

1. "Legislation banning synthesized, purified or derivative products from kratom for marketing, distribution, promotion and sale including but not limited to the unregulated mitragynine along with the 7-hydroxymitragynine and MGM-15 market."
2. "The safety and efficacy of kratom, and its derivatives, being determined through research and clinical trials and subsequently evaluated by the relevant regulatory entities."
3. "The FDA and state legislatures classifying (7-OH) kratom products as adulterated or misbranded when sold in child-appealing forms."
4. "Public education campaigns by physicians warning parents and youth of (7-OH) kratom risks, including adverse cutaneous effects."

The update says the brief cites data on kratom use disorder, National Poison Data System reports, and warnings from the U.S. Department of Justice and the Food and Drug Administration. The AMA page does not reproduce those figures, and KNT has not reviewed the full brief. America's Poison Centers reported 901 7-OH exposure reports in the first half of 2026, [as KNT reported in September][huntington-new-york-kratom-adolescents-panel-theresa-mari-kolodny-opioid-americas-poison-centers-901-7oh-reports-first-half-2026]; whether the AMA brief uses that figure or another is not stated in the update.

The reference to "adverse cutaneous effects" is new to the AMA's public kratom materials as far as KNT can determine. The update does not elaborate on it, and the association's June policy did not mention skin effects.

## How the position has moved since June

The AMA's House of Delegates [adopted policy in June targeting synthetic 7-OH products][ama-adopts-policy-targeting-synthetic-7oh-kratom], as KNT reported at the time. The October brief restates that position and widens the list of named targets to include "the unregulated mitragynine" market and MGM-15. MGM-15 did not appear in public reporting until this summer, and its inclusion tracks the DEA's August 26 temporary order placing it, MGM-16, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in Schedule I.

The second position, that safety and efficacy be "determined through research and clinical trials and subsequently evaluated by the relevant regulatory entities," restates a sequence the FDA has described for years: kratom has no approved use, and the agency has said any therapeutic claim would need to go through the drug-approval process. Read alongside the first position, the brief asks states to remove synthesized, purified, and derivative products from the market now and to let clinical research decide what, if anything, returns.

## Where the kratom advocacy groups stand

The AMA's language on purified mitragynine separates it from the position the two largest kratom trade groups have taken this week. The American Kratom Association (AKA) [urged Mississippi Attorney General Lynn Fitch on October 1](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/american-kratom-association-urges-mississippi-035200996.html) to amend her call for a statewide ban on all kratom and instead support legislation "specifically prohibiting dangerous synthetic and chemically manipulated opioid products," naming mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16. "Mississippi should separate the natural botanical from the synthetic opioids," said Mac Haddow, the AKA's senior fellow on public policy, according to the release. The Global Kratom Coalition took a similar position in Mississippi, [as KNT reported yesterday][mississippi-attorney-general-fitch-ban-kratom-all-forms-next-session-lafayette-metro-narcotics-hearings-synthetic-haddow-misled-gkc].

Both groups support bans on MGM-15 and on synthetic 7-OH, which puts them in agreement with part of the AMA's first position. The disagreement is over mitragynine. The AKA's position, restated at its September 30 press conference in Oxford, is that a product may be called kratom only when it retains the leaf's alkaloid profile at a ratio of at least 100 parts mitragynine to one part combined 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, and that products meeting that standard should be regulated through labeling, testing, and age limits. The AMA brief, as summarized in the update, would ban "purified" mitragynine products outright. Neither trade group had responded to the AMA update in coverage reviewed by KNT as of publication.

The AMA update does not name any state, and it does not describe the brief as model legislation. Its stated purpose is to help states "prepare for 2027 legislative sessions." Thirty-six states introduced kratom-related bills during the 2025-26 cycle, [according to a Governing review](https://www.governing.com/policy/why-more-states-are-eyeing-kratom-bans) published September 29, and the AMA document arrives as several of those states, Mississippi among them, are drafting for January. The DEA's search of a Mississippi store in connection with the Ole Miss deaths, [reported separately today][ole-miss-dea-affidavit-mosleh-charges-express-mart-water-valley-owner-aydah-they-took-it-all-wmc-timeline-rahmani-aka-letter-fitch], is the kind of enforcement backdrop the brief's authors are likely to cite.
