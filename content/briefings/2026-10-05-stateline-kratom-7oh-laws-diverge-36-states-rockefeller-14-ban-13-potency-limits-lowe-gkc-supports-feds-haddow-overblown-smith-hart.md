State laws on kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) are pulling in different directions as products containing concentrated 7-OH and other chemically modified kratom compounds are sold next to natural leaf products, [Stateline's Amanda Watford reported on October 5, 2026](https://stateline.org/2026/10/05/kratom-7-oh-laws-diverge-amid-the-spread-of-potent-synthetic-products/). The report, also carried by [News From The States](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/kratom-7-oh-laws-diverge-amid-spread-potent-synthetic-products), frames the question facing legislatures as how to address what Stateline called "increasingly potent kratom-related products without treating traditional kratom leaf the same way." Stateline described concentrated 7-OH as a kratom derivative and noted that botanical kratom contains naturally occurring 7-OH at low levels, a distinction the report carried through its account of the federal rule.

## The Rockefeller Institute and NCSL counts

At least 31 states regulate kratom in some form, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) as cited by Stateline. During the 2025 and 2026 legislative sessions, lawmakers in 36 states introduced legislation on kratom, 7-OH, or synthetic kratom compounds, according to the Rockefeller Institute of Government, which Stateline also cited. The Rockefeller analysis, per Stateline, lists 14 states that ban or schedule kratom, 13 with potency limits, 24 with sales restrictions, and three that ban or schedule products other than "pure leaf kratom." Stateline noted the categories overlap because a state can impose more than one type of restriction. The report added that many cities and towns have adopted their own sales restrictions, and it listed Connecticut, Georgia, Idaho, Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, New York, Tennessee, Virginia, Washington, and Wyoming as states whose lawmakers considered or adopted bans or regulations this year.

## Prohibition in Tennessee and Massachusetts, a regulated market in Rhode Island

Stateline grouped the states by approach. A Tennessee law that [took effect in July][georgia-kratom-study-committee-tennessee-ban-in-effect] prohibits possession, sale, and distribution of kratom statewide, which Stateline said makes Tennessee the 14th state to choose prohibition over regulation. In Massachusetts, Democratic Gov. Maura Healey's administration issued an [emergency order in August][massachusetts-emergency-order-all-kratom-schedule-one] placing all forms of kratom in Schedule I under state law for one year, Stateline reported.

Rhode Island went the other way, according to Stateline, becoming the first state to replace a statewide kratom ban with a regulated market for adults 21 and older. Kratom News Today (KNT) reported in June that the state's health department had [approved its first kratom manufacturer license][rhode-island-first-kratom-license-american-shaman-missouri-suspension]. Stateline said Rhode Island still prohibits products containing synthetic kratom alkaloids or other synthetically derived compounds, and it named mitragynine and 7-OH in that sentence. The report does not spell out how the state's law treats the naturally occurring forms of those two alkaloids beyond allowing the regulated leaf market.

## Oklahoma, Mississippi, and Pennsylvania

Oklahoma's rules, as Stateline described them, cap 7-OH at 1 percent of total kratom alkaloids, ban synthetic kratom-derived compounds and contaminants, and require manufacturers to disclose total alkaloids, mitragynine, and 7-OH on packaging. Donnie Anderson, director of the Oklahoma Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs Control, told Stateline that gaps in state law make it difficult for investigators to pursue suppliers of synthetically enhanced products. Rep. Tim Turner, a Republican and former sheriff and narcotics officer, said he plans to introduce legislation in 2027 targeting concentrated or synthetically enhanced kratom compounds, a plan [KNT reported on September 19][oklahoma-rep-tim-turner-schedule-one-bill-7oh-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-ocme-deaths]. "It is time to stop treating this like just another product on a convenience store shelf," Turner said, according to Stateline. "If companies knowingly manufacture, market, distribute or sell dangerous products that are killing Oklahomans, there needs to be accountability."

Mississippi's 2025 law bars sales to anyone under 21, prohibits certain synthesized or semisynthesized kratom compounds, sets registration requirements, and preserves local governments' ability to restrict kratom, Stateline reported. Republican Rep. Lee Yancey told Stateline he wants the Legislature to revisit the issue in 2027 and consider a statewide ban, following the deaths of two University of Mississippi students. Stateline said investigators found a potent synthetic kratom-derived product during that investigation and that toxicology results were pending. Mississippi Attorney General Lynn Fitch has separately said her office will [push a ban on kratom "in all forms"][mississippi-attorney-general-fitch-ban-kratom-all-forms-next-session-lafayette-metro-narcotics-hearings-synthetic-haddow-misled-gkc] next session, as KNT reported on October 1.

In Pennsylvania, Stateline reported, lawmakers are considering a bill that would let registered retailers sell natural-leaf kratom to adults 21 and older while banning synthetic and adulterated products, including anything containing more than 0.5 percent 7-OH. The bill cleared a House committee unanimously last week and heads to the full House, Stateline said. KNT reported the House Health Committee's [26-0 vote on House Bill 2657][pennsylvania-house-health-committee-26-0-hb-2657-kosierowski-regulation-and-oversight-of-kratom-act-synthetic-ban-21-tobacco-tax] on September 30.

## The federal line

Stateline summarized the federal position as follows: the Drug Enforcement Administration announced in July that it plans to [temporarily place 7-OH above a 0.05 percent concentration threshold in Schedule I][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products], and it placed three related compounds, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, [in Schedule I effective August 26, 2026][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds], through August 26, 2028. The 7-OH change, Stateline reported, would not apply to botanical kratom containing naturally occurring 7-OH below the threshold, and it has not yet taken effect.

Stateline also referred to a June New York Times report on efforts by kratom industry groups to build relationships with Trump administration officials and influence federal policy, noting that the industry has pushed to distinguish leaf from 7-OH products while supporting tougher restrictions on the synthetic ones. Mac Haddow, senior fellow on public policy at the American Kratom Association (AKA), told Stateline: "This is pretty much overblown by the critics of kratom, who are saying that some in the industry are trying to buy their way in to get a policy that they're pursuing, when in fact both sides are doing that."

## Where the advocates and clinicians stand

The groups Stateline quoted hold different positions on the federal action. Matthew Lowe, executive director of the Global Kratom Coalition (GKC), which Stateline described as representing kratom businesses and consumers, said of the federal action: "We support what the feds are doing. It's the right thing to do from a public health perspective." Jeff Smith, executive director of the Holistic Alternative Recovery Trust, which Stateline said represents some 7-OH manufacturers, defended the products in personal terms, telling Stateline that 7-OH products "are really the first thing in almost two decades that have helped me sleep for more than about a half hour at a time" and that they had helped him with chronic pain. The AKA [endorsed the DEA's 7-OH proposal in July][aka-endorses-dea-7oh-scheduling-action], as KNT reported at the time, and Lowe's group told Stateline it supports the federal action. Smith's group represents manufacturers of the products that action would reach.

Kellen Russoniello, director of public health at the Drug Policy Alliance, told Stateline the organization shares public-health concerns about 7-OH and its derivatives but opposes criminalization. "In a sensibly regulated model, the consumer would have much more information by being able to read the bottle and see what's in it, what percentage of certain substances are in there," he said. "It's a much better approach for protecting the consumer."

The clinicians Stateline quoted focused on pharmacology. Dr. Alta DeRoo, chief medical officer of the Hazelden Betty Ford Foundation, said leaf kratom is "weaker and it's hitting more than just the opioid receptor," while 7-OH is "very targeted at the mu-opioid receptor," which she described as the same receptor morphine and fentanyl act on. "It's easier to become dependent and more euphoric with the 7-OH, and so coming off of it is more dramatic as well," DeRoo said. Dr. Sarah Kawasaki, an associate professor of psychiatry and internal medicine at Pennsylvania State University, told Stateline the products are unregulated and that "there's no quality control needed to make sure that what is stated is on the bottle is actually in the bottle." John Puls, a Florida psychotherapist and addiction specialist, told Stateline that clinicians treating kratom or 7-OH dependence often use a short buprenorphine taper, the same approach used for opioid withdrawal. The American Medical Association's updated kratom issue brief, which [KNT covered on October 2][american-medical-association-october-2-state-advocacy-update-kratom-issue-brief-2027-sessions-ban-synthesized-purified-derivative-7oh-mgm-15], supports state legislation banning synthesized, purified, or derivative kratom products.

## The poison center figures and their limits

Stateline cited an analysis published on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) website in March, which KNT [covered in June][cdc-kratom-poison-center-calls-surge], reporting 14,449 kratom exposure reports to U.S. poison centers from 2015 through 2025, including 3,434 in 2025, about 1,200 percent more than in 2015. A news release cited by Stateline put reports involving kratom or its derivatives at 3,190 through June of this year; Stateline calculated that, at that pace, 2026 would finish nearly 68 percent above 2025. The CDC authors, per Stateline, found 233 deaths associated with kratom exposure reports over the 2015 to 2025 period, with more than one substance involved in 184 of them, or 79 percent, and opioids detected in 62 percent.

Stateline was explicit about what the data cannot show. The poison center reports do not identify whether an exposure involved traditional leaf products or concentrated and semisynthetic formulations such as 7-OH, the report said, and the CDC authors could not determine which substance was most responsible for an outcome when several were involved.
