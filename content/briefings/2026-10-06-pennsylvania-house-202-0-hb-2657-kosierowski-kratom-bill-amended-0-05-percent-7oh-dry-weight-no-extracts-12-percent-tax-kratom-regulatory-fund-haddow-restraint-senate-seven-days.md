The Pennsylvania House voted 202-0 on October 5, 2026 to pass House Bill 2657, the Regulation and Oversight of Kratom Act, according to the [House roll call record](https://www.palegis.us/house/roll-calls?sess_yr=2025&sess_ind=0&rc_body=H&bill_body=H&bill_type=B&bill_number=2657). The bill, sponsored by Rep. Bridget Kosierowski, a Lackawanna County Democrat, would allow registered retailers to sell natural leaf kratom to adults 21 and older and would prohibit synthetic and semi-synthetic kratom products, a category that includes concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations containing the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts in raw leaf. The [Pennsylvania Capital-Star reported](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/bill-regulate-kratom-sales-and-ban-synthetic-compounds-passes-pa-house) that the bill now goes to the Senate, which has seven session days remaining, and would also need the governor's signature.

The vote came six days after the House Health Committee [reported the bill 26-0][pennsylvania-house-health-committee-26-0-hb-2657-kosierowski-regulation-and-oversight-of-kratom-act-synthetic-ban-21-tobacco-tax] on September 29. According to the [General Assembly's bill history](https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/hb2657), the House adopted two amendments on second consideration on September 30, A04377 by a vote of 201-2 and A04411 by 200-3, then re-committed the bill to the Appropriations Committee, which re-reported it 37-0 on October 5 ahead of final passage the same day.

## What the amendments changed

The amended text, published as [Printer's Number 3980](https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/text/HTM/2025/0/HB2657/PN3980), differs from the introduced version in several places. The definition of "natural kratom product" now requires that a product contain kratom in natural form, meaning dried, cut and sifted leaf or raw leaf powder; that it not be mixed with any non-kratom ingredient other than an inert food-grade or pharmaceutical-grade encapsulating agent with no pharmacological activity; that it not contain a kratom extract; and that it not contain more than 0.05 percent 7-OH on a dry weight basis. The 0.05 percent dry-weight figure matches the plant-material threshold in the Drug Enforcement Administration's [pending federal proposal][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] to temporarily schedule concentrated 7-OH products, though neither the bill text nor the coverage of the vote draws that comparison.

The bill's separate definition of "synthetic kratom product" is unchanged from the introduced version: any intermediate or finished product with 7-OH exceeding 0.5 percent of its total alkaloid composition, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above 10 parts per million, any synthetic alkaloid including synthetic mitragynine or synthetic 7-OH, or kratom in a form that does not qualify as a natural product. The amended text retitles Section 3 as "regulation of synthetic or semi-synthetic kratom products" and prohibits manufacturing, delivering, selling, or possessing with intent to sell those products, with exceptions for Controlled Substance Act registrants, licensed practitioners, and products licensed by the Food and Drug Administration. The Capital-Star and The Center Square both described the bill as banning synthetic kratom; the Capital-Star's October 3 report on the committee vote, [carried by PhillyVoice](https://www.phillyvoice.com/kratom-regulations-legislation-pennsylvania-house/), cited the 0.5 percent alkaloid-fraction figure, and neither outlet's coverage of the floor vote mentioned the new dry-weight limit on natural products.

The tax provision was rewritten. The introduced bill applied the state's tobacco products tax to natural kratom; the amended text instead imposes "a tax at the rate of 12% of the purchase price paid by a consumer for each separate sale at retail of a natural kratom product," collected by the retailer and paid to the Department of Revenue. Fees and tax receipts would be deposited in a new Kratom Regulatory Fund in the state treasury, split between a Kratom Administration Account appropriated to the Department of Health and a Kratom Enforcement Account appropriated to the Office of Attorney General. [The Center Square reported](https://www.thecentersquare.com/pennsylvania/article_db7cae74-822c-4b4f-bbd1-3a8775c4ac89.html) that the tax is projected to raise $4.4 million in its first year, plus about $241,000 in additional sales tax.

Label requirements were expanded. In addition to the processor's name and address, the FDA disclaimer, and a natural-form verification carried over from the introduced bill, the amended text requires a lot or batch code, a production or packaging date, net weight, a statement advising against use by minors or by anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding, and a notice reading "WARNING: KRATOM MAY CAUSE DEPENDENCE AND OPIOID-LIKE WITHDRAWAL. USE MAY IMPAIR JUDGMENT." The Department of Health could require additional warnings after publishing notice in the Pennsylvania Bulletin, with a 60-day delay before any new warning takes effect. Registration and renewal fees for processors and retailers remain capped at $100 each, retailers would still have to use a transaction scan device to verify age, and products would still have to be sold in resealable, child-resistant packaging.

Penalties are unchanged: a knowing violation of the synthetic-product prohibition would be a misdemeanor carrying a fine of up to $15,000, up to five years in prison, or both, while violations of the natural-product requirements would carry civil penalties of up to $10,000 per offense, with each day a separate offense. The amended text adds that the Department of Health may suspend, revoke, or refuse to renew a registration. The effective-date section was also revised: the introduced bill made most provisions effective immediately with the registration and civil-penalty sections effective in 90 days, while the amended text delays Sections 3 through 6, which cover the synthetic prohibition, natural-product rules, penalties, and the tax, by 180 days.

## What lawmakers and the AKA said

Kosierowski told the Capital-Star that "this legislation is not about pretending that kratom is risk-free. It is about acknowledging the reality that kratom is already here, and establishing rules that make the marketplace more transparent and accountable." The Center Square quoted her saying that "kratom is already here" and that the question is "how do we establish standards to protect our customers?" Rep. Brenda Pugh, a Luzerne County Republican, told The Center Square that "kratom is a big deal" and that people using "such a natural product to deal with pain should be assured of its safety."

The American Kratom Association's position, as reported, supports the synthetic ban and opposes parts of the natural-product regime. Mac Haddow, the association's senior fellow on public policy, told The Center Square that registration fees and taxes amount to a "restraint" on business and argued that age-restricted kratom should be sold through ordinary retail channels the way cigarettes and alcohol are. In the Capital-Star's October 3 report, Haddow said the association broadly supports the bill's aims but wants two changes: language allowing products made through extraction methods using FDA-approved solvents, provided the finished product is no more potent than natural leaf, and stronger language to keep manufacturers from evading the ban by deriving new synthetic compounds from kratom alkaloids. The amended text adopted September 30 moves in the opposite direction on the first point, excluding any kratom extract from the natural-product definition. The coverage available for this briefing does not include an association response to the amended text or to the final vote.

The association's stance in Pennsylvania is consistent with its position on federal action. It [endorsed the DEA's proposal][aka-endorses-dea-7oh-scheduling-action] to schedule concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products in July while opposing restrictions on natural leaf, and it told the Capital-Star in September that it supports regulation and opposes a ban in Pennsylvania, as [covered here][pennsylvania-health-department-reviewing-7oh-request-lawmakers-push-regulation].

## The numbers behind the bill

The Capital-Star's October 3 report cited state figures showing 33 overdose deaths in Pennsylvania since 2025 in which kratom was present and 106 poison control center calls through June 30, 2026, of which 41 involved 7-OH. The report did not say how many of the deaths involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products rather than natural leaf kratom, and the Department of Health's statement in that report said only that the agency "is reviewing the situation and considering all options to ensure any decisions support the health and safety of Pennsylvanians."

## What happens next

The Capital-Star reported that spokespeople for Senate Majority Leader Joe Pittman and Senate President Pro Tempore Kim Ward did not respond to requests for comment on whether the chamber would take up the bill in its remaining seven session days. The bill record shows no Senate committee referral as of the morning of October 6. Separately, Utah health officials on October 5 [linked four deaths to tablets containing mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15][utah-four-deaths-four-months-buzzers-cats-claw-tablets-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-moss-confirmed-deaths-caused-by-these-products], two of the synthetic compounds that HB 2657's synthetic-product definition would reach in Pennsylvania through its pseudoindoxyl limit and its synthetic-alkaloid clause.
