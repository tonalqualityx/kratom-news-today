A federal judge in Manhattan has refused to dismiss a proposed consumer lawsuit against Mitra-9 Brands LLC, the maker of ready-to-drink kratom beverages sold under the Mitra9 name, [Law360's Mike Curley reported on October 6, 2026](https://www.law360.com/articles/2534609/kratom-maker-can-t-ditch-buyer-s-addiction-false-ad-suit). U.S. District Judge Laura T. Swain of the Southern District of New York found that the complaint "sufficiently alleges that it knew about the addictive qualities of those products but didn't warn consumers," according to Law360. The suit frames the alleged failure to warn as false advertising.

The decision is procedural. It allows the case to move forward and makes no finding on whether Mitra-9 is liable, per Law360. The article identifies Bursor & Fisher as counsel for the plaintiff and lists Lippes Mathias and Michael Best & Friedrich among the firms in the case. The portions of the article available without a subscription do not name the plaintiff, give the docket number, or specify which statutes the surviving claims rest on, and Kratom News Today (KNT) had not reviewed the court's order at the time of this briefing.

## What is known about the products

Nothing in the Law360 coverage describes the products at issue as concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, and Law360 refers to them only as the company's kratom products. An [AdExchanger profile of the company by Joanna Gerber](https://www.adexchanger.com/marketers/how-mitra9-advertises-in-a-regulated-industry-rife-with-consumer-mistrust/), published October 5, described Mitra9's line as 12-ounce ready-to-drink kratom and kava beverages made from extract rather than whole leaf. AdExchanger did not report the products' alkaloid content and did not mention 7-OH. Whether the beverages contain 7-OH above the trace levels found in natural leaf is not addressed in either report, and KNT could not determine it from the available coverage.

The theory the court allowed to proceed turns on what the company knew about its products' dependence potential and what it told buyers, according to Law360's description of the ruling. The [wrongful-death suit filed in September by the family of a Menifee, California woman][family-lawsuit-kratom-death-menifee-california], by comparison, rests on an alleged death that the family attributes to a kratom product.

## A marketing profile published the day before

The AdExchanger piece ran a day before the ruling was reported and did not mention the litigation. In it, Mitra9 founder and CEO Dallas Vasquez described a company that relies on both direct ad buys and programmatic advertising while working around platform restrictions. "Programmatic is absolutely more restrictive," Vasquez told AdExchanger, adding that "our products cannot be sold on certain platforms," which the outlet said requires the company to verify compliance before placing ads.

Vasquez told AdExchanger the company tries to answer consumer skepticism with education and with published testing, including certificates of analysis and batch testing. "In any emerging category, transparency does more work than hype," Vasquez said, per the article. "Before you compete for preference, you have to earn a customer's understanding." AdExchanger positioned the brand's messaging around mood and social occasions and noted, in its own framing, that kratom's alkaloids act on opioid receptors, which it cited as the source of the safety concerns the company's marketing has to address. KNT has not reviewed Mitra9's advertising and cannot say whether the complaint cites any of the marketing described in the profile.

## Where the case fits

The ruling joins a set of 2026 cases against kratom companies built on failure-to-warn and deceptive-marketing theories, brought by public plaintiffs as well as private ones. The city of Baltimore [sued several manufacturers in July][baltimore-sues-kratom-manufacturers-deceptive-marketing] over allegedly misleading claims about addiction potential and safety, according to the Baltimore Sun, and Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes [sued Botanic Tonics in September][arizona-attorney-general-mayes-sues-botanic-tonics-feel-free-consumer-fraud-act-first-in-nation] under the state's Consumer Fraud Act, alleging the company promoted its kratom-and-kava shots as energy drinks while failing to disclose addiction and withdrawal risks, according to the attorney general's office. The Law360 article does not compare the Mitra-9 ruling to those cases, and KNT has not seen reporting on dispositive rulings in either.

Law360 did not report a comment from Mitra-9 or its counsel on the ruling, and the company's reaction was not available in the coverage reviewed for this briefing. The American Kratom Association and other trade groups had not publicly addressed the decision as of October 7, based on the coverage available to KNT.
