Three kratom sellers and distributors sued by the City of Baltimore have asked a Maryland federal judge to throw out the case, arguing the city "is trying to step into enforcement shoes that are meant to be filled by state actors," [Law360's Mike Curley reported](https://www.law360.com/productliability/articles/2535595/kratom-cos-say-baltimore-overreached-with-marketing-suit) on October 8, 2026. The city's suit, which [KNT covered when the Baltimore Sun first reported it][baltimore-sues-kratom-manufacturers-deceptive-marketing] in late July, alleges the companies misled consumers about the addictive nature of kratom. The defendants are represented by Kilpatrick Townsend and the Law Offices of Thomas J. Thomas, according to Law360, whose report sits behind a paywall and does not identify the assigned judge.

The case is a marketing-deception action rather than an injury suit, and the products it describes span both categories that have divided this year's regulatory debate. The complaint targets O.P.M.S.-branded kratom products, which it describes as capsules, loose powder, and liquid extracts, and it separately quotes marketing for ROXY-branded 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) tablets in 16 mg, 20 mg, and 60 mg strengths, [according to the complaint](https://courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/kratom-complaint.pdf) posted by Courthouse News Service. Tablets at those strengths are concentrated 7-OH preparations, distinct from the trace 7-OH in natural leaf, and the complaint's addiction and safety allegations are directed at both product types without drawing that distinction itself.

## Who the city sued, and under what law

The complaint, dated July 27, 2026 and signed by Baltimore City Solicitor Ebony M. Thompson with outside counsel Gordon, Wolf & Carney, names JOpen LLC, which it says does business as A1 Wholesale, Evolutionary Organics, Innovo Activas, American Kratom-D, and Party Nuts; Martian Sales Inc.; Pur Botanicals LLC, doing business as Choice Organics; and unnamed John Doe entities. The city alleges the defendants "share principals and membership" and operate as "a web of shell companies and alter egos" that collectively design, label, package, and sell the products, per the complaint. Through July 1, 2026, the complaint says, the products were widely sold in Baltimore at gas stations, convenience stores, corner stores, and smoke and vape shops, and online through the Party Nuts site.

The sole legal basis is the city's Consumer Protection Ordinance, Baltimore City Code Article 2, Section 4. The city alleges two kinds of violations: falsely stating or implying in labeling and marketing that the products are safe or offer therapeutic benefits, and failing to disclose that the active ingredients "are highly addictive substances similar to opioids." Each misleading inducement and each sale is pleaded as a separate violation carrying its own penalty under Section 4-3(B). The requested relief includes the maximum statutory penalties, injunctions requiring addiction disclosures on labels and advertising, an order to stop safety and therapeutic claims, and court-ordered corrective advertising campaigns, according to the complaint's prayer for relief.

## The removal, and the argument against the city

The complaint goes out of its way to stay in state court. "The City brings this action exclusively under the City's Ordinance. No federal claims are being asserted," paragraph 16 states, adding that any claim that could be read as federal "is expressly and undeniably disavowed and disclaimed." The defendants removed it anyway. [The federal docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/74734499/mayor-and-city-council-of-baltimore-v-jopen-llc/) shows a notice of removal from the Circuit Court for Baltimore City filed September 1, 2026, opening the case as No. 1:26-cv-03463 in the District of Maryland. Neither the docket listing KNT reviewed nor Law360's report indicates whether the city has moved to remand.

The dismissal argument, as Law360 summarized it, is about who may enforce: the defendants contend that policing kratom marketing is a job reserved for state actors and that a municipality overreached by bringing the case. The report does not describe the statutory or preemption basis for that position, and KNT has not reviewed the motion itself. Baltimore's complaint anticipates a related objection by pointing to the ordinance's own penalty and injunction provisions, Sections 4-3 and 4-5(d), as the source of the city's authority.

## A second motion-to-dismiss ruling in the same arc

The Baltimore motion arrives the week after a different addiction-disclosure theory survived the pleading stage. U.S. District Judge Laura T. Swain in Manhattan [denied Mitra-9 Brands' motion to dismiss][mitra-9-brands-southern-district-new-york-swain-denies-motion-dismiss-addiction-false-advertising-suit-bursor-fisher-vasquez-adexchanger-transparency] a proposed consumer class action, finding the complaint adequately alleged the company knew its products were addictive and failed to warn, as KNT reported on October 7. That case was brought by a private plaintiff under consumer-protection law; Baltimore's is brought by a city under its own ordinance, and the defendants' challenge goes to the city's standing to enforce rather than to the sufficiency of the addiction allegations. Law360's report gives no briefing schedule or hearing date.
