A Chicago-area addiction treatment provider says concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, sold in tablets, energy shots, and gummies at gas stations and vape shops, have become the most common reason new patients walk through its doors. Brightside Recovery has seen a threefold increase over the past several months in patients coming in for 7-OH, and nine in ten patients arriving in the past month were using it, co-founder and executive vice president Phil Atteberry told [WTTW News](https://news.wttw.com/2026/10/07/addiction-recovery-clinic-sees-increase-7-oh-patients-feds-seek-crack-down-opioid-drug) reporter Eunice Alpasan in a story published October 7, 2026. The products at issue are concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations, which contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts found in natural kratom leaf; WTTW's story describes 7-OH as "a synthetic compound related to kratom" and does not address natural leaf products.

Brightside operates clinics in Northbrook, North Aurora, Olympia Fields, Rockford, and Peoria, according to WTTW and to [Becker's Behavioral Health](https://www.beckersbehavioralhealth.com/behavioral-health-addiction-treatment/illinois-addiction-clinic-reports-threefold-jump-in-7-oh-patients/), which summarized the WTTW report on October 8. The provider specializes in opioid use disorders and treated patients through the height of the fentanyl overdose crisis, WTTW reported.

## What the clinicians described

"It's very accessible, but very dangerous and as powerful as any of the opioids that we've been working on," medical director Dr. David Kushner told WTTW. Kushner said the effects of 7-OH resemble those of opioids and that patients may experience severe withdrawal symptoms when they stop abruptly, according to the station. The number of 7-OH patients at the clinic now exceeds the number of fentanyl patients, Kushner said.

Atteberry attributed the increase to people seeking help in light of the Drug Enforcement Administration's (DEA) pending move to place concentrated 7-OH products in Schedule I, which the agency [proposed in July][dea-temporary-schedule-one-high-7oh-products] and [reissued this week with a revised threshold][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14]. He also raised the concern that some people in withdrawal may turn to fentanyl or heroin instead of treatment, WTTW reported. Atteberry told the station he visited more than 50 vape shops to leave information that clerks and owners could pass to customers. "We just wanted to give them information that if someone is looking for treatment, there are options out there," he said.

## The state's figures point the other way

The Illinois Department of Public Health offered a different data point in a statement to WTTW. The agency said it was "aware of the rise of synthetic substances and other opioid agents that can cause harm when consumed," and that it "has seen a steady decrease of kratom/7-OH fatalities over the last five years." The department said it distributes naloxone and opioid test strips to schools and first responders through its Local Health Protection Grant and that "the use of naloxone and opioid reversal agents are still recommended to prevent overdose and to reduce harm." The statement did not give the fatality counts behind the five-year trend, and neither WTTW nor Becker's reported them.

The clinic's figures are its own intake numbers rather than a population estimate, and WTTW did not report how many patients the threefold increase represents. The DEA's [August order][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds] placed three lab-made compounds related to 7-OH in Schedule I, and the scheduling of concentrated 7-OH itself has not taken effect, as Becker's noted.
