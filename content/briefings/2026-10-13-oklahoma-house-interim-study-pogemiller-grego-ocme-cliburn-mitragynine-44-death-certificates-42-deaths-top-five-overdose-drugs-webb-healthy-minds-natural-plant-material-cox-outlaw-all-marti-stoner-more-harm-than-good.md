Mitragynine was listed as a contributory factor on 44 Oklahoma death certificates from 2021 to 2025, and mitragynine or another kratom alkaloid was listed as a factor in 42 deaths between January 2025 and June 2026, Dr. Kacey Cliburn, deputy chief forensic toxicologist at the Oklahoma Office of the Chief Medical Examiner, told a House interim study on Monday, October 12, 2026, according to a [release from the Oklahoma House of Representatives](https://www.okhouse.gov/posts/news-20261012_2). Cliburn said the laboratory began identifying more cases involving potent alkaloids in early 2026 and issued a public service alert in May, and that mitragynine now sits among the top five drugs listed as a cause of overdose death in the state, alongside methamphetamine, fentanyl, cocaine, and ethanol.

"Our office has never experienced a situation like this, in which an uncontrolled, over-the-counter commercial product has displaced a drug in the top five listed in the cause of death," Cliburn said, according to the release.

The release does not say whether the products involved in those deaths were concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, natural leaf kratom, or something else. Concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts naturally present in kratom leaf, and the medical examiner's figures are reported by alkaloid, not by product type. The release describes kratom as "often paired with additives such as mitragynine, an active alkaloid, and 7-hydroxymitragynine, or 7-OH." Mitragynine is the principal alkaloid of the kratom leaf itself. Cliburn and Chief Forensic Toxicologist Dr. Jesse Kemp told the committee that the products, "especially with derivatives combined with other substances," can carry a high risk of addiction and negative health consequences including death, according to the release.

## How the numbers compare with earlier Oklahoma figures

The figures presented Monday are the third set of Oklahoma medical examiner numbers to surface in recent months, and they are not directly comparable to one another. In July, [the Oklahoma Bureau of Narcotics and State Department of Health warned retailers][oklahoma-warns-retailers-liability-kratom-alkaloid-limits] about liability under the state's Kratom Consumer Protection Act, and KTUL reported at the time that the medical examiner had attributed more than 50 deaths to kratom products since January 2025. In September, [Rep. Tim Turner's announcement of a state Schedule I bill][oklahoma-rep-tim-turner-schedule-one-bill-7oh-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-ocme-deaths] cited medical examiner figures of 38 deaths from January through June 2026 involving 7-OH, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, or MGM-15 alone and 73 more involving one of those compounds combined with another substance. [KOSU reported](https://www.kosu.org/oklahoma-kratom-regulation-interim-study) on October 13 that kratom compounds were found in 58 fatal overdose toxicology reports between January 2025 and May 2026, citing the same office. Each figure covers a different window and counts differently, and none of the reporting reviewed for this briefing reconciles them.

## The study and who testified

Reps. Ellen Pogemiller, D-Oklahoma City, and Jim Grego, R-Wilburton, hosted the study before the House Alcohol, Tobacco and Controlled Substances Committee to examine the marketing and sale of kratom and THC drinks to Oklahoma children, according to the House release. Pogemiller said the goal was to "look at policy solutions this upcoming legislative session that work to protect Oklahomans from dangerous and accessible substances," and told KOSU she had been "overwhelmed by emails and calls to our office with people who have personal stories." Grego said he joined the study after hearing from Pittsburg County resident Steve Covington, whose son is in rehabilitation, and described a man with a family and a job whose addiction to a product "marketed as a natural substance that could boost his energy and help with soreness has almost ruined him."

Oklahoma Bureau of Narcotics Director Donnie Anderson was among the presenters who described kratom as marketed as a plant-based herbal supplement from a Southeast Asian tree, and numerous presenters said the products are easy to find in convenience stores and gas stations and easy for teens to buy, according to the release. Angela Goins told the committee her brother died after becoming addicted to what she described as kratom pills with dangerous additives, and said the products change too quickly for law enforcement or lawmakers to keep up. Tiffany Sprague said her daughter, Isabella Gardner, became addicted to a kratom drink marketed as a natural supplement while pursuing competitive dancing; KOSU reported that Gardner first tried the products at 18 at a smoothie shop that sold drinks from Botanic Tonics, an Oklahoma-based company that KOSU said settled an $8.75 million class-action lawsuit in 2025 after being accused of marketing some products as a healthy alternative to alcohol. Gardner and 19-year-old Braxton Bunch described addiction, detox, and recovery, and addiction counselor Frank Turner told the committee "drugs are killing our kids," according to the release.

## The recommendations

Tane Webb, a substance use policy analyst with the Healthy Minds Policy Initiative, told the committee that many consumers are unaware of undisclosed synthetic ingredients in products sold over the counter at gas stations and vape shops, and that enforcement of Oklahoma's existing Kratom Consumer Protection Act "has not kept pace with the changing landscape," according to the House release. His recommendations, as listed in the release, include raising the legal purchase age to 20 or older and prohibiting free or promotional distribution, updating state law to limit legal kratom products to natural plant material, establishing consistent standards for alkaloid limits and concentration measurement, broadening enforcement beyond kratom and expanding state product-testing capacity, and increasing education among health care providers, vendors, and the public. KOSU reported that Healthy Minds counts at least 29 states regulating kratom to some degree and ten that ban it.

A "natural plant material" limit would track the line federal regulators drew on October 9, when [the DEA adopted an HHS recommendation][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14] to schedule 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl only above 1 milligram per gram or when mitragynine falls below a 100-to-1 ratio to those compounds. The Oklahoma release does not say what alkaloid limits Webb proposed.

Michella Carter and Melissa Brous, technical managers of the Oklahoma State Bureau of Investigation's seized drug and forensic toxicology units, put the cost of kratom testing at almost $2.7 million in one-time expenses and almost $800,000 a year in recurring costs, according to the release. [News 9](https://www.news9.com/lawmakers-kratom-families-raise-alarms-thc-drinks-youth-access) and [News On 6](https://www.newson6.com/tulsa-oklahoma-news/oklahoma-lawmakers-weigh-stricter-kratom-regulations) reported the same estimate.

## Ban or regulate

The witnesses and lawmakers did not agree on the remedy. Former Rep. Doug Cox, R-Grove, told the committee the state had "been down this road" with marijuana and opioids. "If you are really interested in saving Oklahoman's lives; if you are really interested in keeping Oklahoma families from being destroyed, then outlaw all of it," Cox said, according to the release. KOSU reported that Sprague, Gardner, and other advocates consider anything short of a complete ban insufficient, and that Rep. Tim Turner, R-Kinta, has already announced plans to seek a statewide ban.

Rep. T.J. Marti, R-Broken Arrow, took the opposite view after the study. "We'd be doing more harm than good if we just said it's all banned," Marti told KOSU. "Is it a perfect drug? No. But I believe it to be safer than the alternative." Marti said banning the most natural form of kratom would push people toward unregulated products with no state oversight, and compared it to the shift toward synthetic illegal opioids after prescription limits, according to KOSU.

Judge Kenneth Stoner, who oversees Oklahoma County's treatment courts, told the committee that "neither 'natural' nor 'legal' means harmless, but acknowledging harm does not automatically establish that prohibition is the best response," according to KOSU. A ban may reduce access, Stoner said, but it can push remaining demand toward sellers who do not check identification, and criminal enforcement carries its own costs in investigations, prosecution, incarceration, and criminal records that disrupt employment, housing, and family stability. Marti and Stoner both recommended limiting which products can be sold, increasing accountability for noncompliant manufacturers and retailers, and creating testing requirements for products behind store counters, KOSU reported. The outlet said it is unclear which approach lawmakers will take next year, and that either would require new funding and legal frameworks.

The House release does not include a response from kratom trade or advocacy organizations, and KNT did not find one in the coverage reviewed for this briefing. The American Kratom Association, which supports restrictions on concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products while opposing bans on natural leaf, [is holding a Capitol press conference and congressional briefing this week][american-kratom-association-capitol-press-conference-october-13-rayburn-congressional-briefing-october-14-doj-dea-october-9-thresholds-media-mischaracterizations-ole-miss-synthetics-posing-as-kratom] on the federal threshold action. Botanic Tonics and the Global Kratom Coalition [asked Utah's attorney general last week to dismiss their challenge][botanic-tonics-global-kratom-coalition-utah-attorney-general-brown-dismiss-kratom-regulation-act-lawsuit-tenth-circuit-appeal-udaf-approved-pure-leaf-product-fox-13] to that state's Kratom Regulation Act. Michigan lawmakers are [weighing a similar ban-versus-regulate choice][michigan-poison-center-347-kratom-7oh-cases-2021-2025-87-in-2025-81-percent-vohra-science-informed-framework-not-blanket-ban-hb-5537-sb-1181-cherry-detroit-council-resolution-committee] between a House-passed ban and a Senate regulatory bill.

The study can be viewed on the Oklahoma House website under News & Media, Live Proceedings, listed for October 12 at 9:30 a.m. under the Alcohol, Tobacco and Controlled Substances Committee, according to the release.
