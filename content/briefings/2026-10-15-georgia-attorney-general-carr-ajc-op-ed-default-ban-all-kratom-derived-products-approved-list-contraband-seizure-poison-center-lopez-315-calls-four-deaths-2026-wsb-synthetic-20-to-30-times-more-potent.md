Georgia Attorney General Chris Carr is asking the Legislature to replace the state's current approach to kratom with a law that bans every kratom-derived product by default. In an [op-ed published October 15, 2026 in The Atlanta Journal-Constitution](https://www.ajc.com/opinion/2026/10/georgia-attorney-general-ban-kratom/), Carr wrote that the General Assembly should pass, and the governor should sign, "a default-ban law for all kratom-derived products, with a narrow list of approved products." Under his proposal, "anything not on that list should be treated as contraband, subject to seizure by any law enforcement agency" in the state. The column frames concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, which it describes as tablets, gummies, shots, and powders sold as "kratom," as the core problem, and calls 7-OH "an opioid-like substance that is roughly 13 times more potent than morphine." Those are manufactured preparations distinct from the trace 7-OH present in natural kratom leaf, though the column does not draw that line itself and uses "kratom-derived" and "kratom-related" throughout.

The op-ed landed one day after a [WSB-TV report](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/4-overdose-deaths-linked-kratom-223902339.html) in which Georgia Poison Center director Dr. Gaylord Lopez said the center has been flooded with calls about what the station called "Kratom-like products." "This year alone, we are already at 315 calls, and we have already had four deaths. This has been alarming," Lopez told Channel 2's Tom Regan. "And now, with this synthetic product on the market, it's like 20 to 30 times more potent than just Kratom itself." Lopez said about eight in ten calls involve a patient hospitalized for overdose, and that patients present with their nervous and respiratory systems affected. The report does not name the synthetic product, does not use the term 7-OH, and does not say which of the four deaths or 315 calls involved synthetic products as opposed to natural leaf kratom.

## What Carr says the current law cannot do

Georgia already restricts potent synthetic kratom products under its Kratom Consumer Protection Act, Rep. Rick Townsend, chairman of the House study committee on youth exposure to kratom, [said at a hearing last month][georgia-house-study-committee-kratom-hearing-university-of-georgia-athens-townsend-enforcement], where he described enforcement as the remaining question. Carr's column makes the same point from the prosecutor's side. "While the Kratom Consumer Protection Act banned synthetic 7-OH outright," he wrote, enforcing that ban requires investigators to find a product, seize it, test it, and prove its contents before anyone can act. His proposed fix inverts the burden: approved products would need independent lab testing, accurate labeling, and federal marketing authorization, and everything else would be seizable "without lab work or proof of contents," according to the column.

Carr compared the model to Georgia's earlier handling of gray-market and counterfeit-stamped cigarettes. "A number of years ago, gray-market and counterfeit-stamped cigarettes were commonly sold in Georgia convenience stores," he wrote, and after the state declared them contraband by statute they disappeared from shelves. He also cited his office's consumer alert from last year, which the column links to a November 2025 press release.

A default-ban structure would go further than the measures Georgia lawmakers have considered so far. House Bill 968, which Kratom News Today (KNT) [covered in July][georgia-south-carolina-kcpa-repeal-bills], would repeal the Kratom Consumer Protection Act and move kratom or its key alkaloids into a controlled-substance framework. Carr's op-ed does not mention that bill or any bill number, and it does not say whether natural leaf kratom could qualify for the approved list, since the list as described would require federal marketing authorization that no kratom product currently has. KNT could not determine from the column whether the attorney general's office has drafted legislation.

## The poison center numbers

Lopez's 315 calls and four deaths are 2026 figures. In March, WSB-TV [reported](https://www.wsbtv.com/news/local/atlanta/georgia-poison-center-responding-surge-kratom-related-calls/FWKV2ZA7NBAX7JHAKZDEDOLJP4) that Patrick Filkins of Emory University and the Georgia Poison Center put the center's total at "about 543 cases of kratom exposure" since 2021, with "a 66% increase between 2022 and 2025 alone" and a 400 percent increase from 2024 to 2025 in exposures among children five and under. Carr's column cites the same 543 and 66 percent figures. If Lopez's count is accurate, 2026 calls through mid-October would exceed half of the center's five-year total reported in March. KNT could not reconcile the two sets of figures from the published reports, which may use different definitions of a kratom exposure.

Carr and Lopez also differ on potency. Carr's "13 times more potent than morphine" figure, which WLHR reported he attributed to a Food and Drug Administration determination about concentrated 7-OH, [appeared in coverage of Franklin County's draft ordinance][franklin-county-georgia-commissioners-plan-vote-ban-kratom-derived-products-convenience-stores-amended-alcohol-ordinance-hart-county-sayer-regional-effort-7oh-mgm-15-mgm-16] earlier this month. Lopez's "20 to 30 times more potent than just Kratom itself" compares a synthetic product to kratom rather than to morphine, and the station does not say what product or compound he was describing.

## The Ole Miss deaths as the trigger

Both pieces point to the September deaths of two University of Mississippi students in Oxford, one of them Robert Strang, a 20-year-old from Atlanta. WSB-TV reported that Strang died of inhalation asphyxiation in an overdose involving "a retail synthetic Kratom product," and that the second student died the same day from an overdose also linked to a synthetic product. Carr wrote that "two University of Mississippi students, one of which was from Georgia, were found dead in separate incidents," and that "while full toxicology results are still pending, Georgians should take note of what investigators recovered at the scene," which he described as packaged kratom bought from a retailer.

That description is less specific than what has been reported in federal court. A [filing reported by Fox News Digital on October 12][federal-filing-toxicology-mitragynine-mgm-15-mgpi-both-ole-miss-students-probable-cause-fox-news-defense-attorney-cough-syrup-inhalation-asphyxiation-yahya-mosleh-detained-islam-mosleh-20000-bond] listed mitragynine, MGM-15, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in the toxicology of both students and tied the deaths to Smax Mango Breeze tablets, a manufactured product that the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) says does not contain standard raw kratom. MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl are lab-made compounds the DEA placed in Schedule I in August, and neither is the trace 7-OH found in leaf. Carr's column does not name the tablets or those compounds, and KNT could not determine whether "still pending" refers to a final medical examiner report that has not been released.

The op-ed also arrives as the federal government moves the other direction on scope. The DEA's threshold-based notice for 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, [published in the Federal Register on October 14][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline], would leave products below 1 milligram per gram outside Schedule I. Carr's proposal would treat all kratom-derived products as contraband by default under state law, with the approved list as the only exception. The column does not mention the DEA action.

Georgia is one of three states where officials called this week for broader restrictions after the Oxford deaths. In Mississippi, the state health officer [said he will push for Schedule III classification in 2027][mississippi-state-health-officer-edney-2027-session-schedule-iii-kratom-fda-eliminate-from-private-stores-penalties-on-sellers-hosemann-full-ban-yancey-statewide-prohibition-clarion-ledger], and in Colorado, the home state of the second student, the Castle Rock Town Council [gave final approval to a retail sales ban][castle-rock-colorado-ordinance-final-passage-october-6-retail-kratom-sales-ban-effective-november-2650-fine-364-days-brooks-dead-kid-englewood-bracken-natural-leaf-not-synthetic].
