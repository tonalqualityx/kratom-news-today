A forensic toxicology team in Horsham, Pennsylvania has published a validated method for measuring four kratom-derived alkaloids in blood, along with stability data and concentrations from more than 200 real cases, in a [paper that appeared October 15, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/jat/bkag090) as an advance article in the Journal of Analytical Toxicology. The authors are Lauren K. Eccarius, Sara E. Walton, and Barry K. Logan of the Center for Forensic Science Research and Education (CFSRE) at the Fredric Rieders Family Foundation, Donna M. Papsun of NMS Labs, and corresponding author Alex J. Krotulski of CFSRE. Logan holds appointments at both organizations. The paper is open access under a CC BY 4.0 license.

The method covers mitragynine, 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), and dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, which the authors identify by its market name, MGM-15. According to the abstract, 7-OH "is found naturally in the plant but in small amounts (<2%)," and the paper frames the semi-synthetic products at the center of current enforcement as "distinct from botanical kratom" even though they are sold "under the umbrella term 'kratom.'" That distinction between trace 7-OH in leaf and the concentrated or lab-made alkaloids in manufactured products is the same line the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) drew in its [October 14 Federal Register notice][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline], which proposes a 1 milligram per gram threshold for 7-OH and MGPI.

## What the study reports

The authors describe the analytical problem in the abstract: these alkaloids "present complex analytical and interpretive challenges due to their isomeric forms, incompatibility with certain extraction and instrumental techniques promoting analyte interconversion, instability in blood, and lack of published concentration ranges." The paper presents a quantitative high-resolution mass spectrometry method that, according to the authors, "prevents interconversion and separates isomers."

The stability finding is the one with the most direct consequence for death investigations. According to the abstract, mitragynine, MGPI, and MGM-15 "were relatively stable in blood, whereas 7OH-MG decreased quickly and by 89% over 112 days with concurrent increase in MP." In other words, the authors report that 7-OH in a stored blood sample converts over time into MGPI, so a sample tested months after collection could show less 7-OH and more MGPI than was present at the time of death.

The paper also supplies concentration ranges the authors say were previously unpublished. In a subset of 139 cases, the abstract reports mean blood concentrations of 146 nanograms per milliliter for 7-OH, with a median of 58 and a range of 0 to 5,000, and 194 nanograms per milliliter for MGPI, with a median of 120 and a range of 4.8 to 1,900. In a second subset of 17 cases, the mean MGM-15 concentration was 324 nanograms per milliliter, with a median of 170 and a range of 50 to 2,900. The abstract does not break those cases down by cause of death, product type, or whether other drugs were present, and KNT has reviewed only the abstract and metadata, not the full accepted manuscript.

The authors conclude: "Testing for kratom alkaloids is currently extremely limited and/or subject to potential inaccuracy due to interconversion, leaving adverse events and fatalities under-investigated. Continued vigilance is needed as the kratom alkaloid product landscape rapidly evolves."

## Why the pharmacology matters to the regulatory debate

The abstract states that products containing 7-OH, MGPI, and "synthetically derived" MGM-15 "exhibit greater potencies and activities at the µ-opioid receptor compared to MG and are now highly implicated in intoxications and deaths." It attributes the spread of those products to a "lack of scheduling" that "has caused unregulated markets to proliferate with gas stations, smoke shops, and online retailers selling semi-synthetic formulations."

That description tracks the compounds that have appeared in recent death investigations. A federal court filing reported earlier this month said toxicology found [mitragynine, MGM-15, and MGPI in both University of Mississippi students who died September 21][federal-filing-toxicology-mitragynine-mgm-15-mgpi-both-ole-miss-students-probable-cause-fox-news-defense-attorney-cough-syrup-inhalation-asphyxiation-yahya-mosleh-detained-islam-mosleh-20000-bond], and Utah health officials said laboratory testing found [MGPI and MGM-15 in counterfeit "cat's claw" tablets linked to four deaths][utah-four-deaths-four-months-buzzers-cats-claw-tablets-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-moss-confirmed-deaths-caused-by-these-products] in that state. The new paper does not reference either case.

The paper's description also bears on how 7-OH findings in those cases can be read. Taken together, the abstract's statements mean a toxicology result showing MGPI alongside little or no 7-OH could reflect a product that contained MGPI, a product that contained 7-OH that converted during storage, or the body's own metabolism of mitragynine, which the abstract says proceeds "to 7OH-MG and further to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl." The authors describe their method as preventing conversion during analysis; the abstract does not claim it can recover what converted in a sample beforehand. The DEA placed MGPI, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in temporary Schedule I on August 26, and its separate 7-OH and MGPI proposal remains at the notice-of-intent stage, according to the agency's Federal Register filings.

## What is not in the paper

The abstract does not estimate how many kratom-related deaths nationally have been missed or misattributed, does not assess any specific commercial product, and does not address natural leaf kratom beyond the statement that 7-OH occurs in the plant at under 2 percent. NMS Labs is a commercial forensic laboratory, and the paper's casework presumably reflects samples submitted to it; the abstract does not describe how the 200-plus cases were selected or over what period they were collected. Neither CFSRE nor NMS Labs had issued a press release on the paper in the coverage reviewed for this briefing, and no trade or advocacy organization had commented on it.
