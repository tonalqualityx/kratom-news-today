Texas has counted 111 deaths related to kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) from January through the end of August 2026, [ABC13 Houston reported](https://abc13.com/post/grieving-texas-mom-fights-ban-drug-feds-call-gas-station-heroin/19941290/) on October 16, citing Texas Department of State Health Services data that also show 114 such deaths in 2023 and 14 in 2017. The figures appear in a report by Mycah Hatfield and Sarah Rafique centered on Rae Heath, whose 19-year-old son died in April after, she told the station, moving from kratom to concentrated 7-OH products. Those products are synthetically produced tablets, drinks, and vapes containing the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts found in natural kratom leaf, a distinction the station's report draws and the state's death count does not.

## The death count and what it does and does not say

ABC13 attributed the 2017 and 2023 figures to the Department of State Health Services and said that "Texas has already counted" 111 deaths through August of this year. The station did not describe how the state classifies a death as kratom- or 7-OH-related, whether the category requires toxicology confirmation, or how many of the deaths involved other substances. Nor does the reporting separate deaths involving natural leaf kratom from deaths involving concentrated 7-OH products or the lab-made compounds MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), which the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) placed in temporary Schedule I in August. A forensic toxicology paper [published this week in the Journal of Analytical Toxicology][cfsre-nms-labs-journal-of-analytical-toxicology-method-mitragynine-7oh-mgpi-mgm-15-200-cases-7oh-fell-89-percent-112-days-blood-mgpi-rose-testing-extremely-limited-fatalities-under-investigated-krotulski] argues that testing for these alkaloids is limited and prone to interconversion errors, which bears on how any state tally should be read.

The Texas figures arrive two days after Austin police and EMS told local outlets that [neither agency has confirmed a 7-OH-linked overdose][austin-police-highly-recommends-not-consume-7oh-products-ash-fda-gas-stations-smoke-shops-ems-98-opioid-overdose-calls-september-73-nine-multiple-narcan-doses-stedman-no-confirmed-7oh-overdose-cychlorphine] in the city, even as the police department recommended residents not consume 7-OH products. A statewide count and a local agency's confirmed cases can differ for the classification reasons above, and neither ABC13 nor the Austin agencies addressed the difference.

## Jonas Heath

According to ABC13, Jonas Heath was a music major at Oklahoma State University who wanted to play in an orchestra pit. He died in April. The station reported that his autopsy found he died of "toxicity caused by 7-Hydromitragynine (7-OH), kratom and a derivative of 7-OH," and did not name the medical examiner's office or identify the derivative. Rae Heath told the station she believes her son started with kratom and progressed to 7-OH less than two months before his death. The report does not identify the products or where they were bought.

Heath launched a nonprofit, the 7-OH Truth Project, in September, according to the station. Her account joins those of other families who have [described 7-OH deaths and addiction publicly][families-describe-7oh-deaths-addiction-ahead-of-scheduling] since the federal scheduling push began in July.

## 'Jonas's Law'

ABC13 reported that Heath is talking with Texas lawmakers about a bill she calls Jonas's Law, expected to be filed for the 2027 session; lawmakers can begin filing in November. As the station described it, the bill would classify kratom extracts and synthetic 7-OH as Schedule I controlled substances under Texas law, impose felony charges on manufacturers and unauthorized sellers, add civil penalties, and require mandatory overdose reporting. The report did not name a sponsor, and no bill text has been filed. A similar Texas Senate bill in 2025 did not pass, according to the station.

The proposal as described would reach "kratom extracts" as a category, which is broader than the concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products that the DEA's pending federal action targets. Whether the bill would define extracts by alkaloid concentration, as the DEA's [October 14 notice of intent][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline] does with a 1 milligram per gram threshold, or by process, is not addressed in the report. The American Kratom Association has supported scheduling synthetic 7-OH while opposing restrictions on natural leaf, and its position on a Texas extract ban had not been reported in the coverage reviewed for this briefing.

## Texas already has a 2 percent cap

Texas law already prohibits products in which 7-OH exceeds 2 percent of alkaloid content, ABC13 noted, adding that weak enforcement has kept products above that line accessible. The station's reference point is the April lawsuit in which Attorney General Ken Paxton [sued two California-based online retailers][senators-push-full-kratom-scheduling-states-crack-down], Pure Leaf Kratom and Outcast Distribution, alleging their products contained 86 to 96 percent 7-OH, nearly 50 times the cap, according to the [attorney general's release](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-paxton-sues-kratom-retailers-selling-products-containing-nearly-fifty-times-legal). ABC13 did not report the lawsuit's current status.

The station also cited the Food and Drug Administration's warning that 7-OH may be up to 13 times more potent than morphine and the Justice Department's emergency action on 7-OH. That action is a notice of intent published October 14, and under the DEA's stated timeline a temporary scheduling order could issue no earlier than November 13.

## The Los Angeles comparison

ABC13 included a data point from California about enforcement under existing law. According to the station, Los Angeles County recorded 10 local 7-OH overdose deaths from 2025 into early 2026, and after conducting 5,000 inspections and removing products from nearly 500 locations, it reported no further 7-OH deaths, according to Dr. Brian Hurley of the county's public health department. The report did not say what authority the county used for the removals or over what period the zero-death count was measured.
