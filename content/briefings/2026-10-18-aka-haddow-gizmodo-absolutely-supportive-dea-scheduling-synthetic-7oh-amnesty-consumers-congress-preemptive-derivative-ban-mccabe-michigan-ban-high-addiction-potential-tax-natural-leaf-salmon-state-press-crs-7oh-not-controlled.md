The American Kratom Association is "absolutely supportive of banning or scheduling these synthetics," the association's senior fellow on public policy, Mac Haddow, told [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-dea-is-cracking-down-on-kratom-this-time-some-advocates-are-on-board-2000826560) in a report by Ed Cara published October 17. The synthetics in question are concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, which the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) proposes to place in temporary Schedule I above a concentration threshold, a plan the agency [published in the Federal Register on October 14][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline] after the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) [recommended the threshold approach][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14]. Natural kratom leaf contains 7-OH at roughly 2 percent of its alkaloid content, according to Gizmodo, and the products the DEA is targeting contain the compound at concentrations far above that.

Haddow's position is the one [the association laid out at its October 13 Capitol press conference][american-kratom-association-capitol-press-conference-october-13-rayburn-congressional-briefing-october-14-doj-dea-october-9-thresholds-media-mischaracterizations-ole-miss-synthetics-posing-as-kratom]. What the Gizmodo interview adds is two requests that go beyond the pending order and a competing proposal from a researcher the outlet put alongside him.

## The amnesty request and the call for a congressional standard

Haddow told Gizmodo he would like an amnesty, or a similar protection, for people who have used 7-OH or other synthetic products, so that they do not face federal prosecution once the temporary order takes effect. He argued the protection is warranted because the products were widely marketed as kratom. The outlet did not report any response from the DEA or the Justice Department to that idea.

He also said the association wants Congress to go further than the DEA can. "We think that Congress needs to enact an even tougher standard," Haddow said. "The DEA's limitation right now is that they can only identify a product that's already on the market." He said Congress should preemptively ban any product that is a derivative of the natural alkaloids enhanced or synthesized to cause harm, and he restated the association's support for the Kratom Consumer Protection Act framework of a purchase age limit, mandatory third-party testing for contaminants, and labeling of ingredients and concentrations, along with a federal law to replace state and local rules. "There are a lot of bad actors out there, and we want to clean this marketplace up," he said.

Gizmodo noted that the association sued the federal government earlier this summer over the proposed rules for mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, arguing that the government needed to clarify whether the rules could reach natural kratom. The association [treated the threshold approach as resolving that suit][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14] on October 9. Gizmodo also reported that the latest revision raises the 7-OH threshold from the one the DEA proposed in July, that the same threshold applies to MGM-15 and MGM-16, and that the scheduling is expected to take effect next month on a temporary basis.

## McCabe's alternative

The report's counterweight is Sean McCabe, director of the University of Michigan's Center for the Study of Drugs, Alcohol, Smoking and Health and lead author of a May study that Gizmodo said found roughly 5 million Americans have used kratom at least once, including more than 100,000 children ages 12 to 17, with about 53 percent of recent users reporting a past-year substance use disorder. "I have seen too many people in recovery get taken out by using these products," McCabe told the outlet, referring to both synthetic 7-OH and kratom products.

McCabe proposed a different line than the one HHS and the DEA drew. "One middle ground solution could be to ban all kratom and 7-OH products with high addiction potential," he said, paired with a tax on natural-leaf kratom products with low addiction potential to fund addiction care. "Many of the concerns and issues regarding kratom highlights the lack of funding for evidence-based substance use treatment," he said. Gizmodo reported that his co-author and wife, addiction psychiatrist Vita McCabe, has seen increasing reports of severe addiction to synthetic and natural kratom alike. McCabe also said the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved kratom as a safe or effective treatment for any condition and that other medications to treat addiction have been tested and proven.

Haddow's framing of the same question was that "the natural plant has limitations in terms of what its effects can be." That split, between advocates who accept a potency threshold and researchers who want the line drawn at addiction potential regardless of source, echoes [the division STAT documented in September][stat-gaffney-experts-agree-kratom-7oh-need-regulation-split-on-how-herring-make-it-illegal-dasgupta-threshold-volkow-mitragynine-haddow] and sits well short of [the three senators who asked the DEA on October 8 to schedule the entire plant][senators-grassley-lee-collins-letter-dea-cole-fda-diamantas-expeditiously-finalize-7oh-emergency-order-permanent-schedule-i-entire-kratom-plant-october-15-reply].

## Salmon on the record at Arizona State

The association's board chairman, Matt Salmon, who is also a special adviser to Arizona State University's Office of University Affairs, drew the same line in [The State Press](https://www.statepress.com/article/2026/10/kratom-use-awareness), the university's student newspaper, in an October 15 piece by Summer Westover. "The AKA has been on record for at least two years, calling for a total ban of the 7-OH or the synthetically manipulated mitragynine products," Salmon said. "We've been very, very clear that it is a very very dangerous product, and should not be on the market."

Haddow told the paper it was "statistically improbable" that both University of Mississippi students who died in late September were killed directly by natural kratom. [A federal filing][federal-filing-toxicology-mitragynine-mgm-15-mgpi-both-ole-miss-students-probable-cause-fox-news-defense-attorney-cough-syrup-inhalation-asphyxiation-yahya-mosleh-detained-islam-mosleh-20000-bond] reported toxicology showing mitragynine, MGM-15, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in both students. Of the sellers of such products, Haddow said, "they initially started marketing their product as kratom, and we quickly came out, denounced it."

The State Press also quoted Foster Olive, a professor at the university's John Shufeldt School of Medicine and Medical Engineering, who did not draw the natural-versus-synthetic distinction. "It is an opioid," Olive said of kratom, adding, "It's unregulated, and so therefore it's quite dangerous." He said unregulated products leave a user with no idea what they contain. Arizona permits over-the-counter kratom sales at age 18, the paper reported.

## Where the federal status stands

A Congressional Research Service legal sidebar, dated October 6 and summarized October 15 by [Legis1](https://legis1.com/news/7-oh-kratom-compound-remains-unscheduled-as-dea), states that "absent final scheduling action by DEA or Congress, however, 7-OH is not a controlled substance." According to Legis1's account, the sidebar confirms that mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 have been in temporary Schedule I since August 26, 2026, with the order running through August 26, 2028, and notes that the Controlled Substances Act generally bars judicial review of temporary scheduling orders. The sidebar predates the October 14 notice and describes the July proposal's 0.05 percent plant-material threshold, which the DEA has since withdrawn.

Legis1 reported that the sidebar lists two pending bills: the END 7-OH Act, H.R. 8000, sponsored by Rep. Gus Bilirakis, a Florida Republican, which would permanently place 7-OH in Schedule I while excluding the compound as naturally present in kratom, and the End Gas Station Heroin Act, which would schedule 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl with exceptions for naturally occurring compounds and qualifying finished products. Both remain in committee, according to Legis1.
