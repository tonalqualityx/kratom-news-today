The Arlington, Washington City Council approved an ordinance on October 19, 2026 banning the sale, advertisement, and distribution of kratom within city limits, [the Everett Herald reported](https://www.heraldnet.com/2026/10/20/arlington-bans-the-sale-and-distribution-of-kratom/). The ordinance does not ban possession. City Administrator Paul Ellis told the council it takes effect 30 days after approval, on November 18, 2026, and said he would send written notice to every retailer in the city, according to the paper. Arlington is the first city in Snohomish County to ban kratom, the Herald reported. The vote followed [a special-meeting workshop on October 12, 2026][arlington-washington-council-draft-ordinance-ban-sale-distribution-advertising-all-kratom-products-250-civil-infraction-license-revocation-october-12-workshop-october-19-vote-vanney] at which the council first discussed the draft. The Herald did not report the vote count or how individual members voted.

The ordinance is a full-category ban. The [draft the council reviewed](https://www.arlingtonwa.gov/DocumentCenter/View/17467/ATTACHMENT-B) defines "kratom product" to include any part of the Mitragyna speciosa leaf, any extract, and any synthetic alkaloid or synthetically derived compound containing mitragynine or 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), so natural leaf products fall under it alongside concentrated and synthetic ones. Under the draft, each violation is a class 1 civil infraction carrying a $250 fine, each separate sale, advertisement, or distribution counts as its own violation, and a retailer's city business license may be revoked or denied. The Herald's account of the final vote did not describe the penalties or say whether the text changed between the workshop and adoption.

## What council members said

Council Member Michele Blythe pointed to a "sharp increase" in poison control calls and cases at treatment clinics, the Herald reported. "I think we're just starting to see the level of addiction with use," she said. Council Member Yvonne Gallardo-Van Ornam said she had read every email residents sent and acknowledged that some chronic pain patients find kratom effective, according to the paper. Council Member Rob Toyer noted that many of the emails came from people outside Washington. Council Member Nathan Senff said he was troubled that kratom carries no age limit and could be purchased by children. "I was just really, really concerned for public safety," he said.

Jaime Robb, a healthy communities specialist with Snohomish County, addressed the council on addiction, overdose, withdrawal, and the risks of concentrated products, and said product packaging can appeal to young people, the Herald reported. The paper's account did not identify any public commenters by name and said only that residents had emailed the council. It also did not report whether any Arlington retailer, the American Kratom Association, or the Global Kratom Coalition addressed the council. Both advocacy groups have supported restrictions on concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products while opposing bans on natural leaf, a position neither had publicly applied to Arlington's ordinance as of this briefing.

## A state with no kratom rules, and cities filling the gap

Washington has no state kratom regulation of any kind, including no minimum purchase age, labeling requirement, or concentration limit, the Herald reported. Three bills introduced in the 2026 legislative session would have added rules such as a 21-and-older purchase age, a distribution tax, and licensing fees; none passed, according to the paper. Packaging often carries a "21+" label, but no state rule requires it.

Cities have moved on their own. The Herald listed Spokane, Spokane Valley, Bellingham, Cle Elum, Othello, and Mount Vernon as Washington cities that have banned kratom. KNT covered [Bellingham's 7 to 0 vote][bellingham-washington-city-council-approves-kratom-sales-ban-7-0-gross-misdemeanor] in September, which made violations a gross misdemeanor, and [Mount Vernon's ban][mount-vernon-city-council-bans-kratom-sales] in August. Arlington's $250 civil infraction is a lighter penalty model than Bellingham's.

The Herald's background section described the federal government as moving to classify kratom's compounds as Schedule I drugs. The current federal proposal is narrower than that description. The DEA and the Department of Health and Human Services [proposed on October 9, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14] to control 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl only above a threshold of 1 milligram per gram, a line drawn to leave the trace 7-OH in natural leaf outside Schedule I, KNT reported. Arlington's ordinance makes no such distinction. The products behind the Washington Poison Center's [October 9, 2026 safety alert][washington-poison-center-safety-alert-fake-cats-claw-buzzers-homiez-7oh-mgm-15-mgpi-spokane-stores-utah-four-deaths-tri-city-herald], tablets sold as "cat's claw" in Spokane-area stores that contained the synthetic compounds MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, are manufactured products distinct from natural leaf, and Arlington's ordinance treats both categories the same.

## Quincy weighs a ban against regulation

Across the Cascades in Grant County, the Quincy City Council was scheduled to tell staff on October 20, 2026 whether to draft a ban ordinance or a regulatory framework, [the Columbia Basin Herald reported](https://columbiabasinherald.com/news/2026/oct/19/quincy-council-to-weigh-kratom-ban-regulations-after-divided-public-input/) on October 19. The question reached the council after a Grant County Health District presentation recommended that the city consider regulation or a ban, which KNT [covered in September][quincy-washington-council-sets-october-hearing-kratom-ban-grant-county-health-district]. The October 6 public hearing drew nine written comments, the paper reported: six favored regulation, two supported a ban, and one opposed a ban. One person testified in person, and no ordinance was before the council.

The paper described a council divided on approach rather than on whether to act. Council Member Josie Ferguson said, "I would be in favor of regulation as opposed to banning it." Council Member Jeff Spence said adults 21 and older can decide what they put in their bodies and that the city's role is protecting children, according to the paper. Council Member Dave Dormier asked how the city would distinguish traditional kratom from synthetic or concentrated products and noted that residents could still buy kratom online. Police Chief Ryan Green said the department lacks the training and expertise to identify those products. "Currently there's no formal training on any of that," he said. Rodney Schwint, a Quincy resident, told the council that kratom had improved his quality of life after chronic pain and that he supported restricting concentrated 7-OH products but opposed a blanket ban: "If you guys are going to ban it, I think the only people that you would hurt are the people that it helps."

Warden, Ephrata, George, Mattawa, Royal City, and unincorporated Grant County have full bans, and Moses Lake restricts sales to buyers 21 and older, the Columbia Basin Herald reported. City Administrator Joe Harris told the paper that any ordinance the council adopts would need legal review and additional public steps before taking effect. The outcome of the October 20 meeting had not been reported as of this briefing.
