The Louisiana Department of Health has added three compounds related to 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and mitragynine to Schedule I of the state's controlled dangerous substances list by emergency rule, [the department announced](https://ldh.la.gov/news/7818) on October 19, 2026. The [rule text](https://www.ldh.la.gov/assets/oph/Rulemaking/er/LAC-46-LIII.Added-Controlled-Dangerous-Substance.ER---2026-09.pdf), signed by Secretary Bruce D. Greenstein, names mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16. Those are the same three compounds the Drug Enforcement Administration [placed in temporary federal Schedule I on August 26, 2026][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds], when the agency described them as 7-OH-related substances manufactured and concentrated into opioid products and said its order was not a ban on unaltered natural kratom leaf. In Louisiana that distinction has less practical weight than elsewhere: natural leaf kratom, mitragynine, and 7-OH have all been illegal in the state since Act 41 of 2025 took effect on August 1, 2025, [Red River Radio reported](https://www.redriverradio.org/news/2025-08-04/louisiana-becomes-7th-state-to-ban-kratom-with-its-powerful-psychoactive-compound) at the time.

## What the emergency rule says

The declaration of emergency, filed as LAC 46:LIII.2704 under the pharmacists' chapter of Title 46, invokes the secretary's rulemaking authority under R.S. 40:962(C) and (H) and the state Administrative Procedure Act. Greenstein wrote that he had considered the criteria in R.S. 40:963 and determined that the three substances "have a high potential for abuse," have "no currently accepted medical use for treatment in the United States," and lack "accepted safety for use of the substances under medical supervision," according to the text. The rule says the emergency designation rests on the factors in R.S. 40:962(C)(4), (5), and (6), and that it "shall remain in effect for the maximum period allowed under the Act or until adoption of a final Rule, whichever occurs first." Neither the rule nor the press release gives a calendar effective date. The rule's historical note carries an incomplete Louisiana Register citation, "LR 52:", with no page number, and the department's posting does not give a Register publication date.

The listing is broad. It covers the three named compounds along with "any possible isomers, esters, ethers, salts, and salts of isomers, esters, and ethers thereof, and any material, compound, mixture, or preparation which contains any quantity thereof," unless the substance is "specifically excepted or contained within a pharmaceutical product approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration." A separate subsection states that because the nomenclature for these structures "is not internationally standardized," compounds of the listed structures are covered "regardless of numerical designation of atomic positions." The rule identifies MGM-15 as dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine and MGM-16 as 9-fluoro-dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, or 10-fluoro-dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine "depending on numbering convention."

The department's release says preclinical evidence "indicates the substances are potent mu-opioid receptor agonists that carry risks associated with other opioid agonists, including dependence and respiratory depression." "Louisiana is taking action to protect people from substances that pose a serious risk to public health," Greenstein said in the statement. "These substances have a high potential for abuse and can cause dangerous effects, including dependence and respiratory depression. By adding them to Schedule I, we are taking the necessary steps to protect Louisiana families and communities." The release does not cite any Louisiana poison center data, overdose report, or product seizure, and it does not say whether any of the three compounds has been found in products sold in the state.

## Why a state that already banned kratom is scheduling more compounds

Louisiana's Act 41, Senate Bill 154 by Senator Jay Morris of West Monroe, made it illegal to sell, possess, or distribute kratom products as of August 1, 2025, with fines of $1,000 to $5,000 per offense and prison terms of up to five years depending on the quantity and severity, Red River Radio reported, citing an Office of Alcohol and Tobacco Control advisory. [KATC's report](https://www.acadiananewsfirst.com/2026/10/19/ldh-adds-kratom-substances-illegal-drugs-list/) on the emergency rule noted that kratom has been illegal in the state since 2025. Neither the rule nor the release explains why the three compounds needed separate listing, and the rule text does not reference Act 41. Red River Radio's account of Act 41 described it as scheduling kratom, mitragynine, and 7-OH; the emergency rule lists three compounds with different chemical structures, which the DEA's August order treated as a class separate from the two leaf alkaloids. Neither Louisiana document says whether the earlier law reached those compounds, and KNT could not confirm the department's reasoning from the available records.

In other states, these compounds have turned up in manufactured tablets. Utah's medical examiner [attributed four deaths in four months][utah-four-deaths-four-months-buzzers-cats-claw-tablets-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-moss-confirmed-deaths-caused-by-these-products] to products sold as "Buzzers" and "cat's claw" that contained mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15, KNT reported on October 6, 2026, and the Washington Poison Center [issued a safety alert on October 9, 2026][washington-poison-center-safety-alert-fake-cats-claw-buzzers-homiez-7oh-mgm-15-mgpi-spokane-stores-utah-four-deaths-tri-city-herald] about similar tablets found in Spokane-area stores. The Louisiana release does not mention those products or any comparable finding in Louisiana.

## How local coverage described the compounds

The three Louisiana outlets that covered the rule framed the compounds differently. [WBRZ](https://www.wbrz.com/news/louisiana-department-of-health-issues-emergency-rule-adding-three-substances-commonly-found-in-kratom/) headlined them as "substances commonly found in kratom," attributing that characterization to the FDA. KATC wrote that, according to the National Institutes of Health, the substances "are also known as kratom." [WDSU](https://www.wdsu.com/article/louisiana-ldh-schedule-i-kratom-substances/74195214) described them as "substances found in kratom compounds." None of the three named the compounds, and none distinguished them from the leaf alkaloids. The rule's own text describes mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 by chemical structure, and the DEA's August order, which KNT covered, characterized them as 7-OH-related substances manufactured and concentrated into opioid products rather than as constituents of natural leaf. The department's release splits the difference, calling them "substances related to 7-hydroxymitragynine and mitragynine."

## The federal track

Louisiana's rule arrives while the federal status of the broader 7-OH category is in motion. The DEA's August 26 temporary order covering the same three compounds runs through August 26, 2028. On October 14, 2026 the agency [published two Federal Register notices][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline], one proposing to control 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above a concentration threshold of 1 milligram per gram and the other addressing MGM-15 and MGM-16, with the earliest possible final order on November 13, 2026, KNT reported. The American Kratom Association's Mac Haddow [told Gizmodo on October 18, 2026][aka-haddow-gizmodo-absolutely-supportive-dea-scheduling-synthetic-7oh-amnesty-consumers-congress-preemptive-derivative-ban-mccabe-michigan-ban-high-addiction-potential-tax-natural-leaf-salmon-state-press-crs-7oh-not-controlled] that the association is "absolutely supportive" of scheduling synthetic 7-OH products while opposing bans on natural leaf, a position the Louisiana rule does not implicate directly because leaf is already prohibited there. Stateline [reported on October 5, 2026][stateline-kratom-7oh-laws-diverge-36-states-rockefeller-14-ban-13-potency-limits-lowe-gkc-supports-feds-haddow-overblown-smith-hart], citing a Rockefeller Institute of Government count, that 14 states ban or schedule kratom outright. Neither the department nor the three outlets reported comment from the association, the Global Kratom Coalition, or any Louisiana retailer.
