The Missoula City-County Health Department will prohibit the sale of kratom food and drink products in licensed retail food businesses and will enforce the prohibition during routine inspections after November 2, 2026, [Montana Free Press reported](https://montanafreepress.org/2026/10/19/missoula-county-to-prohibit-sale-of-kratom-in-retail-food-businesses/) on October 19, 2026. The department is applying existing food-safety requirements rather than adopting a new ordinance. Its September 25, 2026 letter to retail food establishments states that no kratom food or drink products are approved for sale in the United States, according to the paper, which reported that the Food and Drug Administration has not approved any kratom product as a drug and has deemed kratom an unsafe food additive. The covered products include teas, energy shots, seltzers, gummies, tablets, and powders, Montana Free Press reported. The letter and the coverage do not distinguish natural leaf kratom from concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products; the prohibition reaches both.

## What inspectors found

Health department staff visited 23 retail food businesses in Missoula County in late August and found 12 of them selling kratom products, Environmental Health Director Shannon Therriault told county commissioners on October 19, 2026, according to Montana Free Press. The businesses included convenience stores, casinos, a small grocery store, and an herb store. Therriault said the products are often marketed as mood stabilizers or pain relievers, the paper reported. She cited 29 Montana overdose deaths from 2020 to 2025 that involved kratom and described kratom as unregulated and potentially addictive. The paper did not report whether those 29 deaths involved natural leaf, concentrated extracts, or synthetic 7-OH products, or whether other substances were present. Jeanna Miller, Missoula's chief health officer, called kratom "still a community health concern," according to the paper.

Retailer reaction has been limited. A couple of businesses asked questions after receiving the letter, and there has been little pushback, Therriault said, according to Montana Free Press. Some retailers did complain of unfair enforcement, the paper reported, because dispensaries, vape shops, and other businesses that do not hold retail food licenses are not subject to the same restriction. Businesses have until November 2 to sell down or remove their stock, [NonStop Local reported](https://www.montanarightnow.com/missoula/montana-mother-pushes-for-wider-kratom-limits-after-son-s-death/article_00bc6b72-e6e1-4cfa-aa1a-b210cff1961f.html).

## Yellowstone County went first

Therriault said the department had been discussing kratom for some time and began looking at local availability after Yellowstone County acted, Montana Free Press reported. RiverStone Health, the Yellowstone County health department, began prohibiting kratom sales in retail food establishments in January 2026 and in May sued four businesses to stop them from selling it, according to the paper. The Blackfeet Tribal Business Council banned the sale, use, and possession of kratom on the Blackfeet reservation in September 2025, with criminal penalties for retailers, the paper reported.

The Montana Legislature passed no kratom regulation in its 2025 session and is expected to take the issue up again in 2027, Montana Free Press reported. KNT covered the interim committee stage of that debate in September, when the Law and Justice Interim Committee [voted 8 to 1 to table a Kratom Consumer Protection Act][montana-interim-committee-tables-kratom-consumer-protection-act-8-1] that would have banned synthesized kratom and set a purchase age of 18, with several members saying the bill was not strong enough. Therriault told Montana Free Press she hopes lawmakers will ban kratom rather than legalize and regulate its sale. That preference differs from the position the American Kratom Association and the Global Kratom Coalition have taken in other states, where both groups have backed regulatory frameworks and restrictions on synthetic 7-OH while opposing bans on natural leaf. Neither organization was quoted in the Montana coverage.

The Missoula approach also differs from where federal regulators have landed. The Drug Enforcement Administration and the Department of Health and Human Services [proposed on October 9, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14] to control 7-OH only above 1 milligram per gram, a threshold intended to leave natural leaf outside Schedule I, KNT reported. Missoula's food-code enforcement draws no line between leaf and concentrate.

## A Helena mother asks every county to follow

Kelli McCann of Helena told NonStop Local that her son Benjamin died of acute mitragynine intoxication after deputies found him dead on his couch during a welfare check on September 13, 2024, when he had not shown up for work. The station's report attributed the cause of death to McCann and did not name a medical examiner or coroner. McCann said her son had used kratom for a long time, starting it for psoriatic arthritis pain, and that kratom bags were on the counter when he was found, according to the station. A police report said there was no indication he had abused the substance as a synthetic narcotic, NonStop Local reported. The report does not say what form of kratom he used, whether natural leaf, extract, or a concentrated 7-OH product, a distinction the coverage leaves open.

"I had no idea that it was connected to the opioid receptors," McCann told the station. She said she wants kratom-related products banned until clinical studies and safety profiles are available, and she pointed to Missoula's enforcement as the model. "I think all the counties should do that," she said. No state or county legislation was named in the station's report, which described only the existing food-code requirements Missoula is enforcing.
