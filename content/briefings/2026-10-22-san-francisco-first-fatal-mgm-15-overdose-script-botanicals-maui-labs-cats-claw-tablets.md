San Francisco has recorded its first known fatal overdose involving MGM-15, a semi-synthetic kratom-derived opioid sold in concentrated tablet form and distinct from natural leaf kratom, according to a [health alert](https://www.sf.gov/news-fatal-overdose-involving-kratom-derived-opioid) the San Francisco Department of Public Health (SFDPH) issued on October 21, 2026. The Office of the Chief Medical Examiner detected the compound, also called dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, in an adult who died in late September, and forensic toxicology confirmed it in both the decedent and the packaging of the product the person had consumed, the alert said. SFDPH identified the product as a "Script botanicals x Maui labs" brand and said its packaging made no reference to kratom or 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH). The department described MGM-15 as a mu-opioid receptor agonist with no approved medical use and said that taking too much can cause a fatal opioid overdose. The victim's age, sex, and other identifying details were not released, [the San Francisco Standard reported](https://sfstandard.com/2026/10/21/sf-records-first-overdose-death-synthetic-opioid-sold-gas-stations/).

The alert positions the San Francisco case within a pattern this publication has tracked since late summer. SFDPH said MGM-15 had previously been identified in overdoses in Utah and Washington, where it turned up in tablets marketed as "uncaria tomentosa" or "cat's claw" and sold in smoke shops and gas stations. Utah's medical examiner [confirmed four deaths in four months][utah-four-deaths-four-months-buzzers-cats-claw-tablets-mitragynine-pseudoindoxyl-mgm-15-moss-confirmed-deaths-caused-by-these-products] tied to "Buzzers" cat's claw tablets, and the Washington Poison Center [warned about fake cat's claw products][washington-poison-center-safety-alert-fake-cats-claw-buzzers-homiez-7oh-mgm-15-mgpi-spokane-stores-utah-four-deaths-tri-city-herald] found in Spokane stores. The Tennessee Bureau of Investigation [flagged the same labeling pattern in September][tennessee-tbi-warns-cats-claw-tablets-contain-mgm-15-kratom-18-lab-submissions]. SFDPH said the San Francisco product differs from the Utah and Washington products.

## What the product was and where it came from

The product tied to the death was sold as a pack of four 100-milligram tablets priced at about $20, according to a photograph health officials released and [SFGATE's description](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/sf-reports-first-death-tied-003354650.html) of it. The San Francisco Standard's photo caption identified the product as "Maui Wowi Paradise Blend Berry Punch," though the paper's body text used the brand name SFDPH gave. Neither outlet reported where the decedent bought it. SFGATE reported that Script Botanicals did not respond to its questions and that Maui Labs did not respond before publication. The paper also reported that the same product still appeared in a listing on the website of Green Dragon CBD, a Missouri-based retailer; KNT noted the listing only as reported and did not independently confirm it.

Alex Krotulski, director of the Center for Forensic Science Research and Education, a federally funded toxicology laboratory, told the Standard that he knows of more than two dozen MGM-15 deaths nationwide, including in California, Nevada, Michigan, and Florida. "The big concern (with these products) is the high potency and the problematic product labeling," Krotulski said. "People have no idea if they're consuming it or how much of it they're consuming." Krotulski's lab published a validated testing method for mitragynine, 7-OH, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, and MGM-15 in the Journal of Analytical Toxicology earlier this month, which KNT [covered on October 17][cfsre-nms-labs-journal-of-analytical-toxicology-method-mitragynine-7oh-mgpi-mgm-15-200-cases-7oh-fell-89-percent-112-days-blood-mgpi-rose-testing-extremely-limited-fatalities-under-investigated-krotulski]; in that paper the authors warned that testing for these compounds remains limited and that related fatalities are likely under-investigated.

## The legal status of the three compounds

The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) placed MGM-15 in Schedule I in late August, the Standard reported, as part of a temporary order covering three synthetic kratom-derived compounds that KNT [reported at the time][dea-finalizes-schedule-one-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds]. SFDPH's alert said the scheduling makes the compound illegal to sell. [SFist noted](https://sfist.com/2026/10/21/sf-reports-first-fatal-overdose-from-kratom-derived-opioid-as-sfpd-raids-smoke-shops/) that kratom and 7-OH are not currently scheduled under federal law, while specific kratom-derived substances including MGM-15 are controlled. The DEA's separate notice proposing to schedule 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above concentration thresholds, [published in the Federal Register on October 14][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline], allows an order on or after November 13, Kratom News Today (KNT) reported.

California's position is different. SFGATE reported that health officials said kratom and 7-OH are "illegal to sell" in California, and the Standard reported that the California Department of Public Health banned kratom and 7-OH products late last year. KNT [covered the state's enforcement campaign in September][california-statewide-enforcement-removes-kratom-7oh-products]. The Standard reported that despite that determination, the products remain widely sold in San Francisco and Oakland. The state health department told SFGATE that kratom products can often appear "healthy" or look like candies or treats.

## SFPD's smoke shop searches

The San Francisco Police Department announced on October 21 that officers, working with the City Attorney's Office, had served search warrants at two smoke shops, SFist reported. On September 10, at a shop on the 1400 block of Haight Street, officers seized items suspected of containing psilocybin mushrooms, kratom, and 7-OH, along with flavored vapes, nitrous oxide canisters, and glass pipes, according to SFist. On October 9, at a shop on the 4600 block of Geary Boulevard that lacked a license to sell tobacco, officers seized more than 200 flavored vapes and more than 400 kratom products, plus tobacco, pipes, suspected psilocybin products, and other evidence, the outlet reported. SFist reported no arrests and no shop names. SFPD said products it seized were often presented as "herbal supplements" or carried "misleading labels," according to SFGATE. None of the coverage said whether either search was connected to the MGM-15 death, and the Standard said explicitly that it did not know.

The SFPD release described kratom products as having stimulant-like effects and 7-OH as producing opioid-like effects, SFist reported. Neither the police nor SFDPH said whether the "kratom products" seized on Geary were natural leaf, concentrated extracts, or 7-OH tablets. The American Kratom Association, whose senior fellow Mac Haddow [told Gizmodo this month][aka-haddow-gizmodo-absolutely-supportive-dea-scheduling-synthetic-7oh-amnesty-consumers-congress-preemptive-derivative-ban-mccabe-michigan-ban-high-addiction-potential-tax-natural-leaf-salmon-state-press-crs-7oh-not-controlled] the group is "absolutely supportive" of scheduling synthetic kratom derivatives while opposing restrictions on natural leaf, was not quoted in any of the San Francisco coverage.

## What SFDPH told clinicians

SFDPH urged clinicians to ask patients about over-the-counter supplement use and to counsel them on risks, and it advised distributing naloxone to anyone at risk, the Standard reported. The department said it has no reason to believe timely naloxone administration cannot reverse MGM-15 intoxication, according to the alert, though the Standard noted the department's caveat that naloxone may not fully restore consciousness in complex synthetic overdoses even when it restores breathing. The alert recommended naloxone and a 911 call for anyone who appears to be overdosing.

The alert followed an earlier SFDPH warning this year about cychlorphine, a synthetic non-fentanyl opioid that the department said caused the April 2026 death of a 16-year-old San Francisco resident who took it in a counterfeit pill. SFDPH said no further cychlorphine deaths had been detected. The Standard reported that police arrested four people in September and seized more than 20,000 counterfeit pills in connection with that case.
