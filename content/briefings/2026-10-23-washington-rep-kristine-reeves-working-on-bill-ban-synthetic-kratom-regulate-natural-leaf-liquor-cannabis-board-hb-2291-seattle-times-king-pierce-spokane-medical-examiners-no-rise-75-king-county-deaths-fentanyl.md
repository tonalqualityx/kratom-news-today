State Rep. Kristine Reeves, a Democrat from Federal Way, told [the Seattle Times](https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/politics/as-kratom-proliferates-in-legal-gray-area-wa-lawmakers-eye-drug-ban/) in a report published October 22, 2026 that she is working on a bill that would ban synthetic kratom and regulate the naturally occurring product. The Times reported that lawmakers will look at regulating kratom and its synthetic versions after several cities in the state moved to ban sales. The warnings that preceded the city bans concerned concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products rather than natural leaf, according to the Washington Poison Center bulletin the Times quoted, which said "Cat's Claw" tablets sold in the state were "laced with highly concentrated chemicals related to kratom" and are "strong opioids." [Kratom News Today's (KNT) coverage of that bulletin][washington-poison-center-safety-alert-fake-cats-claw-buzzers-homiez-7oh-mgm-15-mgpi-spokane-stores-utah-four-deaths-tri-city-herald] identified the compounds the center named as 7-OH, MGM-15, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl. The Times described kratom leaf as containing two psychoactive ingredients, mitragynine and 7-OH, and the death data it reported concerned mitragynine; the paper did not say how many of those cases involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products rather than leaf.

Reeves, who has middle-school-age children, told the paper she first heard about the products from other parents. The vehicle for her effort is House Bill 2291, which the Times reported would place kratom regulation under the authority of the state Liquor and Cannabis Board (LCB). "LCB is going to be coming by anyway to check your tobacco and your alcohol," Reeves told the Times, referring to the gas stations and convenience stores where the products are sold. According to the [Legislature's bill record](https://app.leg.wa.gov/billsummary?BillNumber=2291&Year=2025&Initiative=false), HB 2291, titled the Kratom Consumer Protection Act, was prefiled on January 7, 2026 with Reeves as prime sponsor and eight co-sponsors, received a public hearing in the House Consumer Protection and Business Committee on January 30, and shows no action since. KNT [reported in May][washington-local-governments-fill-kratom-regulatory-vacuum] that the Legislature considered the bill but did not pass it in the 2026 session. Reeves told the Times that one obstacle to a state law is opposition to broad government regulation. "I think it's just the general jurisdiction concern that the state's coming in and telling a community what they can and can't do," she said. The Times did not report when a new version would be introduced or how it would define synthetic kratom.

The compounds the poison center named are the same ones the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) addressed in its [October 14 Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/citation/91-FR-65370), [covered by KNT][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline], which proposed controlling 7-OH above concentration thresholds, an approach the Department of Health and Human Services said targets concentrated 7-OH "without sweeping natural botanical kratom into Schedule I."

## Five cities have moved to ban kratom sales, the Times reports

The Times reported that Mount Vernon, Bellingham, Spokane, and Spokane Valley have already banned kratom, and that Arlington's city leaders approved a sales ban on October 19, giving retailers 30 days to clear the products from their shelves. The Arlington ordinance's text defines kratom product to include natural leaf as well as extracts and synthetic alkaloids, according to the Everett Herald's account of the ordinance, [as KNT reported on October 21][arlington-washington-council-approves-ordinance-banning-sale-distribution-advertising-all-kratom-products-effective-november-18-first-snohomish-county-city-blythe-poison-control-ellis-retailer-notice-quincy-weighs-ban-or-regulation]. The Times described the other four cities' measures only as bans on kratom.

Arlington Mayor Don Vanney told the Times that prohibiting the products from being sold or advertised in the city can help prevent addiction and protect young people who he said can currently buy kratom with no restrictions at gas stations and convenience stores, and he described the ban as one more tool to protect youth. Vanney told the paper that most of the emails the city has received since it began considering the ban came from people in the Midwest and on the East Coast, which he said raised the question of whether the senders were investors or distributors of the products. According to the Times, Arlington Police Commander Peter Barrett said at an October 12 council meeting that police support the ban but that enforcing it with staffing limitations could be a challenge. The ordinance does not ban possession, the Times reported. Vanney told the paper he has spoken with other mayors in north Snohomish County who are interested in similar policies and that he hopes state or national restrictions will also be considered.

## What the county medical examiners say

The Times asked the medical examiner's offices for King, Pierce, and Spokane counties about deaths, and all three said they have not documented a rise in deaths related to kratom. The Times noted that the data is limited because not all deaths are tested and because testing for synthetic varieties has only recently become available. "The synthetic versions of Kratom are especially dangerous and require specialized testing, which did become available this past spring," the Pierce County medical examiner's office said in a statement quoted by the Times.

In King County, all 75 overdose deaths involving mitragynine since 2019 have included at least one other substance, most often fentanyl, Sharon Bogan, a spokesperson for Public Health - Seattle & King County, told the Times.

## Poison center official says label ingredients cannot be verified

Jimmy Leonard, chief clinical officer at the Washington Poison Center, told the Times that because herbal supplements are not regulated, there is no way to know whether the ingredients listed on a package are what is in the product. He pointed to a study published this year in Clinical Toxicology by medical officials in Alabama who tested products after people experienced opioid-like withdrawal symptoms from herbal supplements that should not have caused them, the Times reported. According to the Times, the study found five different products contained kratom-derived ingredients despite none listing kratom on the label. Leonard called the practice "absolutely illegal." "You can't label a bag of sugar as salt and sell it as salt," he said.

The Times reported that the Washington Poison Center's bulletin on the "Cat's Claw" products came a day after the Utah governor's office issued a public health warning linking four deaths in that state to "semi-synthetic kratom chemicals."

## The national numbers the Times cited

The Times reported that a paper the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention released in March showed a 1,200 percent increase in kratom-related reports to the National Poison Data System between 2015 and 2025, and that hospitalizations among people who used only kratom, rather than mixing it with other substances, also rose 1,200 percent in that period. The Times also cited a 2021 American Kratom Association policy brief estimating that between 11 million and 15 million Americans regularly use kratom.

On the industry's position, the Times reported, citing the New York Times, that the Trump administration moved to crack down on some synthetic versions of kratom this summer and that natural kratom companies and their lobby, which the paper said have lost market share to the synthetics, backed the move. American Kratom Association lobbyist Mac Haddow said this month, [as KNT reported][aka-haddow-gizmodo-absolutely-supportive-dea-scheduling-synthetic-7oh-amnesty-consumers-congress-preemptive-derivative-ban-mccabe-michigan-ban-high-addiction-potential-tax-natural-leaf-salmon-state-press-crs-7oh-not-controlled], that the group is "absolutely supportive" of DEA scheduling of synthetic 7-OH while it opposes bans on natural leaf. The Times quoted a DEA description of kratom as producing a stimulant effect at low doses and acting as a sedative at higher doses, and it reported the Mayo Clinic's position that kratom may be easier to obtain than prescription medicines but carries its own risk of addiction.

In Illinois this week, [Shaw Local reported](https://www.shawlocal.com/illinois-valley/2026/10/22/streator-joins-other-la-salle-county-communities-in-banning-synthetic-kratom/) that [Streator's City Council approved an ordinance banning synthetic kratom while allowing natural leaf sales to adults over 21][streator-illinois-council-approves-synthetic-kratom-ban-natural-leaf-over-21-concentration-limits-certificate-of-analysis-mclaughlin-bedei-effingham-county-unanimous-all-kratom-phenibut-7oh-ban-fines-250-750], and [WCIA reported](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/effingham-county-bans-possession-sale-224808612.html) that Effingham County's board approved a countywide ban on all kratom products.
