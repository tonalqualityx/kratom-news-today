The Trump administration reversed a planned full ban on mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI) after a lobbying push by the kratom industry, [The New York Times reported](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/28/us/politics/trump-opioid-kratom.html) on October 28, 2026. The Times described MGPI as a semi-synthetic opioid derived from the kratom leaf, distinct from natural leaf kratom, and reported that unspecified lab tests found the compound to be far more potent than morphine; the Times did not name the labs or studies. The paper said scientists are still exploring how it emerges in otherwise natural products. According to the Times, federal health officials announced three months ago that they planned to ban the compound, and Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced on October 9, 2026 that his department had "followed the science" and decided to allow some levels of it. The thresholds, which [Kratom News Today reported on October 10, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14], would place 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and MGPI in Schedule I only above 1 milligram per gram in solids, 1 milligram per milliliter in liquids, or when the ratio of mitragynine to those two compounds falls below 100 to 1, according to the Justice Department's announcement at the time.

## What the Times says the thresholds allow

The new policy still bans the most concentrated products, according to the Times, but the allowable amounts are 25 to 500 times the levels currently found on the market, the senior author of a study cited by the administration told the paper. The Times identified the study's author as Amar Chittiboyina, a natural products chemist at the University of Mississippi, and reported that a 10-page HHS memo to the Drug Enforcement Administration cited his work. Chittiboyina told the Times his team measured MGPI in eight liquid products currently on the market and found it at about two parts per million, and that the HHS threshold was as much as 500 times as high for those products and "too permissive." The Times reported that the Federal Register notice on the proposal acknowledged that "neither H.H.S. nor D.E.A. has made a finding that these substances are safe to use" at the levels the policy allows.

DEA data cited by the Times identified MGPI in at least 56 overdose cases, 48 of them fatal, from February 2025 to May 2026, even though the compound has only been recognized since 2024, according to the report. Dr. Andrew Kolodny, medical director of the Opioid Policy Research Collaborative at Brandeis University, reviewed the HHS justification and told the Times the decision reflects the industry and its lobbyists writing the policy rather than the science supporting it.

## The donations and the White House visit

The Times reported that David Gabbay, the manager of a large kratom distribution company associated with a network of companies selling the O.P.M.S. brand, and his wife Maya had been giving steadily to Republican candidates and campaign committees this year, reaching about $2.9 million on October 12, 2026. According to the Times, the federal position on MGPI began to shift on August 25, 2026. That day the Gabbays donated nearly $160,000 to the Republican National Committee, bringing their total for the year to $2.2 million, the paper reported. The same day, a Justice Department release announced the emergency temporary scheduling of MGPI and two other compounds while saying it would not press charges over "incidental trace amounts," according to the Times. The American Kratom Association, which the Times described as funded by companies related to Gabbay and others, sued the DEA the next day, as [Kratom News Today reported on August 27, 2026][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order]. Two companies that license O.P.M.S. products, New Hope Botanicals and Martian Sales, filed similar suits in California and Wyoming that also acknowledged MGPI was in their products, according to the Times.

Mac Haddow, the association's spokesman, told the Times he went to the White House to plead his case, and the paper quoted a September podcast appearance in which he said the White House and the DEA "came to us and said, 'Look, we think we can resolve this problem,'" adding, "What they've assured us is that we'll be happy with the outcome." Haddow told the Times he was "very satisfied" with the proposed order, which the paper said is expected to be finalized in the coming weeks. He dismissed the idea that Gabbay's contributions were a major factor, saying others in the industry donated and met with officials and that everyone was playing on the same field, according to the Times. The paper also noted its earlier investigation into Jerry W. Ross, whose company makes the Feel Free drink. [Kratom News Today covered that investigation on October 11, 2026][new-york-post-kratom-industry-lobbying-aka-1-million-opensecrets-botanic-tonics-ross-1-1-million-kennedy-pacs-rnc-stop-gas-station-heroin-checkmate-750000-steck-haddow-seat-at-the-table] alongside a New York Post report on industry spending.

HHS spokeswoman Emily Hilliard told the Times the decision was based on science and that "neither litigation nor political contributions influenced H.H.S.' recommendation." White House spokeswoman Allison Schuster said in a statement that "the administration is taking decisive action to get dangerous synthetic and concentrated products off the shelf to protect Americans" and that "any assertion that they may be based in anything other than scientific evidence is completely false," according to the Times. The DEA and the Justice Department referred questions to HHS, the paper reported. The Gabbays did not respond to requests for comment, and Gwendolyn Payton, a lawyer for the company Gabbay manages, told the Times the couple donates because they advocate for kratom safety and for combating antisemitism, and that MGPI appears in natural products in "very, very minor amounts."

## How the lab results surfaced in wrongful death cases

According to the Times, the lobbying push followed court findings that products at the center of several wrongful death lawsuits contained MGPI. The paper reported that in mid-July, Talis Abolins, a Washington lawyer representing families suing Gabbay and others, learned that a University of California, San Francisco lab had found MGPI in a Maeng Da powder found near Dustin Hernandez, a 29-year-old who died after a seizure in 2021 during a night shift near Dallas. Abolins, who represents families suing Gabbay and others, alleges that the products contributed to the deaths in his cases, the Times reported. In a deposition, Marilyn Huestis, a retired National Institutes of Health toxicologist serving as an expert witness for the defense, testified that the results, if correct, suggested an "adulterated" product and said, "It should not be in there," the Times reported. Huestis told the paper by email that trace amounts of MGPI can be found in botanical kratom, so an allowable threshold for natural products should be set. Payton argued in court records that Hernandez died of benzodiazepine withdrawal and that the lab results should be excluded unless defense experts can test the same powder, according to the Times.

The Times reported that Abolins then found lab reports showing MGPI in other O.P.M.S. extracts, including O.P.M.S. Black Liquid. According to the Times, Josiah Berg, who died in 2023 at age 20 in Vancouver, Washington, and Amandus Harrison, a 46-year-old mother of six in Tennessee who died in 2024, had used that product, and court records in the families' lawsuits describe Harrison's death as a kratom overdose. Expert witnesses paid $850 per hour by Gabbay's legal team sent letters urging policymakers to allow some MGPI in otherwise botanical kratom, according to the Times, as did Dr. Rahul Gupta, who led the White House drug policy office under President Biden, and Jack Henningfield, who previously worked for Purdue Pharma. Gupta, who the Times said suggested the level HHS adopted, told the paper by email that he wrote "in my own capacity" and was not paid by any litigant, the association, or any industry organization. Henningfield did not respond to a request for comment, according to the Times.

## Where the positions sit

The administration says it is targeting products manufactured and formulated to be highly potent, according to the Times, which cited the "Smax Pseudo DHM" packets listing MGPI as an ingredient that agents seized near two University of Mississippi students found dead in September, a case [Kratom News Today reported on October 12, 2026][federal-filing-toxicology-mitragynine-mgm-15-mgpi-both-ole-miss-students-probable-cause-fox-news-defense-attorney-cough-syrup-inhalation-asphyxiation-yahya-mosleh-detained-islam-mosleh-20000-bond]. The American Kratom Association supports the thresholds, according to the Times, which reported the association sought an allowance for some MGPI in otherwise natural products. Dean Francis, president of the nonprofit End Kratom Addiction, told the Times the discovery of potent compounds in natural kratom should force a reckoning comparable to the removal of cocaine from Coca-Cola in 1929, and said he found it hard to believe the country would knowingly permit a potent opioid in any product. Lawyers for families of people who died after consuming kratom containing MGPI filed a motion earlier this month arguing the thresholds harm their cases by letting companies call their products lawful and natural, the Times reported, and David Berg, Josiah's father, told the paper he does not believe any level is safe. The telehealth provider QuickMD separately said on October 27, 2026 that kratom and 7-OH dependence accounted for half of its new addiction patients, as [Kratom News Today reports today][quickmd-report-kratom-7oh-half-new-addiction-treatment-patients-august-2026-up-from-10-percent-2024-100000-visits-lissack-55-percent-september-buprenorphine-off-label].
