Calls to the Washington Poison Center involving kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) increased 433 percent over the past three years, with the sharpest rise in 2026, according to data the center provided to [KIRO 7](https://www.kiro7.com/news/local/kratom-related-calls-surge-washington-amid-push-regulations/SCPDMJAMKNC6RNLJQ6KN5CAYRE/) for a report by Deedee Sun published October 27, 2026. The center logged 176 calls through the end of September, and September brought a call nearly every day, the station reported. The calls range from overdoses to people going through withdrawal, according to KIRO 7. The station reported that poison experts are particularly concerned about concentrated 7-OH products. KIRO 7 described these as products manufacturers isolate or concentrate to levels it said are far more potent than the trace amounts in natural leaf. The report did not break down how many of the 176 calls involved concentrated 7-OH products and how many involved natural leaf kratom.

## What the poison center said

Dr. Jimmy Leonard, the center's chief clinical officer, described concentrated 7-OH to KIRO 7 as "essentially an unregulated opioid" and said it can slow or stop breathing and can cause death. Leonard told the station he is particularly concerned about accidental exposures involving children, because some 7-OH products come as small tablets that resemble candy in flavors such as Sour Strawberry and Blue Razz. He said even a small amount could leave a child comatose and that the center has seen a few children who needed naloxone, according to the report. Leonard told the station that a parent who finds kratom or 7-OH in a child's room should treat it as an opioid exposure and seek help, KIRO 7 reported. The center issued a separate safety alert earlier this month about tablets sold as "Cat's Claw" that contained 7-OH, MGM-15, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, as [Kratom News Today reported on October 9, 2026][washington-poison-center-safety-alert-fake-cats-claw-buzzers-homiez-7oh-mgm-15-mgpi-spokane-stores-utah-four-deaths-tri-city-herald].

## No statewide rules, and shelves unchanged

Washington is one of 16 states without any statewide regulations governing kratom and has no statewide minimum purchase age, KIRO 7 reported, citing the American Kratom Association, which the station said counts 33 states with some form of kratom regulation and nine with bans. The station reported that Bellingham, Mount Vernon, and Spokane have adopted local bans, and Arlington's council approved a sales ban that takes effect November 18, 2026, as [Kratom News Today reported on October 21, 2026][arlington-washington-council-approves-ordinance-banning-sale-distribution-advertising-all-kratom-products-effective-november-18-first-snohomish-county-city-blythe-poison-control-ellis-retailer-notice-quincy-weighs-ban-or-regulation]. KIRO 7 said it found kratom and 7-OH products openly advertised and sold at stores around Seattle, and that most packaging it examined said purchasers should be 21 or older, with some carrying warnings about addiction, withdrawal, and death.

The station reported that the Drug Enforcement Administration took emergency action in August placing mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 in Schedule I, that the Justice Department recommended in October removing 7-OH products above a certain strength from shelves, and that the DEA is not currently seeking to ban all kratom products. Multiple smoke shops KIRO 7 visited said the federal action so far had little effect on what they sell. "We didn't have to pull anything," said Cassie York, an employee at University Smoke Shop in Seattle, who told the station that demand for stronger products has grown with kratom's popularity. York said she often sees former opioid users buying kratom, acknowledged it can be abused, and said the industry could use somewhat better regulation and labeling, according to the report.

## Reeves plans another run at a consumer protection act

State Rep. Kristine Reeves, a Federal Way Democrat, told KIRO 7 that lawmakers "absolutely" have a responsibility to protect people from kratom and related products and that she plans to push for legislation again in the upcoming session. Reeves said House Bill 2291, her Kratom Consumer Protection Act, did not pass last session because lawmakers were still familiarizing themselves with the topic, and she said budget challenges in Olympia could complicate a 2027 effort, according to the station. "The synthetic version, that is the version that most mimics fentanyl," Reeves said, "and we've really got to do the work to ensure that that's not in our communities." The Seattle Times reported last week that her bill would ban synthetic kratom and regulate natural leaf under the Liquor and Cannabis Board, as [Kratom News Today reported on October 23, 2026][washington-rep-kristine-reeves-working-on-bill-ban-synthetic-kratom-regulate-natural-leaf-liquor-cannabis-board-hb-2291-seattle-times-king-pierce-spokane-medical-examiners-no-rise-75-king-county-deaths-fentanyl].

The KIRO 7 report also profiled Tiffany Mercer of Vancouver, Washington, who told the station she began using kratom daily on a friend's assurance that it was a non-addictive herbal supplement, escalated to every few hours, and experienced severe withdrawal when she tried to stop, before an emergency room physician treated her with Suboxone, a medication used for opioid use disorder. The report did not specify whether the products Mercer used were natural leaf kratom or concentrated 7-OH preparations. Mercer now runs an Instagram page called "Kicking Kratom," according to KIRO 7. The station reported that global kratom product sales exceed $2 billion a year, citing market research it did not name.
