Seven researchers from the United States, Canada, and Malaysia published a perspective article in Frontiers in Pharmacology on October 22, 2026 that proposes a standardized definition of kratom, sorts products into four categories by alkaloid content, and recommends specific limits on mitragynine per serving and on 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) in leaf products. The paper, titled ["Defining kratom: evidence-based policy guidelines for a rapidly evolving market,"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1925809/full) lists Erin Berthold of Planted in Science Consulting, Paula N. Brown and Michael Chan of the BC Institute of Technology, Oliver Grundmann, Christopher R. McCurdy, and Abhisheak Sharma of the University of Florida, and Darshan Singh of Universiti Sains Malaysia as authors, with Brown and Grundmann listed as corresponding authors. The authors write that banning or strictly scheduling leaf and traditional kratom preparations is not supported by the evidence, while synthetic duplicates and chemically altered alkaloids should be treated as unapproved novel drugs. The paper counts nine states with outright kratom bans and cites a 2017 Food and Drug Administration (FDA) analysis that, according to the paper, supported Schedule I treatment of mitragynine and 7-OH. The Global Kratom Coalition, a kratom industry advocacy organization, issued a [press release on October 29, 2026](https://www.timesargus.com/features/health/experts-and-federal-officials-agree-natural-kratom-leaf-should-not-be-banned/article_eaa1b25e-2766-52dd-8f9b-884c35c27065.html) supporting the paper and urging lawmakers to adopt its approach.

## What the paper proposes

The authors argue that consumers and regulators in the United States struggle to distinguish traditional leaf products from concentrated extracts, isolated alkaloids, and synthetic or chemically altered compounds, and that some products sold as kratom are adulterated or contain no kratom at all, according to the paper's abstract. The paper offers terminology intended as a reference for industry and for federal, state, and other regulators.

The paper sorts products into four categories by alkaloid content. According to the paper, Category 1 covers traditional preparations with less than 10% total alkaloids; Category 2 covers concentrated extracts, with semi-purified extracts at 10% to 40% and purified extracts at 40% to 60%; Category 3 covers isolates above 60%; and Category 4 covers novel drug substances, including synthetic duplicates of kratom alkaloids and chemically altered alkaloids.

For leaf material, the paper proposes that mitragynine make up 0.5% to 2.5% of dried leaf weight and roughly 51% to 82% of the total alkaloid fraction, that total alkaloids stay below 3.0% by weight, and that 7-OH not exceed 0.01% by dry weight. The paper states that naturally occurring trace levels of 7-OH in some post-harvest leaf and products run from 0.003% to 0.04% by weight, and that semi-synthetic 7-OH products contain far more than the plant can provide. The paper does not give a specific figure for those products.

For Category 1 products and leaf, the paper recommends no more than 40 milligrams of mitragynine per serving and no more than two servings in 24 hours, for a daily total of 80 milligrams. For extracts without their own safety data, the paper suggests the same limits apply only when the product carries 40 milligrams or less of mitragynine per serving, mitragynine above 40% of total alkaloids, and 7-OH below 0.01%; the paper adds that extract sellers should show the product is not chemically altered, provide safety data, and file a new dietary ingredient notification with the FDA. For purified isolates and Category 4 products, the paper says there is no basis for a safety determination and recommends they be treated as unapproved novel drugs.

On manufacturing and labeling, the paper recommends that producers follow the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act and the current good manufacturing practice regulations at 21 CFR Part 111, including written specifications, testing, and adverse event collection. The authors propose labels that include the word "kratom" in the ingredient list, along with the serving size, servings per day, duration of use, expiration date, mitragynine percentage, and lot number. They also suggest listing 7-OH content per serving. The paper recommends that products be labeled for adults over 21 and carry warnings about pregnancy, medications, alcohol, and dependence.

## The state landscape as the authors describe it

As of July 2026, the paper counts nine states with outright bans on kratom: Alabama, Arkansas, Connecticut, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana, Tennessee, Vermont, and Wisconsin. The authors describe California as having a de facto ban through actions by its Department of Public Health, list Michigan, Ohio, Idaho, Illinois, and Maryland as states considering full bans, and count 20 states with minimal restrictions and 15 with Kratom Consumer Protection Acts. The paper cites a 2026 Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report article on increases in kratom-related poison center reports from 2015 to 2025.

The paper carries no funding: the authors declared that financial support was not received for the work or its publication. The conflict of interest statement discloses that Berthold was employed by Planted in Science Consulting, LLC, and that Grundmann and McCurdy serve as expert witnesses in kratom-involved legal cases. The remaining authors declared no commercial or financial relationships that could be construed as a potential conflict, according to the statement.

## The Global Kratom Coalition's framing

The Global Kratom Coalition's release, datelined Washington, D.C. and distributed through KoreWire, describes the paper as the work of seven researchers and says federal regulators have separated natural kratom leaf from concentrated synthetic 7-OH and other novel compounds including MGM-15, MGM-16, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl. The release quotes Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s statement from October 9, 2026 that "we are drawing a clear line between natural kratom and dangerous, enhanced opioid products," a statement that, according to Kratom News Today's earlier reporting, accompanied [HHS's revised threshold recommendation to the DEA][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14]. According to the release, the Drug Enforcement Administration's (DEA) temporary scheduling of three synthetic 7-OH derivatives took effect August 26, 2026, and a separate DEA proposal covering concentrated synthetic 7-OH is slated to take effect November 13, 2026.

Matthew Lowe, the coalition's executive director, said in the release that the paper gives lawmakers a ready-made plan grounded in the research, and that blanket bans fail to distinguish natural leaf from concentrated or synthetic products. Semi-synthetic 7-OH products entered the market in late 2023, according to the release. No new data warranting a ban has emerged in the decade since the first federal scheduling attempt, the coalition asserts.

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data cited in the release show kratom-related poison center reports plateaued from 2020 through 2024 and more than doubled in 2025, a rise the coalition attributes to high-potency semi-synthetic products. The release puts the number of Americans who consume natural kratom leaf at an estimated 23 million; the paper estimates 10 million to 16 million consumers in the United States.

Twenty-three states regulate natural kratom leaf while banning concentrated synthetic derivatives, according to the release. The coalition also refers to the September deaths of two University of Mississippi students and says a search warrant affidavit indicated the students bought "Smax" tablets that preliminary testing found contained MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, which the release describes as scheduled compounds, along with concentrated synthetic 7-OH; Kratom News Today reported on [the federal filing describing toxicology results in that case][federal-filing-toxicology-mitragynine-mgm-15-mgpi-both-ole-miss-students-probable-cause-fox-news-defense-attorney-cough-syrup-inhalation-asphyxiation-yahya-mosleh-detained-islam-mosleh-20000-bond].
