The New Jersey Poison Control Center is warning that concentrated and synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products sold in smoke shops, vape stores, convenience stores, and online can produce opioid-like effects, according to a [Rutgers University release](https://www.rutgers.edu/news/why-natural-doesnt-mean-safe-7-oh-products-gain-popularity) dated October 23, 2026 and a [WRNJ Radio report](https://wrnjradio.com/new-jersey-poison-control-center-warns-of-dangerous-opioid-like-effects-of-7-oh-and-kratom-products/) by Jay Edwards on October 29, 2026. The Rutgers release said the warning concerns concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products rather than natural kratom leaf. According to the release, the center, which is based at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School, said wide availability and "natural" marketing do not show that a product is safe.

Diane Calello, the center's executive and medical director, said synthetic 7-OH is sometimes added to natural kratom, and the center said that practice raises the risk of dependence, withdrawal, and overdose, WRNJ Radio reported. Calello also said many consumers may not realize how much the market for these products has changed. Bruce Ruck, the center's managing director, said that "a product's availability is not a measure of its safety," according to the Rutgers release and WRNJ Radio. Ruck added that familiarity with a product can create a false sense of security.

The center said product labels may not accurately state ingredients or concentrations, and it listed trouble breathing, loss of consciousness, seizures, dependence, and withdrawal as possible serious effects, WRNJ Radio reported. Withdrawal symptoms the center described include muscle aches, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, tremors, and trouble sleeping, according to the station. The Rutgers release said people who regularly use these products may have opioid-like withdrawal symptoms when they cut back or stop.

The center noted that New Jersey officials recently moved to list three synthetic kratom-related compounds as controlled dangerous substances, and said products bought before those restrictions took effect may still be in circulation, according to both reports. Neither report identified which New Jersey agency took that step or gave a date. Kratom News Today (KNT) reported in September on [the public alert from Attorney General Jennifer Davenport and the Division of Consumer Affairs][new-jersey-attorney-general-davenport-public-alert-three-synthetic-kratom-compounds-controlled-dangerous-substances]. According to that alert, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 became controlled dangerous substances in New Jersey under the Drug Enforcement Administration's temporary Schedule I order, and natural kratom was not included.

WRNJ Radio reported that the center advised calling 911 for severe symptoms such as trouble breathing, seizures, or passing out, and said its Poison Help Line at 1-800-222-1222 is free, confidential, and available around the clock. The Rutgers release and the WRNJ Radio report did not include case counts or comment from industry or advocacy groups.
