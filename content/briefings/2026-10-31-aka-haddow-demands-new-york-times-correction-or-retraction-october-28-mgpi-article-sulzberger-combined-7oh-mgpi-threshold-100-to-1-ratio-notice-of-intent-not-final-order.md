The American Kratom Association (AKA) has demanded that The New York Times correct or retract its October 28, 2026 article on the federal government's revised approach to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), [the association said in a release distributed on October 30, 2026](https://eznewswire.com/newsroom/aka-demands-correction-retraction-nyt-dea-opioid-scheduling). According to the release, the association sent a letter to Times publisher A.G. Sulzberger arguing that the article, headlined "Trump Administration Backs Down on an Opioid Ban After Kratom Industry Lobbying," materially mischaracterized the Drug Enforcement Administration's (DEA) revised proposal for concentrated or added 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and MGPI by leaving out two of its provisions. According to the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) announcement of the thresholds, the proposal targets concentrated 7-OH and MGPI and is distinct from the trace 7-OH naturally present in raw kratom leaf. Kratom News Today (KNT) [reported on the Times article on October 28, 2026][new-york-times-trump-administration-backs-down-mgpi-ban-kratom-industry-lobbying-gabbay-2-9-million-haddow-white-house-chittiboyina-25-to-500-times-kolodny-56-overdose-cases-48-fatal].

The dispute concerns the threshold structure that HHS recommended and the DEA adopted in notices of intent published in the Federal Register on October 14, 2026, which [KNT covered on October 10, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14]. According to the Justice Department's October 9, 2026 announcement, 7-OH and MGPI would be placed in Schedule I when either substance alone, or both combined, exceeds 1 milligram per gram in solid products or 1 milligram per milliliter in liquids, or when the ratio of mitragynine to those compounds is less than 100 to 1. MGPI is a compound that the Times described as a semi-synthetic opioid derived from kratom and distinct from natural leaf.

## What the association says the Times left out

The release identifies two omissions. First, according to the AKA, the revised proposal does not give manufacturers separate allowances for 7-OH and MGPI. "It combines those two potent opioid compounds under a single concentration threshold," Mac Haddow, the association's senior fellow on public policy, said in the release. Second, the association said the proposal includes an independent 100-to-1 ratio requirement under which a product can face Schedule I controls even when its combined 7-OH and MGPI concentration falls below the numerical limit. Haddow said in the release that ignoring those provisions distorts the scope and intent of the federal action.

The association listed what it is asking the Times to do, according to the release syndicated by [Fortune](https://fortune.com/press-releases/aka-demands-correction-retraction-nyt-dea-opioid-scheduling-2026-10-30/): correct the article's treatment of the combined threshold, explain the ratio test, clarify that the October action remains a notice of intent rather than an effective final order, distinguish scheduling thresholds from findings of product safety or market authorization, reconsider the headline, and correct republished and syndicated versions. If the central characterization cannot be fixed without rewriting the piece, the association said, the article should be retracted. "The New York Times owes its readers a correction that explains what the DEA actually proposed," Haddow said in the release.

The release said the association does not dispute that journalists should scrutinize government decisions, including lobbying and political contributions, and argued that such scrutiny does not excuse omitting material provisions of the rule. The release does not name any company, donor, or contribution figure. The Times article, as KNT reported, described the contributions of O.P.M.S. distribution manager David Gabbay and his wife to Republican candidates and committees, quoted Haddow describing a White House visit, and reported that HHS said neither litigation nor political contributions influenced its recommendation.

## Where the two accounts overlap

Several points the association raised also appear in the Times article. The Times reported that the Federal Register notice acknowledged that "neither H.H.S. nor D.E.A. has made a finding that these substances are safe to use" at the permitted levels, and the paper described the proposed order as expected to be finalized in the coming weeks rather than already in effect. [As KNT reported on October 14, 2026][dea-7oh-mgpi-notices-published-federal-register-october-14-91-fr-65370-dea-1570-mgm-15-mgm-16-dea-1644-earliest-order-november-13-harris-lessen-up-regulation-crazy-aka-rayburn-briefing-senators-october-15-deadline], the DEA's notices state that the temporary scheduling order may be issued no earlier than November 13, 2026. The Times article's headline and opening framed the change as a retreat from a full MGPI ban following industry lobbying, which is the characterization the association disputes; KNT's October 28 report drew the threshold figures from the Justice Department's announcement rather than from the Times.

Haddow told the Times he was "very satisfied" with the proposed order, according to the October 28 article, and the association said earlier in October that its federal lawsuit over the DEA's August MGPI order, which [KNT covered on August 27, 2026][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order], had been resolved. The association's release does not say whether the Times has responded to the letter, and KNT has not independently confirmed receipt.

## A journal article on kratom and opioid classification

A journal article published this week takes a different view of how kratom should be classified. [A blog post by Alexander Stump on the Community Anti-Drug Coalitions of America (CADCA) site](https://www.cadca.org/blog/kratom-is-an-opioid/), published October 29, 2026, summarizes an article titled "Kratom Is an Opioid" by Andrew Kolodny and Christopher Holstege in the [Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology](https://doi.org/10.1097/JCP.0000000000002272). According to the CADCA summary, the authors argue that kratom should be classified as an opioid because mitragynine activates the same receptors as morphine, is converted in the body to 7-OH, and produces euphoria, dependence, tolerance, and withdrawal, and they caution against policies that target only concentrated 7-OH products while leaving other kratom products widely available. Kolodny, the medical director of the Opioid Policy Research Collaborative at Brandeis University, was quoted in the Times article saying the HHS justification reflected the industry writing the policy, according to the October 28 report. The CADCA post cites poison center reports involving kratom rising from 2015 to 2025 and reaching 3,434 in 2025, with about one quarter of single-substance exposures resulting in hospitalization, according to the post's summary of the article. KNT has not reviewed the journal article's full text.
