The American Medical Association (AMA) has told the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) that kratom, cannabis, and other botanical products and their derivatives should be regulated by the agency and have their safety and efficacy established through clinical trials before they are marketed or prescribed for any condition, and that it supports banning synthesized, purified, or derivative kratom products, including the 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) market, a category of concentrated or synthetic preparations distinct from the trace 7-OH naturally present in raw kratom leaf, [according to a letter dated October 28, 2026](https://searchlf.ama-assn.org/letter/documentDownload?uri=/unstructured/binary/letter/LETTERS/lff.zip/2026-10-28-Letter-to-Graham-re-FDA-RFI-Botanicals.pdf) that the association [announced in a press release on October 30, 2026](https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/ama-urges-fda-pursue-safety-standards-botanical-products). The three-page letter, addressed to FDA Deputy Commissioner for Policy, Legislation, and International Affairs Grace R. Graham and signed by AMA Chief Executive Officer John Whyte, responds to Docket No. FDA-2026-N-9550, "Advancing Development of Botanical Drug Products; Request for Information," according to its text.

The letter follows two earlier AMA kratom actions this year. Kratom News Today (KNT) [reported on June 14, 2026][ama-adopts-policy-targeting-synthetic-7oh-kratom] that the AMA House of Delegates adopted policy targeting synthesized and derivative kratom products, and [on October 2, 2026][american-medical-association-october-2-state-advocacy-update-kratom-issue-brief-2027-sessions-ban-synthesized-purified-derivative-7oh-mgm-15] that the association updated its kratom issue brief for state legislatures ahead of 2027 sessions. The October 28 letter repeats the issue brief's language nearly verbatim, stating that the AMA "supports legislative and other efforts banning synthesized, purified, or derivative products from kratom for marketing, distribution, promotion, and sale, including but not limited to unregulated mitragynine along with the 7-hydroxymitragynine and MGM-15 market," and adds that the association has worked with multiple medical societies to support new state laws "protecting consumers from kratom and 7-OH products."

## What the letter says about kratom

According to the letter, the AMA "was pleased" to see the Drug Enforcement Administration's (DEA) emergency scheduling action for three kratom-related compounds, quoting the DEA order's description of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI) and "two synthetic compounds known as MGM-15 and MGM-16" as substances that "pose an imminent hazard to public safety." That order, which [KNT covered on August 27, 2026][aka-federal-lawsuit-dea-emergency-scheduling-order], placed the three compounds in Schedule I effective August 26, 2026, according to the Justice Department's August 25, 2026 announcement. The letter does not mention the DEA's separate October 14, 2026 notices of intent for 7-OH and MGPI above a concentration threshold, which [KNT reported on October 10, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14].

The letter also says the AMA "was particularly pleased" that the DEA relied on an FDA assessment stating that "7-OH produces respiratory depression, physical dependence, and withdrawal symptoms characteristic of classical opioids, such as morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, and hydrocodone," and quotes a finding that "7-OH produces respiratory depression with more than threefold greater potency than morphine," a finding the letter attributes to unnamed studies. The letter does not separate 7-OH found in trace amounts in raw leaf from the concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products that the FDA assessment addressed, according to its text. The ban language it endorses names "unregulated mitragynine," the main alkaloid in kratom leaf, alongside 7-OH and MGM-15. Citing an FDA estimate that 1.7 million Americans aged 12 and older used kratom in 2021, the letter says the AMA commends the FDA "for its work to protect consumers from harmful botanicals, including kratom."

## The broader argument on botanicals

Beyond kratom, the letter names cannabis, kava, kanna, and "other botanicals that are increasingly used by consumers for specious health claims made by their manufacturers or influencers," and states that today's formulations and synthesized derivatives differ substantially from historical use. According to the letter, the AMA is "deeply concerned that consumers often are the real-world guinea pigs who suffer tragic consequences, including dependence, use disorders, and death" as manufacturers release more potent products in forms including teas, powders, tablets, capsules, tinctures, gummies, oils, and injections. The letter says the FDA approval process for drugs containing cannabis or other botanicals "should not be short-circuited or circumvented merely because of anecdotal support or growing public use," and that political considerations or conflicts of interest should not be allowed to overrule scientific evidence in the agency's decisions.

The letter also sets out the association's cannabis research positions, including support for well-controlled studies of cannabis and related cannabinoids in patients with serious conditions, a request that the National Institutes of Health, the DEA, and the FDA develop a special schedule and administrative procedures for cannabis research, and a request that the National Institute on Drug Abuse supply cannabis of consistent strengths to DEA-registered investigators conducting FDA-approved studies. Its closing recommendation, according to the letter's text, is that any product containing kratom, cannabis, or other botanicals, and its derivatives, be regulated by the FDA, with safety and efficacy determined through clinical trials before the product can be marketed or prescribed as a treatment for any condition.

In the press release, AMA President Willie Underwood III said consumers are becoming "the real-world guinea pigs" as botanical manufacturers develop increasingly potent products, "and suffering tragic consequences, including dependence, substance use disorders, overdose, and death." Underwood added: "Calling something 'natural' doesn't make it safe. We need science, not social media hype, to determine what belongs in our medicine cabinets."

## How the AMA position compares with the Global Kratom Coalition's

The AMA's recommendation that botanical products go through the drug approval process before marketing differs from the position the Global Kratom Coalition took on October 29, 2026, when, [as KNT reported on October 30, 2026][frontiers-in-pharmacology-defining-kratom-perspective-grundmann-mccurdy-brown-four-product-categories-40-mg-mitragynine-per-serving-0-01-percent-7oh-leaf-ceiling-global-kratom-coalition-blanket-bans], the coalition backed a Frontiers in Pharmacology perspective paper proposing product categories, a per-serving mitragynine limit, and a 7-OH ceiling for leaf products, while arguing that natural leaf should be regulated rather than banned or scheduled. The AMA letter commends the DEA's scheduling of MGPI, MGM-15, and MGM-16, while the coalition's October 29 statement, as KNT reported, endorsed the paper's position that concentrated synthetic 7-OH and isolated MGPI should be treated as novel drug substances rather than as kratom. The two positions differ on mitragynine-based leaf products: the AMA letter recommends FDA regulation and clinical trials before marketing, and the coalition argued for regulation of natural leaf rather than a ban or scheduling. KNT has not reviewed other submissions to the FDA docket.
