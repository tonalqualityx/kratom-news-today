The California Department of Public Health (CDPH) has embargoed or destroyed approximately $10 million worth of kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products and is pursuing legal action against manufacturers, according to [an October 31, 2026 release](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/10/31/trick-treat-or-illegal-product-california-keeps-cracking-down-on-dangerous-products-hiding-in-plain-sight/) from Governor Gavin Newsom's office. The Halloween-themed release, which also covered intoxicating hemp and illicit cannabis enforcement, described kratom-derived substances as "a dangerous and evolving drug threat" and said CDPH is targeting products and raw materials at retail locations and manufacturing facilities while coordinating with state enforcement partners. The release did not name any manufacturer, describe the legal action, or give a date range for the $10 million figure beyond "to date."

The release groups natural leaf kratom, concentrated 7-OH products, and the synthetic compound MGM-15 under a single heading and does not break the $10 million figure down by product type. It therefore does not indicate how much of the embargoed or destroyed material was concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations, which contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts present in natural kratom leaf, and how much was natural leaf kratom. The release's own description of the category focuses on products "often marketed as health-conscious supplements" whose contents, it said, "are anything but that."

## The updated enforcement figures

According to the release, the Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control (ABC) has conducted 9,209 visits, removed 8,230 kratom and 7-OH products from shelves, and reported a 97.97 percent compliance rate among the locations it has visited since enforcement began. Those numbers update the figures the governor's office published on September 2, 2026, when [KNT reported][california-statewide-enforcement-removes-kratom-7oh-products] on [that release](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/02/governor-newsom-announces-nearly-15300-illegal-hemp-kratom-and-7-oh-products-removed-from-california-store-shelves/) that ABC had removed 7,888 prohibited kratom and 7-OH products across 8,038 site visits with a 97.75 percent compliance rate. Comparing the two releases, ABC conducted 1,171 additional visits and removed 342 additional products over the intervening two months. The September release did not include a CDPH dollar figure or a reference to legal action against manufacturers.

Pan, who is CDPH director and state public health officer, was the only official quoted on kratom in the release. "Kratom-derived substances like 7-OH and synthetic compounds like MGM-15 have been associated with addiction, overdose, and death, and consumers should not assume these products are safe alternatives to opioids or other drugs," Pan said in the release. "We urge Californians to protect yourself and your loved ones by avoiding using 7-OH and kratom-related products." The release's closing section told Californians to "stay away entirely from Kratom," alongside reminders to buy cannabis only from licensed retailers and to check that hemp products contain no detectable THC.

## Where California's posture stands

The release does not restate the legal basis for the kratom enforcement. As KNT noted in its coverage of [San Francisco's first fatal overdose involving MGM-15][san-francisco-first-fatal-mgm-15-overdose-script-botanicals-maui-labs-cats-claw-tablets] on October 22, 2026, the San Francisco Standard reported that CDPH banned kratom and 7-OH products late last year, and SFGATE reported that health officials consider kratom and 7-OH "illegal to sell" in the state. MGM-15, also called dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, is a semi-synthetic kratom-derived compound that the Drug Enforcement Administration placed in Schedule I in late August along with mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-16, according to that coverage. The September release referred only to kratom and 7-OH and did not mention MGM-15.

The kratom section sat between two larger enforcement summaries. On intoxicating hemp, the release said ABC has conducted 24,590 visits, removed 7,403 products, and recorded a 99.36 percent compliance rate under emergency regulations that bar detectable THC or other intoxicating cannabinoids per serving in hemp food, beverages, and dietary products, prohibit sales to anyone under 21, and limit packages to five servings. The release said Assembly Bill 8, which the governor signed in 2025, applies cannabis rules to intoxicating hemp. On illicit cannabis, the release said the Unified Cannabis Enforcement Task Force has seized or destroyed an estimated $1.47 billion in product since 2022, and it cited the governor's recent signing of Assembly Bill 2249 on cannabis packaging and marketing that may appeal to children.

## What the release leaves open

For manufacturers and distributors, the reference to legal action against manufacturers is the new element, and the release gives no detail on which companies, what claims, or which court. The $10 million valuation is attributed to CDPH without a methodology, a count of enforcement actions, or a breakdown between retail seizures and manufacturing-site embargoes. ABC's compliance rate covers the licensees it inspects, which include convenience stores, bars, and smoke shops, and the release does not address retailers outside ABC's licensing system or online sales into the state.

No kratom trade association, manufacturer, or retailer was quoted in the release, and the release did not reference the federal distinction between natural leaf kratom and concentrated 7-OH or MGPI products that the Department of Health and Human Services and the DEA drew in their October notices. The American Kratom Association and the Global Kratom Coalition have both publicly supported restrictions on synthetic and concentrated 7-OH products while opposing bans on natural leaf, positions KNT has reported in prior coverage. Neither organization's response to the California release appeared in the coverage reviewed for this briefing.
