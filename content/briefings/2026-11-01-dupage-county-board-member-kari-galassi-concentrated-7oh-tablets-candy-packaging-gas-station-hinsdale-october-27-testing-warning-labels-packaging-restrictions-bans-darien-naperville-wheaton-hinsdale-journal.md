A DuPage County Board member has told the board that a gas station about half a mile from her Hinsdale, Illinois home is selling concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) tablets packaged to resemble candy, products that contain the alkaloid at levels far above the trace amounts found in natural kratom leaf, according to an [October 30, 2026 report](https://hinsdalejournal.com/articles/hinsdale-board-member-warns-of-kratom-sold-at-nearby-gas-station-mvu0ydq2) in the Hinsdale Journal. Kari Galassi raised the issue at the county board's October 27 meeting, the Journal reported, describing kratom-derived products packaged to look like Skittles and other candy and promoted with neon lights outside the store, which the article did not name. Galassi said concentrated 7-OH differs from traditional kratom leaf and carries risks including addiction and slowed breathing, according to the Journal.

In a letter published by The Hinsdalean, Galassi described "dangerous products, including concentrated 7-OH opioids, packaged to resemble candy and sold right where our children shop," the Journal reported. She urged collaboration among public health officials, law enforcement, municipal leaders, and state legislators and proposed product testing, warning labels, packaging restrictions, and potential bans. The Journal did not report any board action, and it said it was not known whether the Village of Hinsdale or its police department has considered local restrictions; no related village meetings, agenda items, or forums had been announced.

## How the board member came to the issue

Galassi said she first learned about kratom from news coverage of packaged kratom-derived products found after the September deaths of two University of Mississippi students, and she acknowledged that kratom's role in those deaths remains under investigation, according to the Journal. The article quoted Oxford, Mississippi police as saying packaged kratom "sold from a retail store" was found in both investigations and that the department did "not have confirmed information indicating that kratom or any other substance was a contributing factor in either death," with autopsy and toxicology reports unreleased as of mid-October. A federal court filing reviewed by Fox News in October reported that toxicology found mitragynine and the Schedule I synthetic compounds MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in both students' blood, [as KNT reported on October 12][federal-filing-toxicology-mitragynine-mgm-15-mgpi-both-ole-miss-students-probable-cause-fox-news-defense-attorney-cough-syrup-inhalation-asphyxiation-yahya-mosleh-detained-islam-mosleh-20000-bond]; the full toxicology report has not been publicly released.

The Journal summarized the federal picture as follows: a July 6 notice proposed temporarily placing 7-OH in Schedule I, the Justice Department said on October 9 that the Drug Enforcement Administration would withdraw that notice and issue a new rule published October 14, the earliest the controls could take effect is November 13, and three related compounds, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16, have been in Schedule I since late August. The article also cited Food and Drug Administration warnings that kratom and concentrated 7-OH products can cause dependence and serious adverse effects.

## County health department and the suburban ban map

The DuPage County Health Department considers kratom an addictive opioid and supports a statewide ban in Illinois, the Journal reported. The article did not quote a department official or describe any county-level ordinance under consideration.

The Journal placed Galassi's remarks against a growing list of local prohibitions in the Chicago suburbs and downstate. Naperville voted 8-0 in early September to ban retail kratom sales effective October 1, [as KNT reported][naperville-city-council-votes-ban-kratom-sales]; the Journal reported that Darien approved a ban on October 19 and that Wheaton adopted one in April. Winnetka, Plainfield, Minooka, Normal, and Bloomington also have bans, according to the Journal. Naperville's ordinance, which covers natural and synthetic kratom, differs in scope from the approach Streator adopted on October 21, which [KNT covered][streator-illinois-council-approves-synthetic-kratom-ban-natural-leaf-over-21-concentration-limits-certificate-of-analysis-mclaughlin-bedei-effingham-county-unanimous-all-kratom-phenibut-7oh-ban-fines-250-750]: a ban on synthetic kratom with concentration limits and a certificate-of-analysis requirement that leaves natural leaf available to adults 21 and older.

At the state level, the Journal reported that the Illinois Kratom Control Act prohibits sales to anyone under 18 but does not broadly regulate product strength, labeling, or purity for adult sales. Two bills in the 2026 spring session, one proposing a statewide ban and the other a consumer protection framework, did not advance, according to the article.

## What is and is not established

The Journal's account rests on Galassi's description of what she saw and on her published letter; the article did not identify the retailer, report a test of the products, or quote the store. The distinction Galassi drew between concentrated 7-OH and traditional leaf is the same line the Department of Health and Human Services and the DEA drew in October, when, according to the [Justice Department's October 9 announcement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-announces-updates-emergency-scheduling-actions-7-oh-and-related-opioid), HHS recommended Schedule I control for 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above concentration and ratio thresholds rather than for natural leaf kratom. Whether DuPage County pursues a county ordinance, as several Illinois municipalities have, or defers to the statewide ban the county health department supports was not addressed in the report. No kratom trade association or retailer was quoted.
