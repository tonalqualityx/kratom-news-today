Kratom makers are increasingly marketing their products for energy, focus, and athletic performance through free samples at gyms, sponsored sporting events, social media influencers, and wellness podcasts, [The New York Times reported](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/02/well/kratom-wellness-marketing-addiction.html) on November 2, 2026. The Times said the marketing often omits risks it listed as addiction, liver injury, seizures, and fatal overdoses. The report, published in the paper's Well section, profiles three people who said they became addicted after encountering kratom through a gym, a podcast, or a sponsorship offer. It carries responses from two of the companies named, MIT45 and Botanic Tonics, the maker of Feel Free. The Times did not specify whether the products the three people used were natural leaf kratom, extracts, or concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) preparations, and it discusses 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI) in a separate regulatory passage of the article. Kratom News Today (KNT) reviewed the article through its [syndicated republication](https://dnyuz.com/2026/11/02/they-tried-kratom-for-its-health-benefits-then-they-became-addicted) on DNyuz.

## How the Times says the products are being marketed

According to the Times, MIT45 sponsors the men's division of the International Fitness and Bodybuilding Federation and described the partnership as having the potential to "transform the fitness industry." The paper reported that the company previously paid individual athletes to promote its products and that it continues to sell at gyms and through health and wellness channels. MIT45 chief executive Ryan Niddel told the Times that sponsored athletes were told to avoid health claims, that the products posed little risk when used as recommended, and that the product has a place in the market but is not for everyone and is not meant to be used in high quantities over long periods, according to the paper.

The Times reported that Mitra-9 recruited mixed martial arts fighters, wrestlers, and sobriety content creators for a social media campaign, and that Kingdom Kratom sells sample packs that its website suggests customers test "across a training week." Mitra-9 is separately defending a proposed consumer class action in the Southern District of New York over alleged failure to warn about addiction, as [KNT reported on October 7, 2026][mitra-9-brands-southern-district-new-york-swain-denies-motion-dismiss-addiction-false-advertising-suit-bursor-fisher-vasquez-adexchanger-transparency]. The Times did not report a response from Mitra-9 or Kingdom Kratom.

Feel Free, the kava and kratom tonic made by Botanic Tonics, distributed free samples through partnerships with university athletic programs, according to the Times, which did not name the universities or the years involved. The paper also reported that the brand ran an Instagram campaign under the hashtag #alcoholalternative, and that the campaign was the subject of a class action that ended in an $8.75 million settlement. Arizona's Family reported in September 2026 that the settlement required the company to add a label warning that kratom "can be habit-forming and harmful if not consumed as directed," as KNT noted when [Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes sued Botanic Tonics][arizona-attorney-general-mayes-sues-botanic-tonics-feel-free-consumer-fraud-act-first-in-nation] under the state's Consumer Fraud Act on September 23, 2026.

## The three accounts

Hilary Tesluck told the Times she first tried kratom at 31 as an alternative to her anxiety medication and initially felt it eased her anxiety and depression. After about a year and a half of what the paper called moderate use, Tesluck said she increased her dose and frequency to suppress appetite and lose weight, and at her peak took up to 100 capsules a day. Tesluck told the Times she often became severely nauseous, re-dosed after vomiting, and fell asleep unexpectedly while working from home, which she attributed to high doses. Tesluck said she realized she was addicted after running out during a snowstorm and waking drenched in sweat with muscle aches and anxiety, according to the paper. The Times said Tesluck now works for a nonprofit focused on kratom addiction but did not name it; Tesluck testified before a Georgia legislative study committee in August 2026 as executive director of End Kratom Addiction, as [KNT reported at the time][georgia-brunswick-kratom-hearing-west-chester-ordinance].

Jasmine Adeoye, also 31, told the Times she first heard about kratom on The Skinny Confidential Him & Her Show, a podcast now called The Bossticks, on which co-host Michael Bosstick called kratom a "magical elixir," according to the paper. Adeoye said she initially drank Feel Free occasionally on weekends, that her use increased after a stressful remote job left her anxious and isolated, and that within a year she was drinking two two-ounce bottles a day, sometimes back to back. The Times reported that Adeoye maxed out two credit cards to pay for the product, that her sleep worsened, and that heart palpitations woke her at night. "It very quickly turns on you," Adeoye told the paper. The Times did not say whether Bosstick responded to a request for comment.

Jason Ellis, a podcast host and former professional skateboarder and mixed martial arts fighter who had discussed past alcohol and painkiller misuse on his show, told the Times that friends in the fight community described kratom to him as "kind of like a painkiller, but herbal." According to the paper, Feel Free founder Jerry W. Ross offered Ellis free cases of product and a cut of sales through a listener discount code in 2023 in exchange for promotion, and within about six months Ellis was drinking up to three bottles a day. Ross did not respond to the Times' request for comment, the paper said. Ellis, Tesluck, and others described relapses and withdrawal symptoms including sweating, chills, and muscle aches, according to the Times, and Tesluck said she experienced months of mental fog after quitting following several attempts that included a detox program.

## What the companies and a clinician said

Aaron Henninger, chief marketing officer of Botanic Tonics, told the Times the company is "continuing to invest in research," that Feel Free now clearly lists kratom on its labels, and that it advises people with substance abuse histories not to use the product. The Times reported that Feel Free's labeled serving size is half a bottle and that the company warns against consuming more than one bottle in 24 hours, describing the product as potentially "habit-forming." Niddel's comments for MIT45 are described above; the paper did not report a response from Mitra-9, Kingdom Kratom, Ross, or Bosstick.

Dr. Cornel Stanciu, director of addiction services at New Hampshire Hospital, told the Times: "The public deserves accurate, nuanced information rather than messaging driven primarily by commercial incentives." According to the paper, Stanciu and other clinicians it consulted said people with past alcohol or drug problems may be especially vulnerable to the products and that targeted marketing can create a false sense of safety, especially for young people. The Times wrote that "some studies suggest between 29 and 40 percent" of regular kratom users become dependent, without naming a study in its text. The link the Times attached to that statement points to a [2025 online survey study in Drug and Alcohol Dependence Reports](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41322679/) by Katherine Hill, Edward W. Boyer, and Kirsten E. Smith, whose abstract reports that 38.1 percent of 367 surveyed US adults who had used kava or kratom met past-year DSM-5 substance use disorder criteria for kratom. The abstract describes a self-selected survey sample, and the authors wrote that substance use disorder findings should be interpreted in light of other substance use and patient factors.

## The regulatory frame in the Times report

The Times reported that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved no kratom product for any medical use, that federal officials moved to temporarily ban products with high levels of 7-OH, which the paper described as a synthetic form, and that officials had planned to ban the semi-synthetic compound MGPI but agreed to allow it at certain levels after industry lobbying. That lobbying account is the subject of the paper's October 28, 2026 report, which [KNT covered at the time][new-york-times-trump-administration-backs-down-mgpi-ban-kratom-industry-lobbying-gabbay-2-9-million-haddow-white-house-chittiboyina-25-to-500-times-kolodny-56-overdose-cases-48-fatal]. The Times repeated its earlier figure that MGPI has been linked to at least 48 fatal overdoses and wrote that doctors it consulted are concerned that synthetic products can be far more potent than natural kratom, and that even products marketed as natural or whole leaf can contain compounds such as MGPI. The paper reported that some states have banned kratom or restricted sales to minors while the products remain available at gas stations, smoke shops, and online in many areas.

The article does not describe any of the named brands' products as concentrated 7-OH preparations, and it does not include a response from the American Kratom Association, the Global Kratom Coalition, or other trade groups.

The Times report closes with Tesluck saying she still hears from people who were introduced to kratom through gym or social media samples, and that companies continue to release stronger derivative products, according to the paper.
