The Mississippi House Drug Policy Committee heard a day of testimony on November 4, 2026 on whether the state should ban all kratom products or build a tighter regulatory system, with most witnesses urging a full ban and a natural-leaf advocacy group arguing for a registry that would separate tested leaf products from synthetic derivatives, [Magnolia Tribune](https://magnoliatribune.com/2026/11/05/mississippi-lawmakers-gather-information-on-kratom-as-2027-session-nears/) and [Mississippi Today](https://mississippitoday.org/2026/11/05/mississippi-lawmakers-hear-testimony-amid-debate-over-statewide-kratom-ban/) reported. The hearing was driven by the September deaths of two University of Mississippi students in Oxford, and the products at issue in that case were synthetic kratom-related compounds rather than natural leaf: Rebecca Strang told the committee the Smax Mango Breeze product her son bought tested positive for MGM-15 and MGPI, according to Magnolia Tribune, compounds the Drug Enforcement Administration placed in Schedule I in August in an action the [Justice Department said](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-emergency-scheduling-three-potent-opioid-compounds) was directed at manufactured and concentrated opioid products rather than traditional botanical kratom.

Committee chair Rep. Beth Waldo, a Republican from Pontotoc, led the hearing, which Mississippi Today described as lawmakers considering steps to further restrict kratom beyond the 2025 law that limited purchases to people 21 and older and prohibited synthetic kratom products. The hearing came one day before the Mississippi State Medical Association's call for a statewide retail ban in 2027, [Kratom News Today (KNT) reported on November 5][mississippi-state-medical-association-lampton-urges-2027-legislature-statewide-kratom-sales-ban-permanent-state-scheduling-concentrated-synthetic-7oh-otc-screening-nearly-40-local-bans], and two days after state agents executed 14 search warrants in the Jackson metro area, [KNT reported on November 4][mississippi-department-of-public-safety-operation-preferred-purp-14-search-warrants-madison-rankin-hinds-november-2-200-pounds-kratom-labeled-products-mgm-15-mgm-16-mgpi-800-pounds-thc-dea-hsi-luckey-bailey-wjtv-supertalk].

## Rebecca Strang's testimony

Rebecca Strang, identified by Mississippi Today as being from Atlanta, told lawmakers that "all kratom products need to be banned," according to that outlet. Magnolia Tribune reported that she told the committee she had "44 days of experience with kratom" because her son Robert had died 44 days earlier, and that the product found where he died contained MGM-15, MGM-16, and MGPI. "The Smax Mango Breeze that Robert purchased in Oxford on September 20th tested positive for MGM 15, and MGI. My son was dead the next day," she said, according to Magnolia Tribune. "Why was it still on the shelf?"

Strang argued that the DEA's August scheduling should have meant products containing those compounds were off the shelf before her son walked into the store, and she brought two products she said she bought within two blocks of the Capitol that each contained at least one of the scheduled compounds, Magnolia Tribune reported. "How do I have easier access to kratom, a defined opioid, than I do to pseudoephedrine?" she asked, according to the outlet. The second Oxford death the same day, that of 18-year-old Aiden Hamilton, also involved a synthetic kratom product, Magnolia Tribune reported. The federal case arising from those deaths produced a not-guilty plea from Islam Mosleh on October 29, [KNT reported][islam-mosleh-pleads-not-guilty-federal-possession-intent-to-distribute-grand-jury-indictment-oxford-mississippi-500-units-synthetic-kratom-yahya-mosleh-no-plea-wreg].

## The state health officer's ask

When Waldo asked State Health Officer Daniel Edney what he wanted to say, he answered, "I think you just heard it," referring to Strang's testimony, according to Magnolia Tribune. Edney told the committee that manufacturers' repeated changes to the molecular makeup of products have created loopholes around existing bans while increasing potency, and that the higher-dose synthetic products are the ones sold in convenience stores and vape shops, the outlet reported. "Can we all not agree that at the very least that we must do is get this stuff out of the gas stations and the vape stores?" Edney asked, according to Magnolia Tribune.

Edney asked the committee to consider a law giving his office authority to place emergency temporary bans on new products as they appear, which would give the Legislature time to act, Magnolia Tribune reported. Edney had previously asked for shelf-removal authority during the 2026 session, when several scheduling bills died, [KNT reported on October 25][mississippi-rep-lee-yancey-synthetic-kratom-ban-bill-controlled-substance-senate-reluctant-boyd-shelf-removal-authority-edney-schedule-iii-desoto-county-draft-ordinance-november-2]. Edney said kratom overdoses in toxicology reports have escalated mainly within the last five years, according to Magnolia Tribune, and the outlet reported that he advised anyone dealing with chronic pain or withdrawal symptoms to consult their physician.

## Police and the narcotics bureau

Oxford Police Chief Jeff McCutchen, who Mississippi Today identified as president of the Mississippi Association of Chiefs of Police, told the committee the 2025 law was insufficient because it sets fines of up to $1,000 for selling a restricted product and requires officers to test products for compliance, Mississippi Today reported. McCutchen said his investigators must buy each suspect product and pay a lab to test it, and that "every product is going to test different," according to Magnolia Tribune. "Financially, we can't do that. We can't keep up with that in the labs," he said.

McCutchen cited a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention report that he said found 14,449 kratom-related overdose exposures from 2015 to 2025, with 3,334 in 2025 alone, Magnolia Tribune reported. He called for removing all kratom products from shelves, license suspensions, civil penalties, and escalated penalties for sales to minors, according to the outlet. "We can't build a system where the burden falls primarily on law enforcement to say, 'You have to repeatedly catch a business selling these dangerous products.' It's set up for us to fail," he said, according to Magnolia Tribune.

Col. Phillip Pope, director of the Mississippi Bureau of Narcotics, agreed that enforcement requires buying products from gas stations and sending them to laboratories with taxpayer dollars, Mississippi Today reported. Pope called for an outright ban on all kratom products and compared them to the methamphetamine and spice products of past epidemics, according to Magnolia Tribune.

## The convenience store association's position

Philip Chamblee, executive director of the Mississippi Petroleum Marketers and Convenience Stores Association, told the committee the association's board voted unanimously the previous week to support a statewide ban on the sale of all kratom products, Mississippi Today reported. Magnolia Tribune's account did not mention Chamblee's testimony. A retail trade group endorsing a ban on a product category its members sell is a notable position, and neither outlet reported the board's reasoning.

## The natural-leaf case and the registry proposal

Sheldon Bradshaw, an attorney representing Botanicals for Better Health and Wellness and a former chief counsel of the Food and Drug Administration, told the committee that natural forms of kratom have been unfairly associated with the Oxford deaths and that synthetic derivatives market themselves to suggest they are natural products, Mississippi Today reported. "The snake oil salesmen want people to believe they're kratom, but they're not kratom," Bradshaw said, according to that outlet. According to Magnolia Tribune, Bradshaw said his organization supports bans on synthetic products but wants to avoid an outright ban on all kratom, and that he acknowledged risks with botanicals and said his group "strongly encourages state and local governments to put in place regulations and guardrails."

Bradshaw proposed a registry of retailers authorized to sell whole leaf, crushed or powdered leaf, and full-spectrum extract products, according to Magnolia Tribune. Under his proposal, registered retailers would pay a fee and provide third-party lab testing for heavy metals and pesticides, products would be made in FDA-inspected facilities with warning labels, including warnings about use with other botanicals or prescription drugs and during pregnancy, labels would carry dose information and a QR code to testing data, and minors would be barred, the outlet reported. Bradshaw argued the system would relieve law enforcement of testing each product, according to Magnolia Tribune. Magnolia Tribune reported that Bradshaw described natural kratom as containing between 0.003 and 0.004 percent mitragynine while synthetic forms can be as high as 98 percent; the outlet did not say whether those figures referred to the leaf's alkaloid content or to a product's total composition.

A kratom user also testified. Mississippi Today identified her as Sherry Owings, 63, while Magnolia Tribune spelled the name Sherry Owens; the outlets agree that she described using natural kratom for about nine years after changes to her prescription pain medications and asked lawmakers to focus any ban on synthetic products. "I don't want to have to go backwards," she said, according to Magnolia Tribune. "No one even knew what kratom was until this synthetic stuff started coming out in these gas stations."

## Testing and treatment testimony

Ikhlas Khan, director of the National Center for Natural Products Research at the University of Mississippi, whose name Magnolia Tribune rendered as "Icklas A. Khan," told the committee his team has tested products in the state since 2014 and found processes that increase the potency of the natural plant by 50 to 100 times, according to Magnolia Tribune. "This is not about any plant, it's about selling these designer drugs," Khan said, according to the outlet. Dr. Damon Darsey, an emergency physician, told the committee that typical hospital drug screens cannot detect the unscheduled compounds and that naloxone has no effect on the synthetic products, Magnolia Tribune reported; he said he suspects overdoses tied to synthetic kratom products are undercounted as a result.

Wendy Bailey, executive director of the Mississippi Department of Mental Health, said the agency's helpline has seen a sharp increase in calls over the last two months from people experiencing addiction to kratom, Mississippi Today reported. According to Magnolia Tribune, Bailey proposed a public education campaign aimed at young people and said, "Natural does not always mean safe."

Mississippi Today described the broader debate as one in which critics call kratom highly addictive and difficult to regulate while advocates argue it can be a tool for treating opioid use disorder, chronic pain, and depression; the outlet attributed those positions to the two camps rather than to named witnesses. Neither outlet reported that the committee took any vote, set a bill number, or scheduled a further hearing.
