The Pocatello, Idaho City Council voted on November 6, 2026 to direct city staff to proceed with a proposed ordinance that would make it unlawful to sell or offer kratom products within city limits, [the Idaho State Journal reported](https://www.idahostatejournal.com/psa/pocatello-takes-step-toward-banning-kratom-sales-as-families-doctors-describe-addiction-and-death/article_717ead7b-3d30-48ef-afcc-082aa2a21bc1.html). According to the Journal, the ordinance would not criminalize possession or personal use, the council did not vote on the ordinance itself, and Mayor Mark Dahlquist did not announce a date for that vote. The proposed ordinance as described by the Journal covers kratom as a category. It does not distinguish concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products from natural leaf kratom, a distinction the Justice Department drew in [its August 25, 2026 emergency scheduling announcement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-emergency-scheduling-three-potent-opioid-compounds), which described that federal action as directed at manufactured and concentrated opioid products rather than traditional botanical kratom. According to the Journal, the distinction came up only in testimony.

## What the council did

Councilmember Hayden Paulsen made the motion to direct staff to proceed and Councilmember Dakota Bates seconded it, after about 25 minutes of public testimony, the Journal reported. The Journal did not report a vote count, penalties, an effective date, or a schedule for readings of the ordinance.

Mallarie Bascom, the Pocatello Police Department's licensing enforcement officer, told the council the goal is to reduce the routine availability of an unregulated psychoactive product, according to the Journal. Bascom said Idaho has no consistent standards for testing, labeling, manufacturing, or age limits, and that a licensing system with age limits and inspections would not work because the city cannot verify product contents or label accuracy. Bascom cited state figures of 124 kratom-related deaths in Idaho since 2020, nine of them in Bannock County, and said poison center reports involving kratom rose about 1,200 percent between 2015 and 2025. The Journal's account does not say whether those state figures distinguish natural leaf kratom from concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products.

Bascom described the federal emergency scheduling of concentrated 7-OH as "a step in the right direction" that creates no retail framework, and said Bannock County commissioners plan their own ban once their language is finalized, the Journal reported. Police Chief Roger Schei told the council that state legislation was addressed last year but did not make it out of committee, that waiting on the state is no guarantee, and that he did not want another family to suffer a loss while the city waited, according to the Journal.

## The testimony

Eight speakers supported the ordinance, many describing losses in their families or community, the Journal reported. Tia Rustici of Meridian said her 23-year-old daughter, Kylie, died of acute mitragynine intoxication after becoming addicted to powdered kratom she used to manage a chronic illness, according to the Journal. Danae Jones of Pocatello said her niece died after using natural kratom powder and asked the council not to leave loopholes that would keep any kratom products on shelves, the Journal reported. Neither speaker, as quoted by the Journal, described a concentrated 7-OH product; Rustici referred to powdered kratom and Jones to natural kratom powder.

Dr. Ryan Hope, a pain and addiction specialist who the Journal said has practiced in Pocatello and Blackfoot for 19 years, said about 25 percent of opioid-addiction patients at his clinic over roughly the past year were addicted to kratom, and that some patients spent $100 to $150 a day, according to the Journal. Hope also said some patients benefit, the Journal reported. The Journal also reported testimony from Pocatello nurse Angela Brush, who described kratom withdrawal as comparable to opioid withdrawal; from Jim Abel, who said a family member had two seizures while trying to quit; and from Brooklyn Jones, who the Journal said works in a lab screening umbilical cord tissue and lost a cousin to kratom toxicity, and who said maternal use has been associated with neonatal withdrawal. Becky Latham, executive director of Community Youth in Action, said Bonneville County residents would drive to Pocatello to buy kratom if the city did not act, and resident Michelle York said a city ban would not stop purchases elsewhere but would raise awareness, the Journal reported.

## The opposition

Pat Solier, who the Journal said represents the Idaho-based company Animal Farm, opposed the ordinance as written. Solier said the federal government found that kratom itself did not meet the scheduling standard while synthetic products did, that he expected a federal ban on products such as 7-OH on November 13, 2026, and that Idaho faces a patchwork of at least seven local standards, according to the Journal. Solier urged the council to remove synthetics but wait on state action regarding natural leaf kratom, which the Journal said lawmakers have indicated they will take up next session. Pocatello resident Joyce Bybee said a sales ban would mostly affect business owners because people could still obtain the product elsewhere, and questioned whether a city is the right body to decide what may be sold, the Journal reported.

The Drug Enforcement Administration's notice of intent to temporarily place 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above a specified threshold in Schedule I was scheduled for publication in the Federal Register on October 14, 2026, [Kratom News Today (KNT) reported on October 10, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14]. According to that KNT report, the statute the notice cites, 21 U.S.C. 811(h), bars a temporary order until 30 days after publication, which the report said puts the earliest date on or after November 13, 2026. The American Kratom Association said in an October 9, 2026 statement that it applauded the DEA and the Justice Department for what it called targeted scheduling of 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, according to that KNT report. The Journal did not report any statement from the association or from the Global Kratom Coalition on the Pocatello proposal.

## Idaho's local patchwork

The Journal reported that Idaho Falls, Blackfoot, and Bingham County have already adopted restrictions. Idaho Falls banned kratom sales in June 2026 after the Bonneville County coroner attributed six deaths to kratom, [KNT reported on June 8, 2026][idaho-falls-kratom-ban-botanic-tonics-wrongful-death]; according to that report, the coroner's statements did not specify whether the products were concentrated or synthetic 7-OH preparations or natural leaf kratom. Coeur d'Alene took a different approach in September 2026, adopting an ordinance that requires sellers to obtain a city permit, bars sales to anyone under 21, and prohibits products above two percent total 7-OH or one milligram of 7-OH per serving, while leaving natural leaf kratom on sale to adults, [KNT reported on September 16, 2026][coeur-dalene-idaho-council-kratom-ordinance-21-plus-permit-2-percent-7oh-cap]. The Journal noted that the Food and Drug Administration has not approved kratom for medical use.
