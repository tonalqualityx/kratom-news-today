A group of Oregon legislators is working on a bill for the 2027 session that would ban sales of concentrated 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and other opioid-like substances derived from kratom, [The Lund Report reported on November 3, 2026](https://www.thelundreport.org/content/oregon-lawmakers-local-officials-eye-curbs-kratom) in a story by Lynne Terry that the [Lincoln Chronicle republished on November 7](https://lincolnchronicle.org/oregon-lawmakers-local-officials-eye-curbs-for-kratom/). State Rep. Jules Waters, a West Linn Democrat, is leading the group, which according to the report is still drafting the language. The products at issue are concentrated 7-OH pills and similar derivatives sold in convenience stores, which the report distinguished from the kratom leaf itself, where 7-OH occurs only as a minor constituent.

## The legislative effort

"What we're seeing in other states is people attacking it at the local government level," Waters told The Lund Report. "But the problem with that is it's not uniform. So some sort of statewide inclusive regulation or a ban needs to happen." The group includes Sen. Lisa Reynolds, a Portland Democrat and pediatrician, and Rep. Matt Bunch, a Beavercreek Republican appointed to the seat vacated by Rep. Christine Drazan, who is running for governor, according to the report. "This is not a partisan issue," Waters said.

Waters said she hopes to pass a ban and would otherwise seek much tighter restrictions, The Lund Report reported. Oregon's existing law, which took effect in 2023, requires kratom processors to register with the state and sets a minimum purchase age of 21, according to the report; Waters said that is not enough. "If somebody in a mini-market is not checking ID on this stuff, then it's accessible for children, and that's not acceptable to me," she said.

Waters told The Lund Report she expects opposition from small convenience stores and gas station owners, who she said see large profits from kratom. Richard Kosesan, executive director of the Oregon Neighborhood Store Association, said the group's board has not discussed kratom. "There'll be some pushback if it's an outright ban, if it is more regulatory in nature, in terms of age restriction and sales restriction, I think that's a different matter," Kosesan said, according to the report. He added that many stores, including in the convenience sector, choose not to carry it. The report did not include comment from kratom manufacturers or from the American Kratom Association or other advocacy groups, so their position on the Oregon proposal is not established in this reporting.

## Coos County's proposed ordinance

Coos County Commissioner Drew Farmer proposed an ordinance at the end of October that would ban sales of kratom derivatives in the county, The Lund Report reported. The draft reads in part: "To protect the public health and reduce the availability of these concerning opioid-like products, no person shall sell, offer for sale, distribute or otherwise provide within Coos County any synthetic, semisynthetic, derivative or concentrated kratom product," according to the report. Farmer said he had not encountered opposition and that fellow commissioners want cities in the county to adopt matching ordinances so the rules are uniform. Coos County commissioners held a work session on a draft sales ban on October 1, 2026, at which the county's public health director said the concern was synthesized high-potency 7-OH products, [Kratom News Today (KNT) reported on October 3, 2026][coos-county-oregon-commissioners-work-session-ordinance-ban-kratom-sales-unincorporated-areas-lynch-synthetic-emergency-rooms].

The Lund Report's story was built around Keenan Neal, a 43-year-old Coos County resident living in sober housing who said a friend offered him a kratom pill for a toothache in February and that he moved to 7-OH products a month later. Neal described withdrawal that was "just like withdrawing from heroin" and said he is now on Suboxone, according to the report. Jesse Dorfmeister, a nurse practitioner at North Bend Medical Center in Coos Bay who specializes in addiction treatment, told The Lund Report he has about three dozen patients who became dependent on kratom products, roughly one-eighth of his caseload, and that they only began appearing in his practice in the past two years.

## National figures cited

The Lund Report cited a March 2026 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention report counting more than 230 deaths linked directly to kratom between 2015 and 2025, and a 1,200 percent increase in poison center cases over the same period, along with a Wall Street Journal report that kratom was detected in 5,200 overdose deaths between 2020 and 2024. The report also cited Nutritional Outlook's figure of more than $440 million in 2024 kratom sales, up more than 20 percent, and the Rockefeller Institute of Government's count of about a dozen states that ban or tightly restrict kratom or products like 7-OH. The CDC and poison center figures as cited do not distinguish natural leaf kratom from concentrated 7-OH products.

On the federal side, the report noted that the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) withdrew a 2016 plan to schedule both mitragynine and 7-OH after public opposition, and that its current notice covers 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl above a threshold, with restrictions possible as early as November 13, 2026. KNT reported on the threshold approach and the October 14 Federal Register notices [on October 10, 2026][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14].
