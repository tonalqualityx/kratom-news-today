Dr. Michael Marlin, director of the Mississippi Poison Center, said naloxone should be given in every case of respiratory depression, including when a kratom product is suspected, [Magnolia Tribune reported](https://magnoliatribune.com/2026/11/12/mississippi-poison-center-director-advises-the-use-of-naloxone-in-all-overdoses-including-kratom/) on November 12, 2026. Magnolia Tribune reported that a recent legislative Drug Policy Committee hearing included concern, from sources the outlet did not name, that naloxone could be ineffective in an overdose involving a kratom product. Marlin separated botanical leaf products from newer derivatives, telling Magnolia Tribune that the derivatives, which the outlet listed as dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, MGM-15, and MGM-16, are the greater concern. According to the Justice Department's [August 25, 2026 announcement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-emergency-scheduling-three-potent-opioid-compounds), the federal temporary scheduling of MGM-15, MGM-16, and a third compound was directed at manufactured and concentrated opioid products rather than traditional botanical kratom.

## The basis Marlin gave

Marlin pointed to a controlled study in rats that were given morphine, mitragynine, and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), and said naloxone reversed the effects of all three, Magnolia Tribune reported. The outlet did not name the study or its authors.

"Naloxone should be administered in every case of respiratory depression, irrespective of what they think might be going on," Marlin said, according to Magnolia Tribune.

Marlin told the outlet that mitragynine acts at the mu-opioid receptor, the target of morphine and other opioids, and that naloxone binds to that receptor strongly enough to displace most drugs. Marlin said the same reversal applies to 7-OH, according to the outlet. Magnolia Tribune described 7-OH as a synthetic form of kratom. The outlet's description does not separate the trace 7-OH present in kratom leaf from the concentrated 7-OH products sold at retail.

Magnolia Tribune described the medical research on naloxone in kratom overdoses as limited.

## Newer compounds and larger doses

Marlin told Magnolia Tribune of a suspicion that MGM-15 and MGM-16 are more potent than older derivatives. According to the outlet's account of Marlin's remarks, cases involving dihydro-7-hydroxymitragynine, MGM-15, or MGM-16 may call for more naloxone than other cases. When naloxone does not work, responders have to control the airway, Marlin said, according to the outlet.

Magnolia Tribune reported that MGM-15 and MGM-16 can still be found at some retailers after their federal scheduling. The outlet also reported, without naming who holds the suspicion, that the two compounds are suspected to have been involved in the deaths of two University of Mississippi students in September 2026. The federal scheduling was aimed at concentrated products, according to the Justice Department announcement cited above, and Magnolia Tribune's November 12, 2026 report does not specify whether the products in the students' deaths were concentrated or synthetic preparations or natural leaf kratom. Rebecca Strang, the mother of one of the students, told the Mississippi House Drug Policy Committee on November 4, 2026 that the product her son bought tested positive for MGM-15 and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), [Kratom News Today (KNT) reported on November 6, 2026][mississippi-house-drug-policy-committee-hearing-waldo-rebecca-strang-all-kratom-banned-edney-emergency-ban-authority-mccutchen-pope-chamblee-convenience-stores-bradshaw-registry-magnolia-tribune-mississippi-today], citing Magnolia Tribune and Mississippi Today.

## Marlin's view of a ban

Marlin gave Magnolia Tribune a mixed answer on whether lawmakers or the federal government should ban all kratom products. According to the outlet, Marlin listed risks with kratom products that include a lack of standardized dosages and of quality assurance, and said botanical leaf products have in the past been contaminated with metals and, in one case, salmonella. Marlin said there is little evidence that leaf products used alone have resulted in overdoses, according to the outlet. Marlin also warned, the outlet reported, that leaf products can contribute to an overdose when used with other opioids, alcohol, or sedating substances.

Marlin also told the outlet that people who use botanical leaf products would not stop using drugs if those products went away, and expressed concern that they would shift to fentanyl or other illicit opioids. A federal Schedule I classification for kratom would hinder research into its effects, Marlin said, according to Magnolia Tribune. Marlin agreed that synthetic products should be banned, citing their unknown chemical composition and the lack of research on them, the outlet reported.

The Oxford police chief and the director of the Mississippi Bureau of Narcotics called for a ban on all kratom products at the November 4, 2026 hearing, while attorney Sheldon Bradshaw of Botanicals for Better Health and Wellness supported a ban on synthetic products and proposed a registry of retailers selling tested leaf products, KNT reported on November 6, 2026.
