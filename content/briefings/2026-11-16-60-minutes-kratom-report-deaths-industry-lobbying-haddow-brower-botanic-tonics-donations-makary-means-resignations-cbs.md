CBS News' 60 Minutes [aired a report](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/kratom-60-minutes-transcript/) on November 15, 2026 that examined deaths attributed to kratom, the spread of concentrated synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products, and the money the industry has spent in Washington. Correspondent Jon Wertheim reported the segment, which CBS described as the first part of a two-week report on the opioid crisis. The deaths the program described were attributed to mitragynine, the main alkaloid in kratom leaf, according to CBS. The program treated synthetic 7-OH tablets and shots as a separate and more potent product category.

## The deaths CBS described

Four parents told 60 Minutes that their sons died after using kratom. In a [companion article](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/parents-whose-sons-died-from-kratom-use-warn-about-substance-60-minutes/), CBS reported that two of the deaths were attributed solely to mitragynine and that the other two involved mitragynine along with prescription or over-the-counter medications. CBS did not attribute any of the four deaths to a concentrated or synthetic 7-OH product.

Justin Brower, a toxicologist at NMS Labs, a forensic testing facility outside Philadelphia, told the program he has seen "dozens and dozens of deaths attributed just to kratom, no other drugs at all." Brower estimated that deaths linked to kratom have risen about tenfold since 2014. CBS reported that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention detected kratom in more than 5,000 overdose deaths from 2020 to 2024.

Brower was responding to a statement by Mac Haddow, whom CBS called the public voice of the American Kratom Association (AKA). The program played a clip of Haddow at a June 2026 congressional briefing saying, "There is no known dose of mitragynine or kratom that would cause a death." Asked about that claim, Brower said, "Yeah, that's not true at all."

The AKA gave 60 Minutes a written statement that, according to CBS, distinguished natural kratom from "dangerous chemically manipulated products." The association has taken the same position on federal scheduling: it sued the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) over the reach of an August order and has supported controls on concentrated 7-OH, as Kratom News Today (KNT) [reported in October][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14].

## Leaf kratom and synthetic 7-OH

The segment described the two categories separately. According to CBS, mitragynine activates opioid receptors and the body converts some of it into 7-OH, which acts on those receptors more intensely. The program said products containing synthetic 7-OH, sold as tablets and liquid shots, have since reached the market. Alex Krotulski, a toxicologist who directs the Center for Forensic Science Research and Education, described the move from mitragynine to 7-OH as a shift to "something ten times more potent."

Melanie Clark, a Michigan woman interviewed for the segment, told CBS she became dependent on Feel Free, a kratom and kava drink made by Botanic Tonics, within about three weeks. CBS reported that she later relapsed and began using synthetic 7-OH tablets made by a different company, which put her in the hospital.

Dr. Andrew Kolodny, medical director of the Opioid Policy Research Collaborative at Brandeis University, told the program that "the effects produced by kratom are very similar to the effects produced by oxycodone, heroin, even fentanyl." CBS also cited the Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) warning that kratom carries a "risk of serious adverse events, including liver toxicity, seizures, and substance use disorder."

## Lobbying, donations, and two resignations

60 Minutes reported that it found public filings from the last decade showing millions of dollars committed to lobbying the FDA, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), the White House, and Congress, much of it from the AKA. CBS did not give an exact total. The program also reported that Botanic Tonics and its founder, J.W. Ross, donated nearly $1.2 million to Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s campaigns and the Make America Healthy Again political action committee. Botanic Tonics did not respond to 60 Minutes, according to CBS.

CBS reported that Markwayne Mullin, whom it identified as secretary of the Department of Homeland Security, has disclosed an investment in Botanic Tonics of up to $1 million. Mullin confirmed the investment and denied lobbying, the program said.

The federal government announced in July its intent to schedule 7-OH and three related substances, CBS reported, and "last month, HHS changed its recommendation, increasing the amount of 7-OH allowed in products by 20-fold." KNT's [reading of the October notice][hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14] put the new concentration threshold of 1 milligram per gram at 0.1% by weight, against 0.05% in the July notice, and noted that the new threshold language dropped the July notice's per-unit cap. The two figures measure the change differently.

Dr. Marty Makary, the former head of the FDA, and Calley Means, a former senior adviser at HHS, both told 60 Minutes they recently resigned from their posts, in part because of the kratom policy changes, according to CBS. Both said the changes were shaped by business interests, the program reported. It played a clip of Means on Joe Rogan's podcast saying, "The amount of lobbying on this was unrelenting."

HHS declined an interview and sent a statement, CBS reported: "Neither political contributions nor outside pressure influenced our recommendation. Our responsibility is to protect Americans, and that is exactly what we did." The [DEA notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/public-inspection/2026-20943/schedules-of-controlled-substances-temporary-placement-of-7--hydroxymitragynine-and-mitragynine) that carried the October threshold states that "neither HHS nor DEA has made a finding that these substances are safe to use at below-threshold levels."
