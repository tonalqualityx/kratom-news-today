The American Kratom Association (AKA) asked CBS on November 17, 2026 to correct what the group called major errors in the 60 Minutes report on kratom that aired on November 15, 2026, according to an [AKA release](https://www.americankratom.org/news/aka-demands-cbs-60-minutes-correct-major-errors). The Global Kratom Coalition (GKC) published [its own response](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/global-kratom-coalition-reacts-60-231500218.html) the same day, as did [Botanic Tonics](https://www.reuters.com/press-releases/botanic-tonics-responds-60-minutes-kratom-7-oh-2026-11-17/), a company that sells a kava and kratom tonic. All three releases argue that the broadcast did not separate natural kratom leaf, which contains 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) in trace amounts, from concentrated and synthetic 7-OH products. Kratom News Today (KNT) [covered the broadcast][60-minutes-kratom-report-deaths-industry-lobbying-haddow-brower-botanic-tonics-donations-makary-means-resignations-cbs] on November 16, 2026.

The statements are the organizations' own characterizations of the broadcast. The AKA and GKC releases do not report a response from CBS. The [CBS transcript](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/kratom-60-minutes-transcript/) of the segment is public.

## The dispute over "twentyfold"

Both advocacy groups dispute a statement in the broadcast that federal officials raised the permitted 7-OH level twentyfold. According to the AKA release, the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) originally proposed a threshold of 0.5 milligrams per gram and the October 2026 revision set it at 1 milligram per gram, which the AKA calls a twofold increase. "The mathematical error is serious," Mac Haddow, the AKA's senior fellow on public policy, said in the release. The GKC release describes the same change as a move from 0.05 percent to 0.1 percent.

Both releases also state that the revised proposal added a second test: the ratio of mitragynine to 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI) cannot fall below 100 to 1. The GKC argues in its release that the ratio can make the October 2026 version more restrictive than the earlier one for some products. The AKA says in its release that it sent these figures to CBS four days before the broadcast.

Members of Congress read the same change differently. KNT [reported on November 17, 2026][grassley-seven-republican-senators-letter-dea-fda-explain-reconsider-7oh-mgpi-threshold-collins-scott-crapo-cassidy-moran-capito-risch] that eight Republican senators, in a letter described by Sen. Chuck Grassley's office, called the combined threshold "inappropriately high and unsound."

## What else the AKA asked CBS to address

The AKA release lists several items the group says the broadcast left out:

- An NMS Labs advisory that, according to the AKA, warns that older or limited toxicology methods may miss 7-OH and MGPI. The AKA argues that deaths attributed to mitragynine alone may therefore need reassessment.
- A June 1, 2026 announcement by the National Institutes of Health that, according to the AKA, an investigational new drug application for purified mitragynine took effect with the Food and Drug Administration, permitting Phase I human safety research.
- An eight-page letter the AKA says it sent CBS on November 11, 2026, along with an offer of an on-air interview that the group says CBS did not take up.

The AKA release also asks CBS to distinguish between deaths associated with kratom and deaths in which natural kratom was shown to be a cause. The release discloses that the AKA spent more than $14 million on state and federal lobbying from 2016 through 2025 and says the broadcast gave no comparable accounting for manufacturers of concentrated 7-OH products.

## The GKC and Botanic Tonics responses

The GKC release states that the broadcast interviewed no natural kratom consumer and no researcher running federally funded kratom studies. The release cites Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data showing 5,208 overdose deaths from 2020 through 2024 in which mitragynine was detected, and the GKC argues that detection does not establish causation. The GKC also disputes the broadcast's figure for how much mitragynine the body converts into 7-OH. The release puts the figure at one to five percent, compared with roughly 20 percent in the broadcast as the GKC describes it.

The GKC release does not argue against regulation. "Natural kratom leaf is not risk-free and should be regulated," the release states, and it calls for adult-only sales, independent testing, and labeling for natural leaf alongside restrictions on concentrated synthetic 7-OH.

Botanic Tonics said in its release that overdose statistics based on mitragynine detection do not show whether a person consumed natural leaf, an extract, a concentrate, or several substances together, and the company called for overdose reporting by product type. The company stated that its tonic contains natural kratom leaf and no concentrated synthetic 7-OH. That description is the company's own, and KNT has not tested the product.
