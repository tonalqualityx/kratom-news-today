New York Gov. Kathy Hochul signed legislation on November 23, 2026 that prohibits the sale of products containing concentrated or synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), according to a [release from the governor's office](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-legislation-protect-new-yorkers-against-harmful-effects-concentrated-and). The products at issue are concentrated and laboratory-made 7-OH preparations, which are distinct from the trace 7-OH that occurs naturally in kratom leaf. The release states that the law, S8925A/A9156B, bars sales of products in which 7-OH exceeds two percent of total alkaloids or one milligram per serving. Other kratom products may still be sold, the [Albany Times Union reported](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/gas-station-heroin-york-bans-180407868.html). The Legislature passed the bill in June, as Kratom News Today (KNT) [reported at the time][new-york-legislature-passes-synthetic-kratom-7oh-ban].

## What the law does

According to the governor's release, businesses that knowingly violate the law face civil penalties of up to $500 for a first violation and $1,000 for each later one. The release states that money recovered goes to the New York State Drug Treatment and Public Education Fund.

A [release from Sen. Christopher J. Ryan](https://www.nysenate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026/christopher-j-ryan/senator-ryans-bill-ban-sale-7-oh-products-signed), the Senate sponsor, states that the penalties apply to businesses that knowingly sell the products and do not apply to people who are addicted. The release names Assemblymember Phil Steck as the Assembly sponsor.

The governor's release gives no effective date. [The Daily Gazette](https://www.dailygazette.com/cjfund/health/new-york-bans-synthetic-kratom/article_63cc181e-836b-45c2-88a4-73f463409178.html) and the Times Union both reported that the law takes effect in 90 days.

Accounts of the limit differ in one respect. The governor's release and the Daily Gazette describe two tests, the two percent share of total alkaloids and the one milligram per serving ceiling. A [Spectrum News report](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/nys/central-ny/news/2026/11/23/hochul-signs-law-restricting-sale-of-7-oh-in-kratom) describes only the per-serving limit.

## How the state describes the products

The governor's release states that naturally occurring kratom products generally contain 7-OH at low levels, while many products sold at gas stations and smoke shops contain stronger, laboratory-made 7-OH in much higher amounts. The release links those stronger products to opioid-like effects, dependence, and withdrawal.

"The concentrated 7-OH products being banned today have the potential to create dependency, withdrawal and other adverse health effects," state Health Commissioner Dr. James McDonald said in the release.

Steck said in the release that 7-OH products are 13 times more potent than morphine, and that the issue came to his attention after the November 2024 death of Ballston Spa resident Nick Scribner. The Times Union reported that Steck first sought to ban all kratom after Scribner's death.

The release states that the law builds on two bills Hochul signed in 2025, which barred kratom sales to people under 21 and required ingredient and warning labels. It also states that kratom is not federally regulated or approved by the Food and Drug Administration, and that the Drug Enforcement Administration has announced its intent to publish a temporary order placing 7-OH above a specified threshold in Schedule I.

## Where advocates and critics stand

Kratom trade and advocacy groups backed the narrower approach. Matthew Lowe, executive director of the Global Kratom Coalition, told the Daily Gazette that the state had "drawn a clear line between natural kratom leaf and the novel synthetic tablets being sold fraudulently under its name." The Times Union reported that industry groups including the American Kratom Association had urged lawmakers to ban only the synthesized, concentrated versions.

Cari Scribner, Nick Scribner's mother, wants the state to go further. She told the Daily Gazette she was "more than a little disappointed" that the law does not cover botanical kratom. "I recognize this law is important, but I see it as only the first step," she said. Scribner told the Times Union she hopes the state will align with counties that have adopted broader bans.

Those county measures reach natural leaf kratom as well. The Daily Gazette reported that since August, Albany, Saratoga, Rensselaer, and Warren counties and the town of Amsterdam have moved to ban both botanical kratom and synthetic 7-OH, and that Gloversville is weighing a similar ban. KNT [reported on November 15][upstate-new-york-counties-kratom-sales-bans-hamilton-county-november-5-columbia-tables-herkimer-warren-scribner-carlucci-times-union] on the spread of those county bans.

## Poison center figures

The Times Union reported that the Upstate New York Poison Center, which covers 54 counties, recorded 47 kratom-linked cases in 2025 and 122 so far in 2026. William Eggleston, a clinical toxicologist at the center, told the newspaper the numbers are "likely an under-representation." The Times Union's account does not break those cases down by whether they involved concentrated or synthetic 7-OH products or natural leaf kratom.
