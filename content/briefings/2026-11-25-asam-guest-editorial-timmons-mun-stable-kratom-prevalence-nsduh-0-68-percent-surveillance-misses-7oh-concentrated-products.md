National survey data that show steady kratom use may be missing a change in what people are using, according to [a guest editorial the American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) published](https://www.asam.org/news/detail/2026/11/24/guest-editorial---stable-prevalence-in-a-rapidly-changing-kratom-marketplace--what-national-surveillance-might-be-missing) on November 24, 2026. Authors Patricia Timmons and Chung Jung Mun wrote that past-year kratom use among U.S. adults remained relatively stable at approximately 0.68 percent from 2021 through 2023 in the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH). The survey asks broadly about kratom, the authors wrote, and does not separate whole-leaf products from concentrated extracts or from 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16. The editorial describes those four as highly concentrated and newer synthetic or semi-synthetic products, and the authors wrote that the products may carry substantially different risks than traditional whole-leaf kratom.

## The study behind the editorial

Timmons is a registered nurse and postdoctoral fellow at the University of New Mexico's Center on Alcohol, Substance use, and Addictions. Mun is a clinical psychologist and associate professor at Arizona State University's Edson College of Nursing and Health Innovation. The editorial draws on the pair's own analysis of NSDUH data, which its reference list places in the Journal of Addiction Medicine in 2026 with a third author, M. Cano. The ASAM page states no funding sources or conflicts of interest for the authors.

Use was uneven across the adult population, according to the editorial. The authors wrote that past-year kratom use exceeded two percent among adults reporting past-year serious psychological distress or a major depressive episode, exceeded five percent among subgroups reporting illicit substance use or prescription medication misuse, and was highest among adults reporting past-year ketamine use. The editorial gives no year-by-year figures or sample sizes.

## What the authors say the survey cannot show

NSDUH cannot say how often respondents use kratom, how much, which products, or why, Timmons and Mun wrote. The authors noted that the survey describes kratom as available in forms such as powder, pills, or leaf, and that it does not specifically capture concentrated extracts, ready-to-drink beverages, or the four named compounds. The editorial says some products are marketed as kratom even though, in the authors' account, they contain concentrated or chemically modified compounds that differ substantially from botanical kratom.

"The available products are changing faster than the terminology used to measure them," the authors wrote. The authors wrote that the marketplace, the products, and patterns of use may have changed even as the estimate held steady.

Timmons and Mun also cite a 2026 national poison center study in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report. According to the authors, that study found a 1,200 percent increase in kratom-related exposure events between 2015 and 2025, with a substantial increase in 2025. The editorial does not specify whether those exposures involved natural leaf kratom or concentrated 7-OH products. The study is described here only as the editorial describes it.

## Federal scheduling, as the editorial describes it

The authors wrote that the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) announced its intent to temporarily place all four compounds in Schedule I of the Controlled Substances Act. According to the editorial, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, and MGM-16 entered Schedule I as of August 26, 2026, and 7-OH remains in pending status. The DEA action is described here only as the editorial describes it. The Justice Department's [August 25, 2026 emergency scheduling announcement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-emergency-scheduling-three-potent-opioid-compounds) described that action as directed at manufactured and concentrated opioid products rather than traditional botanical kratom.

Kratom News Today (KNT) [reported on November 13, 2026][dea-7oh-mgpi-temporary-scheduling-order-could-come-as-early-as-november-13-npr-poison-center-report-holstege-epstein-wluc-michigan] that, according to [NPR](https://www.wusf.org/2026-11-13/kratom-and-7-oh-can-feel-like-a-miracle-they-can-also-be-addictive), a DEA order on some concentrated 7-OH products could come as early as that day. The ASAM editorial, published 11 days later, describes 7-OH as still pending.

## The recommendations

For researchers and public health agencies, Timmons and Mun recommend collecting product type, frequency, quantity, motivations, and co-use with other substances, and updating surveillance to capture extracts and highly concentrated products.

For clinicians, the authors recommend moving past yes-or-no screening. The editorial suggests asking patients "What does kratom do for you?" and taking a broader substance use history when use is reported. Depending on the case, the authors wrote, that may mean documentation and monitoring alone, or assessment for polysubstance use, medication interactions, mental health symptoms, dependence, or withdrawal. The authors wrote that routine assessment may be especially useful in primary care, pain, and behavioral health settings, and that kratom's psychoactive properties and interactions are incompletely understood.

The piece is an editorial, and it reports the authors' reading of their own survey analysis. It includes no position from kratom trade or advocacy organizations on the scheduling of 7-OH. In a statement [WTAE reported](https://www.wtae.com/article/fayette-county-officials-raise-alarm-over-kratom-overdose-deaths/74524272) on November 24, 2026, the American Kratom Association said it has consistently supported appropriate federal action against products containing chemically manipulated or synthetically enhanced concentrations of 7-OH, as KNT [reported in its briefing on Fayette County, Pennsylvania][fayette-county-pennsylvania-coroner-baker-three-overdose-deaths-mitragynine-only-expanded-toxicology-dea-lab-american-kratom-association-wtae].
