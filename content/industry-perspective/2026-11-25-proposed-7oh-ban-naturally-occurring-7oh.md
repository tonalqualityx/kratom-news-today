*Opinion. Mike Dion runs Kratom News Today.*

[Drink Yum](https://drinkyum.com) is a Miami company that makes a flavored kratom extract shot, sold in 30 ml and 14 ml bottles in Bubble Gum and Tropical Breeze. The product is built on a bet about 7-OH. Drink Yum puts mitragynine at the center of the shot, keeps 7-hydroxymitragynine below what its lab can measure, and posts the test results. The DEA's proposed 7-OH ban would judge products by those same two numbers.

Ivan Estrada, who handles Drink Yum's online distribution, talked with me on November 13 for a Q&A that runs separately. His partner Gunther created the product. Ivan said the shots are sold in smoke shops in Nevada, Arizona and California, and the rest goes out through the website.

## What Drink Yum puts on its lab page

The company's lab results page lists a recent lot, BC01-0045, tested by Cora Science. It shows 255 mg of mitragynine per 30 ml bottle, or 42.5 mg per serving, and 7-OH under 0.33 mg per serving, which the page marks as below the limit of quantitation. Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, the other substance in the DEA notice, also came in below that limit. Each bottle carries a lab ID that a buyer can enter on the site to get the results by email, and the company says it sends a full certificate of analysis on request.

Ivan was plain that he isn't the chemist. Asked whether there are techniques that keep 7-OH low, he said he couldn't explain how it's removed because "it's just not my area of expertise." What he could describe was the routine. "We have to lab test every batch," he said, and for each barrel the aim is to get 7-OH "below level of quantification."

He gave two reasons for building the shot around mitragynine. The first was taste. "The whole concept of Drink Yum was always just about the flavor," he said, and with leaf it was too hard to get the taste right: "the only way to get the taste right was with extract." The second was 7-OH itself. "There's so much conflict going on with 7-OH," he said, "and frankly it's just not the type of product that we want to sell."

## What the proposed 7-OH ban measures

On October 14 the DEA published a notice of intent (91 FR 65370) to temporarily place 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in Schedule I above a set threshold. A product would be covered if it holds more than 1 mg of 7-OH per gram, for solids, or per milliliter, for liquids. It would also be covered if the mass of mitragynine divided by the mass of 7-OH comes out under 100, whatever the concentration. The same two tests apply to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, and to the two substances combined when a product has both.

[That notice](/briefings/hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14/) replaced the July 6 version (91 FR 40917), which the DEA withdrew when it published the new one. The July version had its own test for botanical material. The October notice applies the same two numbers to everything it covers.

## 7-OH vs mitragynine: Drink Yum's numbers on the test

On Drink Yum's published figures, the shot sits under both lines. The site's two mitragynine numbers put six servings in a 30 ml bottle, about 5 ml each. Even at the label's ceiling of 0.33 mg of 7-OH in 5 ml, the shot would hold about 0.07 mg of 7-OH per milliliter, where the notice draws its line at 1 mg. The ratio works out to 42.5 mg of mitragynine against 0.33 mg of 7-OH, about 129 to 1, and the real ratio is higher because the 7-OH is below that ceiling. The notice's line is 100 to 1.

Those are the company's numbers from one listed lot, and Kratom News Today has not tested the product. "Below the limit of quantitation" means below what that lab's method can measure, so 0.33 mg is a ceiling set by the method and not a measured amount. That makes the ratio the tighter of the two tests. With a less sensitive method, a result reported only as "below the limit" could fail to show the ratio at all: at a ceiling of 0.43 mg per serving, the same arithmetic lands under 100.

On November 20 the 7-HOPE Alliance said it had filed [two petitions](/briefings/7-hope-alliance-files-two-dea-petitions-clarification-proposed-7oh-mgpi-temporary-schedule-i-thresholds-testing-methods-rush-subeck/) asking the DEA which lab methods it will accept and how results too close to call will be handled. The group's announcement shows no DEA response.

## Natural 7-OH has no legal category

Ivan was clear about where Drink Yum's 7-OH comes from. Asked about synthetic 7-OH, he said, "I'm not familiar with it, frankly. I see something like synthetic 7-OH and, to me, it looks a little scary." Then: "Ours isn't synthetic. Ours is natural leaf derived."

The notice doesn't sort products by where the 7-OH came from. It says 7-OH has "supposedly been found in trace amounts in some botanical kratom products," and that this is why control is limited to products above the threshold. It also says "neither HHS nor DEA has made a finding that these substances are safe to use at below-threshold levels." Leaf-derived 7-OH concentrated past 1 mg per gram would be covered. If Drink Yum stays outside the rule, its lab numbers will be the reason, and its leaf origin won't come into it.

Ivan's answer on synthetics came back to testing too:

> "I don't want to pretend I know more than I do. If it turns out that it's basically the same, like lab-grown diamonds versus natural diamonds, the same molecular structure, I wouldn't have any problem with it. But let's test it out and find out."

## Synthetic 7-OH and where Ivan would regulate

Ivan didn't object to rules as such. "It's fine to regulate it," he said, "but to just outright ban it and take the most specialized, hyper-concentrated form and attribute it to the whole thing, it's like saying poppy seed bagels are illegal." A company that sells synthetic 7-OH, in his view, is "asking to have a two, three year long business."

The October notice is closer to the regulation he describes than to an outright ban. It reaches products by concentration and ratio, and it doesn't schedule anything below those lines.

## How state 7-OH limits compare

The states measure 7-OH in different ways, so Drink Yum's figures face more than one test. Governor Kathy Hochul signed a [New York law](/briefings/hochul-signs-new-york-law-s8925a-a9156b-banning-sales-concentrated-synthetic-7oh-products-2-percent-total-alkaloids-1-mg-serving-90-days/) on November 23 that bars sales of products with 7-OH above 2 percent of total alkaloids or 1 mg per serving, and it takes effect 90 days after signing. Florida's attorney general already schedules 7-OH under an emergency rule with the same 1 mg and 100-to-1 shape as the DEA notice, through June 30, 2027. Colorado's 2025 law caps 7-OH at 2 percent of a product's alkaloid composition.

Under 0.33 mg per serving is below New York's 1 mg line. Set against the 42.5 mg of mitragynine alone, 0.33 mg is under 1 percent, so its share of all the alkaloids in the shot is smaller still. Drink Yum's site lists ten states it won't ship to, and New York, Florida and Colorado aren't among them.

## What a kratom lab report should show

My own view is short. "Naturally occurring" should retire as a selling point, because under a concentration and ratio test it tells a buyer nothing they can check. A label that gives mitragynine and 7-OH per serving, names the lab and ties the result to the lot in the buyer's hand answers the federal test and the state ones. Drink Yum's page does most of that, including the limit behind its 7-OH result. The step I'd like to see, from Drink Yum and from everyone else, is the full certificate for every lot posted where anyone can read it, instead of sent on request. Ivan said it more plainly: "I think lab reports should be something that's a little bit more common."

## Where the 7-OH rule stands today

The October document is a notice of intent. It said a temporary order would be published on or after November 13. As of November 26 the Federal Register showed no such order, so 7-OH above the threshold isn't federally scheduled yet. In a November 13 status report in the American Kratom Association's lawsuit, Justice Department attorneys wrote that the DEA still intends to publish the order and that the process "has necessitated ongoing consultations with the Department of Health and Human Services," [as KNT reported](/briefings/dea-7oh-mgpi-temporary-scheduling-order-not-issued-november-25-doj-joint-status-report-hhs-consultations-stay-december-14-aka-haddow-pain-news-network/). The notice set no comment period. Once an order is published, it takes effect that day and lasts two years, with a possible one-year extension.
