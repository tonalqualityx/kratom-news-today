*Opinion. Mike Dion runs Kratom News Today.*

I don't drink, I've never smoked, and I live in Vermont, where I couldn't buy a kratom product if I wanted one. I came to this industry sideways. An old friend I worked with back in Pennsylvania developed a new extraction method, and I found my way in through him. My own work is in web. When I started Kratom News Today, the idea was to say what's being done and what's being said, with sources, and to pull back from the "7-OH is killing everybody" headlines.

One phrase keeps coming up in that work: naturally occurring 7-OH. The friends I have in the industry treat the line between synthetic and natural as the line that matters most, and they get frustrated by how often it's blurred, with the worst of the worst on the shelf standing in for the entire industry. I share that frustration. I also think the federal government has changed what the phrase is worth.

## What the proposed 7-OH ban measures

On October 14 the DEA published a notice of intent (91 FR 65370) to temporarily place 7-hydroxymitragynine and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl in Schedule I above a set threshold. A product would be covered if it holds more than 1 mg of 7-OH per gram, for solids, or per milliliter, for liquids. It would also be covered if the mass of mitragynine divided by the mass of 7-OH comes out under 100, whatever the concentration. The same two tests apply to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, and to the two substances combined when a product has both.

[That notice](/briefings/hhs-dea-new-7oh-mgpi-threshold-1-mg-per-gram-100-to-1-mitragynine-ratio-withdraw-july-notice-kennedy-christine-blanche-aka-litigation-resolved-federal-register-october-14/) replaced the July 6 version (91 FR 40917), which DEA withdrew when it published the new one. The July version had its own test for botanical material, 0.050 percent 7-OH by dry weight, and a separate one for synthetic and processed products. The October notice carves out no category for leaf. The same two numbers apply to everything it covers.

## Natural 7-OH has no legal category

"Naturally occurring" isn't a term the notice uses to sort anything. It says 7-OH has "supposedly been found in trace amounts in some botanical kratom products," and that this is why control is limited to products above the threshold. It also says "neither HHS nor DEA has made a finding that these substances are safe to use at below-threshold levels."

So under this test, origin doesn't decide anything. Leaf-derived 7-OH concentrated past 1 mg per gram would be covered. A leaf product stays outside the rule only if its numbers do: 7-OH at or under the concentration line, and enough mitragynine beside it to keep the ratio at 100 or more. Where the molecule started never comes up.

## 7-OH vs mitragynine: the ratio test

The ratio is the part I find most interesting. The Justice Department said HHS proposed these thresholds to separate intentionally enhanced or concentrated products from products whose alkaloid makeup is more consistent with botanical kratom. In the same release it described 7-OH as something that "occurs naturally in botanical kratom at relatively low levels."

Put those two statements together and the ratio test reads like the government's attempt to write "naturally occurring" down as a number. A lab can check a number. Nobody can check an adjective on a label.

## Synthetic 7-OH, in Ivan Estrada's words

I talked with Ivan Estrada of [Drink Yum](https://drinkyum.com) on November 13 for a Q&A that runs separately. Drink Yum is a Miami company that makes a flavored kratom extract shot in 30 ml and 14 ml bottles, with Bubble Gum and tropical flavors so far. Ivan handles online distribution, and his partner Gunther created the product. Ivan said the shots are in smoke shops in Nevada, Arizona and California, and the rest goes out through the website, which lists Vermont among the states it can't ship to.

I asked him about synthetic 7-OH. "I'm not familiar with it, frankly," he said. "I see something like synthetic 7-OH and, to me, it looks a little scary. Ours isn't synthetic. Ours is natural leaf derived."

Then he said the thing I keep coming back to:

> "I don't want to pretend I know more than I do. If it turns out that it's basically the same, like lab-grown diamonds versus natural diamonds, the same molecular structure, I wouldn't have any problem with it. But let's test it out and find out."

He also didn't object to regulation as such. "It's fine to regulate it," he said, "but to just outright ban it and take the most specialized, hyper-concentrated form and attribute it to the whole thing, it's like saying poppy seed bagels are illegal."

Drink Yum tests every batch. Ivan said the aim is 7-OH "below level of quantification" in each barrel, with the product built around mitragynine. Its site lists 42.5 mg of mitragynine per serving and 7-OH below 0.33 mg per serving, under the limit of quantitation, and each bottle carries a batch ID that can be looked up for the lab report. Mitragynine and 7-OH are the two numbers the October test asks about.

## What a lab report has to say now

Here's where I land. "Naturally occurring" should retire as a selling point. Under a concentration and ratio test it tells a buyer nothing they can check, and it tells a regulator nothing at all. I'd rather see every label give mitragynine and 7-OH per serving, name the lab, and tie the result to the batch in the buyer's hand. Ivan said it more plainly: "I think lab reports should be something that's a little bit more common."

The states make this more pressing, because they don't all measure the same way. Governor Kathy Hochul signed a [New York law](/briefings/hochul-signs-new-york-law-s8925a-a9156b-banning-sales-concentrated-synthetic-7oh-products-2-percent-total-alkaloids-1-mg-serving-90-days/) on November 23 that bars sales of products with 7-OH above 2 percent of total alkaloids or 1 mg per serving, and it takes effect 90 days after signing. Florida's attorney general already schedules 7-OH under an emergency rule with the same 1 mg and 100-to-1 shape as the DEA notice, through June 30, 2027. Colorado's 2025 law caps 7-OH at 2 percent of a product's alkaloid composition. That's per gram, per serving and percent of alkaloids, three different yardsticks, and a label that says "naturally occurring" answers none of them.

A lab number has limits of its own. "Below the limit of quantitation" means below what that lab's method can measure, so the number behind it matters as much as the phrase. On November 20 the 7-HOPE Alliance said it had filed [two petitions](/briefings/7-hope-alliance-files-two-dea-petitions-clarification-proposed-7oh-mgpi-temporary-schedule-i-thresholds-testing-methods-rush-subeck/) asking DEA which lab methods it will accept and how results that are too close to call will be handled. The group's announcement shows no DEA response.

## Where the 7-OH rule stands today

The October document is a notice of intent. It said a temporary order would be published on or after November 13. As of November 25 the Federal Register showed no such order, so 7-OH above the threshold isn't federally scheduled yet. The notice set no comment period, and once an order is published it takes effect that day and lasts two years, with a possible one-year extension.

When someone tells me 7-OH is killing people, my first question is which product, and my second is what was in it. Those were always the right questions to ask. The proposed rule makes the second one the government's question too, and a brand with nothing to hide can answer it with a number.
